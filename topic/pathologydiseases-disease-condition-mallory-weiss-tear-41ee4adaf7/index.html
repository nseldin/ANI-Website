Definition

Mallory-Weiss tear develops when forceful retching abruptly raises gastroesophageal pressure and produces a mucosal laceration near the junction. The characteristic pattern is hematemesis after repeated vomiting, coughing, or retching, often with epigastric discomfort and otherwise self-limited bleeding. Confirm or stage it with hemodynamic, CBC, coagulation, type-and-screen, and serial bleeding assessment; distinguish it from variceal bleeding, peptic ulcer, Boerhaave rupture, gastritis, and swallowed blood by stability, pain, imaging, and endoscopy. The priority is to resuscitate and use acid suppression and antiemetic therapy because major hemorrhage can complicate Mallory-Weiss tear. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Abdominal pain pattern, distention, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea or constipation, stool/emesis blood, jaundice, dysphagia, poor intake, dehydration, or guarding are the useful GI clues.

  • Escalate rigid abdomen, peritonitis signs, GI bleeding, shock, severe dehydration, fever with jaundice, or worsening mental status.

Diagnostics

  • hemodynamic, CBC, coagulation, type-and-screen, and serial bleeding assessment

  • upper endoscopy for ongoing, significant, recurrent, or diagnostically uncertain bleeding

  • evaluation for alcohol use, pregnancy, obstruction, eating disorder, or other cause of vomiting

  • Distinguish Mallory-Weiss tear from variceal bleeding, peptic ulcer, Boerhaave rupture, gastritis, and swallowed blood by stability, pain, imaging, and endoscopy.

Labs

  • hemoglobin may fall with bleeding

  • AST/ALT and bilirubin may rise with hepatic/biliary injury

  • lipase rises in pancreatitis

  • albumin and INR reflect liver synthetic function

Treatments

  • resuscitate and use acid suppression and antiemetic therapy

  • apply endoscopic hemostasis for active or high-risk bleeding

  • treat the cause of retching and reverse important coagulopathy

Nursing Priorities

  • Activate emergency response for ongoing large hematemesis, inability to protect the airway, hypotension, tachycardia with poor perfusion, syncope, falling hemoglobin, severe chest pain after vomiting, or rigid abdomen because uncontrolled hemorrhage, aspiration, or esophageal perforation is life-threatening.

  • Assess the amount and color of hematemesis, retching or coughing trigger, melena, dizziness, syncope, alcohol use, anticoagulants, liver disease, prior bleeding, and aspiration risk because mucosal tears range from self-limited bleeding to brisk upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage.

  • Trend airway protection, respiratory status, orthostatic and continuous vital signs when unstable, mental status, skin perfusion, urine output, serial hemoglobin, platelets, INR, and type-and-screen status because early hemoglobin may underestimate acute blood loss.

  • Keep the patient appropriately fasting, establish reliable intravenous access, provide suction and oxygen for clinical need, and administer prescribed fluid, blood, antiemetic, and acid suppression because preventing further retching and restoring perfusion support hemostasis and safe endoscopy.

  • Reconcile anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, and alcohol exposure and coordinate timely endoscopy because medication reversal and endoscopic therapy depend on bleeding severity, thrombotic risk, and direct visualization.

Complications

  • major hemorrhage can complicate Mallory-Weiss tear.

  • aspiration can complicate Mallory-Weiss tear.

  • recurrent bleeding can complicate Mallory-Weiss tear.

  • anemia can complicate Mallory-Weiss tear.

  • rarely perforation from another diagnosis

Red Flags

  • Ongoing large-volume hematemesis or blood pooling in an unprotected airway

  • Hypotension, syncope, confusion, cool mottled skin, or rapidly falling urine output

  • Rapid hemoglobin decline, repeated transfusion need, or recurrent bleeding after initial control

  • Severe chest or upper abdominal pain, subcutaneous emphysema, fever, or rigid abdomen after vomiting

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to seek emergency care for recurrent red or coffee-ground emesis, black stool with weakness, or fainting rather than waiting for the next appointment.

  • Explain that controlling recurrent vomiting and reducing alcohol or ulcerogenic medicine exposure helps prevent another tear while the mucosa heals.

Nclex Traps

  • Severe chest pain and subcutaneous air after vomiting suggest Boerhaave rupture, not a simple mucosal tear

  • Bleeding can stop before endoscopy, so the history remains important
