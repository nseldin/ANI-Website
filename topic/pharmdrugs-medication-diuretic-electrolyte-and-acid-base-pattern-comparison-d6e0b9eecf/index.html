Used To Treat

A bedside interpretation card for predicting and diagnosing medication-related electrolyte and acid-base changes and for distinguishing expected pharmacology from dangerous over-treatment.

Description

Loop and thiazide drugs usually produce hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis; potassium-sparing drugs produce hyperkalemia with a normal-anion-gap acidosis tendency; acetazolamide directly produces bicarbonate loss and hyperchloremic non-anion-gap acidosis. Loops increase urinary calcium and magnesium, whereas thiazides decrease urinary calcium but still waste magnesium. Both loop and thiazide drugs can raise uric acid. Vaptans primarily remove water and can raise sodium; mannitol causes phase-dependent sodium changes because water first shifts into plasma and is then lost in urine. These patterns are not arbitrary side-effect lists. They are fingerprints of the transporter or receptor being changed, the amount of sodium reaching the collecting duct, and the hormonal response to volume contraction.

Mechanism

Loop or thiazide blockade sends more sodium to ENaC. Principal cells reabsorb that sodium, increasing lumen negativity and driving potassium secretion; volume contraction activates renin, angiotensin II, and aldosterone, further increasing potassium and hydrogen secretion. Chloride depletion limits bicarbonate secretion through pendrin, while lower effective arterial volume increases proximal bicarbonate reclamation. Together these processes create chloride-responsive contraction alkalosis. MRAs and ENaC blockers remove the distal electrical and hormonal drive for potassium and hydrogen secretion, causing hyperkalemic acidosis. Acetazolamide bypasses this sequence by directly preventing bicarbonate reclamation. Loop blockade removes the ROMK-generated positive lumen voltage in the thick ascending limb, so calcium and magnesium reabsorption fall; thiazide NCC blockade lowers distal-cell sodium, enhances basolateral sodium-calcium exchange, and favors apical TRPV5 calcium entry, so calcium reabsorption rises. Understanding those causal steps lets the user predict a new drug rather than memorize seven disconnected arrows.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: A pattern can be expected and still be dangerous. Neurologic symptoms with hyponatremia, ECG change with potassium or magnesium disturbance, severe alkalemia or acidemia, progressive oliguria, or hemodynamic instability requires urgent action.

Adverse Effects

  • Loop or thiazide over-effect can produce arrhythmia, muscle weakness, ileus, seizure, delirium, digoxin toxicity, falls, or kidney injury through electrolyte and volume loss.

  • Potassium-sparing over-effect can cause conduction block or arrest; acetazolamide-associated acidosis can worsen respiratory or renal reserve; rapid vaptan correction can cause osmotic demyelination.

Contraindications

  • Do not replace an electrolyte reflexively without identifying the drug, kidney function, acid-base state, urine losses, magnesium, and ongoing treatment. Potassium replacement can become dangerous if an MRA or ENaC blocker is added.

  • Do not call every alkalosis 'contraction' without assessing vomiting, gastric suction, mineralocorticoid excess, alkali load, chloride, urine chloride, pressure, and volume.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before the dose, predict the direction of sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, calcium, and volume. After the dose, compare the actual trend and symptoms with that prediction.

  • Escalate neurologic change, severe weakness, palpitations, new ECG abnormality, marked orthostasis, oliguria, or rapid sodium movement. Correct magnesium when persistent hypokalemia will not respond because magnesium depletion increases renal potassium loss.

Interactions

  • Digoxin toxicity is amplified by low potassium or magnesium; QT-prolonging drugs become more hazardous during electrolyte depletion; RAAS blockers and trimethoprim magnify potassium retention.

  • Vomiting, diarrhea, laxatives, sodium bicarbonate, high free-water intake, SIADH drugs, and nephrotoxins can change or exaggerate the expected pattern.

Key Labs

  • Sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, calcium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, glucose, uric acid, measured osmolality when water balance is central, blood gas when severity requires, and ECG for meaningful potassium or magnesium disturbance.

  • Urine chloride can help separate chloride-responsive alkalosis from ongoing mineralocorticoid or renal chloride-wasting states, but recent diuretic exposure can keep urine chloride elevated and complicate interpretation.

Nclex Traps

  • Contraction alkalosis is not just 'less water means concentrated bicarbonate.' Distal hydrogen loss, RAAS activation, chloride depletion, and increased proximal bicarbonate reclamation sustain it.

  • Potassium and hydrogen often move in parallel at the distal nephron: loop and thiazide pathways lose both; aldosterone or ENaC blockade retains both.

    Note

    Older adults more often have CKD, low thirst reserve, orthostasis, polypharmacy, and narrow electrolyte tolerance, so small fluid losses can produce falls, delirium, acute kidney injury, or dysnatremia.
