Definition

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy is an immune-mediated glomerular disease in which IgA-containing immune deposits accumulate in the kidney mesangium and drive inflammation. The resulting filtration-barrier injury leaks blood and protein into urine and may slowly scar nephrons, although the course ranges from stable microscopic hematuria to progressive kidney failure.

Pathology

Kidney biopsy shows dominant or codominant mesangial IgA deposits by immunofluorescence, usually with C3, plus variable mesangial proliferation, segmental sclerosis, endocapillary inflammation, crescents, and chronic tubular-interstitial scarring. The Oxford MEST-C features describe tissue injury but do not replace clinical trends in proteinuria, blood pressure, and estimated filtration.

Pathophysiology

  • Many patients produce galactose-deficient IgA1. Autoantibodies recognize this altered IgA, forming immune complexes that evade normal clearance and lodge in the mesangium. Mesangial cells activate complement and inflammatory pathways, which injure capillary loops and recruit additional inflammation.

  • Hematuria reflects glomerular barrier disruption; sustained protein leakage is itself toxic to tubules, so proteinuria both measures and accelerates progressive nephron loss.

Etiology

Primary IgA nephropathy reflects interacting mucosal-immune, genetic, and environmental factors rather than one exposure. An IgA-dominant pattern can accompany liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease, infection, and other systemic conditions; identifying a secondary context changes management. An upper-respiratory or gastrointestinal infection may trigger visible hematuria by stimulating mucosal IgA production, but the infection is not evidence of bacterial kidney infection.

Risk Factors

  • Persistent proteinuria, hypertension, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate, and chronic biopsy scarring predict progression

  • Family history and ancestry-associated genetic susceptibility

  • Male sex in some populations

  • Smoking, obesity, excess sodium intake, and cardiovascular risk that add nonimmune kidney stress

Signs Symptoms

  • Microscopic hematuria or episodes of cola-colored urine, often during or soon after a respiratory or gastrointestinal illness

  • Proteinuria, foamy urine, edema, and hypertension

  • Flank discomfort during gross hematuria in some patients

  • Asymptomatic disease detected on urinalysis or progressive chronic kidney disease with fatigue, nausea, pruritus, or volume overload

  • Rare rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with abrupt filtration loss and crescentic injury

Diagnostics

  • Urinalysis with microscopy, quantified urine protein, serum creatinine with estimated filtration, blood pressure, and longitudinal trends define the clinical kidney injury.

  • Kidney biopsy is required to establish primary IgA nephropathy and distinguish it from other glomerular diseases; serum IgA alone cannot diagnose it.

  • Review liver disease, infection, autoimmune disease, medications, family history, and gastrointestinal symptoms for secondary or mimicking causes.

  • Use biopsy findings and validated prediction tools as part of shared prognostic discussion, not as a single irreversible forecast.

Labs

  • Urine shows glomerular hematuria, often with dysmorphic red cells or casts, and variable albumin-predominant proteinuria.

  • Creatinine and estimated filtration may be normal early and decline over time; potassium and bicarbonate change as kidney function worsens.

  • Complement levels are often normal in primary IgA nephropathy; marked hypocomplementemia should prompt evaluation for another immune-complex process.

  • Serum IgA may be elevated but lacks sufficient sensitivity and specificity for diagnosis.

Assessment

Assess the timing and appearance of hematuria, recent mucosal illness, urine output, edema, blood pressure, weight, medication and NSAID use, pregnancy plans, family history, rash, abdominal or joint symptoms, and systemic infection. Trend quantified proteinuria and estimated filtration over time because a single creatinine or dramatic episode of visible blood does not define progression risk.

Differential

Differentiate primary IgA nephropathy from IgA vasculitis with systemic purpura, postinfectious glomerulonephritis, lupus and other immune-complex disease, Alport syndrome, thin basement membrane disease, ANCA-associated vasculitis, anti-GBM disease, infection-related IgA-dominant glomerulonephritis, and urologic sources of hematuria.

Treatments

  • Foundation therapy reduces intraglomerular pressure and protein leakage through individualized blood-pressure control, sodium reduction, smoking cessation, exercise and weight support, and a maximally tolerated renin-angiotensin system blocker when appropriate.

  • Selected patients with chronic kidney disease benefit from an SGLT2 inhibitor and other kidney-protective therapy based on current filtration, comorbidity, pregnancy potential, and prescribing criteria.

  • Disease-specific therapy is chosen by nephrology from current KDIGO guidance after optimized supportive care and a careful risk-benefit discussion. Options may target mucosal IgA production, endothelin and angiotensin signaling, complement, or broader immune activity; availability and evidence differ by region and phenotype.

  • Rapidly progressive crescentic disease requires urgent specialist evaluation for an immunosuppressive approach; isolated stable hematuria is not treated as that emergency.

  • Kidney replacement therapy or transplantation is used for kidney failure. IgA deposits can recur in a transplant, but transplantation remains an effective treatment.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose or treat IgA nephropathy from elevated serum IgA or hematuria alone.

  • ACE inhibitors, angiotensin-receptor blockers, and several disease-specific agents are unsafe in pregnancy and require contraception or transition planning.

  • Systemic glucocorticoids and other immunosuppression can cause serious infection and metabolic toxicity and are not routine for every patient with proteinuria.

  • Avoid dual renin-angiotensin blockade and unmonitored nephrotoxins such as NSAIDs.

Safety Considerations

  • Check creatinine and potassium after starting or increasing kidney-protective medicines; an expected hemodynamic change must still be distinguished from volume depletion or true acute injury.

  • Gross hematuria with falling urine output can cause acute tubular obstruction and warrants urgent kidney-function assessment.

  • Screen infection and vaccination status before immunosuppression, and teach patients not to stop steroids abruptly when a supervised course is used.

  • A normal complement or stable creatinine at one visit does not remove the need to trend proteinuria and blood pressure.

Nursing Priorities

  • Trend blood pressure, edema, daily weight when unstable, urine appearance and output, quantified protein, creatinine, potassium, and bicarbonate.

  • Reconcile NSAIDs, supplements, renin-angiotensin blockers, SGLT2 inhibitors, immunosuppressants, and pregnancy plans at every transition.

  • Teach sick-day and volume-depletion instructions for kidney-active medicines according to the prescriber's plan and escalate oliguria, vomiting, or hypotension.

  • Support a practical lower-sodium eating plan without causing inadequate nutrition, and reinforce smoking cessation and cardiovascular risk reduction.

  • Before and during immunosuppression, monitor for fever, mucosal infection, glucose change, mood effects, gastrointestinal bleeding, and other regimen-specific toxicity.

Red Flags

  • Rapidly falling urine output, rising creatinine, severe edema, pulmonary congestion, or dangerous hypertension

  • Gross hematuria with clots, inability to void, severe flank pain, or hemodynamic change

  • Hemoptysis or respiratory distress, which suggests a pulmonary-renal syndrome rather than uncomplicated IgA nephropathy

  • Fever or systemic illness during immunosuppression

Complications

  • Chronic kidney disease and kidney failure

  • Hypertension, cardiovascular disease, edema, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis

  • Acute kidney injury during gross hematuria or rapidly progressive disease

  • Treatment-related infection, metabolic injury, or fetal harm when safety controls fail

  • Recurrence after kidney transplantation

Prognosis

The course is heterogeneous. Many patients retain useful kidney function for decades, while others progress despite few symptoms. Persistent proteinuria, declining filtration, hypertension, and chronic biopsy damage are more informative than the intensity of one episode of hematuria. Regular reassessment matters because risk and treatment eligibility change over time.

Prevention

Primary IgA nephropathy cannot currently be prevented. Progression risk can be reduced through proteinuria and blood-pressure control, avoidance of tobacco and nephrotoxins, healthy weight, and reliable follow-up. Promptly treat infections for their own indications, but do not use antibiotics to suppress sterile IgA-mediated kidney inflammation. Plan pregnancy and medication changes with nephrology and obstetric specialists before conception.

Patient Education

  • Blood in the urine comes from inflamed glomerular filters; it does not automatically mean a bladder infection.

  • Record home blood pressure and attend urine and kidney-function monitoring even when you feel well.

  • Avoid unapproved NSAIDs and supplements, and ask before contrast studies or new medications.

  • Seek urgent care for sharply reduced urine, breathlessness, severe swelling, very high blood pressure symptoms, or visible blood with inability to void.

Special Populations

  • Children often present with infection-associated visible hematuria and require pediatric interpretation of filtration, blood pressure, and biopsy findings.

  • Pregnancy increases kidney and hypertensive risk and makes several cornerstone medicines unsafe; preconception planning is essential.

  • Advanced kidney disease, frailty, liver disease, and active infection change the benefit-risk balance of immunosuppression and newer disease-specific therapies.

Nclex Traps

  • Berger disease is IgA nephropathy; it is not the same as IgA vasculitis, although the kidney lesion is closely related.

  • A kidney biopsy, not a serum IgA level, establishes the diagnosis.

  • Hematuria that begins near an upper-respiratory illness differs in timing from classic poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis.

  • Proteinuria reduction is a central treatment target because filtered protein contributes to tubulointerstitial injury.
