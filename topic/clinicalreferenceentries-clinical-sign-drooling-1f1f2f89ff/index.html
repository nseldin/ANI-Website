Definition

Drooling is visible pooling or loss of saliva from the mouth because saliva production is greater than the person's ability to contain and swallow it safely. It does not always mean the salivary glands are producing too much.

Plain Language

Drooling means saliva repeatedly pools or spills from the mouth instead of being swallowed.

Why It Matters

Drooling can reveal impaired swallowing or oral-motor control and can increase aspiration, skin-injury, dehydration, and nutrition concerns. Sudden drooling with inability to swallow, muffled voice, tripod posture, stridor, or breathing difficulty can signal critical upper-airway obstruction.

Associated Conditions

  • developmental oral-motor immaturity, impaired lip or tongue control, reduced swallowing, dysphagia, neurologic disease, medication, oral pain, excessive saliva production, obstruction, or acute upper-airway disease

Assessment Method

Observe at rest, during speech, and around meals without provoking swallowing; document anterior spillage, pooling, frequency of wiping, wet voice, cough, choking, lip seal, tongue and jaw control, alertness, respiratory effort, ability to swallow secretions, age and baseline, onset, medications, oral pain, focal neurologic findings, and aspiration history.

Positive Finding

Saliva repeatedly pools at the lip or spills beyond the mouth because production exceeds safe and effective oral clearance.

Mechanism

Drooling occurs when saliva production exceeds swallowing capacity or when impaired lip closure, oral sensation, tongue and jaw coordination, or swallow frequency prevents normal clearance; obstruction can make swallowing secretions impossible.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Drooling finding raises concern for developmental oral-motor immaturity, impaired lip or tongue control, reduced swallowing, dysphagia, neurologic disease, medication, oral pain, excessive saliva production, obstruction, or acute upper-airway disease because drooling occurs when saliva production exceeds swallowing capacity or when impaired lip closure, oral sensation, tongue and jaw coordination, or swallow frequency prevents normal clearance; obstruction can make swallowing secretions impossible. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Drooling does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Drooling to focus evaluation of developmental oral-motor immaturity, impaired lip or tongue control, reduced swallowing, dysphagia, neurologic disease, medication, oral pain, excessive saliva production, obstruction, or acute upper-airway disease and the reviewed alternatives: developmental drooling, oropharyngeal dysphagia, medication effect, oral pain, upper-airway obstruction. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Observe at rest, during speech, and around meals without provoking swallowing; document anterior spillage, pooling, frequency of wiping, wet voice, cough, choking, lip seal, tongue and jaw control, alertness, respiratory effort, ability to swallow secretions, age and baseline, onset, medications, oral pain, focal neurologic findings, and aspiration history. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Drooling as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Drooling does not always mean excess saliva, visible anterior drooling does not measure silent posterior aspiration, absence of coughing does not exclude aspiration, and a young child's developmental drooling must be distinguished from acute inability to swallow. Therefore, Drooling cannot confirm or exclude developmental oral-motor immaturity, impaired lip or tongue control, reduced swallowing, dysphagia, neurologic disease, medication, oral pain, excessive saliva production, obstruction, or acute upper-airway disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • developmental drooling

  • oropharyngeal dysphagia

  • medication effect

  • oral pain

  • upper-airway obstruction

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as developmental drooling, oropharyngeal dysphagia, medication effect, oral pain, upper-airway obstruction.

False Negative Situations

Drooling may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of developmental oral-motor immaturity, impaired lip or tongue control, reduced swallowing, dysphagia, neurologic disease, medication, oral pain, excessive saliva production, obstruction, or acute upper-airway disease.

Modern Status

Current nonspecific sign; it is developmentally common in infants and young toddlers, but new, persistent, or worsening drooling in an older child or adult requires context-specific assessment. Safety Precautions

Observe Drooling with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Observe at rest, during speech, and around meals without provoking swallowing; document anterior spillage, pooling, frequency of wiping, wet voice, cough, choking, lip seal, tongue and jaw control, alertness, respiratory effort, ability to swallow secretions, age and baseline, onset, medications, oral pain, focal neurologic findings, and aspiration history. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Sudden drooling with stridor, tripod posture, muffled voice, inability to swallow, tongue or floor-of-mouth swelling, cyanosis, or respiratory distress is an airway emergency; keep the patient calm and upright, do not force a throat examination or oral intake, and activate controlled airway assessment, while acute facial weakness or speech change requires stroke evaluation.
