Definition

Legionella pneumophila is a clinically important bacterium associated with Legionnaires' disease, severe atypical pneumonia, and Pontiac fever. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    identity-classification

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Legionella pneumophila is a Gram-negative bacillus with a obligate aerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, motile, and facultative intracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Building water-management programs and control of aerosol-generating water sources are central; routine person-to-person isolation is generally unnecessary. The reviewed route description is the reviewed waterborne transmission and environmental exposure transmission route categories. The reviewed incubation estimate is 2 to 14 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Inhaled water aerosols deliver organisms that replicate within alveolar macrophages by altering phagosome trafficking and provoke systemic inflammation. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Legionella pneumophila is associated with Legionnaires' disease, severe atypical pneumonia, and Pontiac fever. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Urinary antigen rapidly detects mainly serogroup 1, while lower-respiratory culture or molecular testing broadens detection and supports outbreak investigation. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
