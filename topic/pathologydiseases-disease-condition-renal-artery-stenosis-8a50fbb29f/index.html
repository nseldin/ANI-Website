Definition

Renal artery stenosis develops when narrowing of one or both renal arteries lowers renal perfusion and activates renin-mediated hypertension, sometimes impairing kidney function. The characteristic pattern is resistant or abrupt hypertension, asymmetric kidneys, an abdominal bruit, recurrent flash pulmonary edema, or creatinine rise after renin-angiotensin blockade. Confirm or stage it with duplex ultrasonography, CT angiography, or MR angiography chosen for kidney function and local expertise; distinguish it from primary aldosteronism, essential hypertension, fibromuscular dysplasia, and other causes of kidney decline by renin pattern, imaging, age, and vascular context. Signs Symptoms

  • Urine output change, edema, thirst, dry mucosa, dizziness, flank/suprapubic pain, dysuria, confusion, weakness, weight change, or abnormal heart rhythm can reveal renal, fluid, or electrolyte stress.

  • Escalate anuria, severe potassium abnormality, pulmonary edema, seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, or worsening acidosis.

Diagnostics

  • duplex ultrasonography, CT angiography, or MR angiography chosen for kidney function and local expertise

  • serial blood pressure, potassium, creatinine, and albuminuria

  • catheter angiography when intervention is being considered or noninvasive studies conflict

  • Distinguish Renal artery stenosis from primary aldosteronism, essential hypertension, fibromuscular dysplasia, and other causes of kidney decline by renin pattern, imaging, age, and vascular context.

Labs

  • creatinine and BUN rise when filtration worsens

  • potassium may rise with kidney failure

  • urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is concerning

  • WBC/nitrites/leukocyte esterase support UTI

Treatments

  • optimize blood pressure, lipid, smoking, and antiplatelet risk management for atherosclerotic disease

  • monitor kidney function and potassium closely with renin-angiotensin blockade

  • reserve revascularization for selected high-risk or refractory presentations

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for flash pulmonary edema, severe hypertension with target-organ injury, rapidly rising creatinine, oliguria after medication change, refractory heart failure, or acute flank pain with hematuria because destabilized cardiorenal perfusion may require urgent specialist intervention.

  • Assess abrupt or resistant hypertension, abdominal bruit, recurrent flash pulmonary edema, asymmetric kidney size, vascular disease, smoking, unexplained kidney decline, and creatinine change after renin-angiotensin blockade because high-risk renovascular syndromes are more informative than an incidental narrowing.

  • Measure blood pressure accurately in both arms initially and trend pressure, orthostasis, weight, lung sounds, edema, urine output, creatinine, potassium, and albuminuria because both uncontrolled pressure and treatment-related perfusion loss can injure the kidneys.

  • Administer prescribed antihypertensive, statin, antiplatelet, and vascular-risk therapy while checking creatinine and potassium after ACE inhibitor or ARB initiation or titration because efferent dilation can reveal bilateral or solitary-kidney flow dependence.

  • Coordinate duplex, angiographic, or other imaging preparation and renal-protection measures and document contrast risk because anatomy must be linked to a clinically meaningful syndrome before invasive treatment is considered.

Complications

  • flash pulmonary edema can complicate Renal artery stenosis.

  • hypertensive emergency can complicate Renal artery stenosis.

  • progressive kidney failure can complicate Renal artery stenosis.

  • contrast or procedural kidney injury

  • acute kidney decline from bilateral flow dependence

Red Flags

  • Sudden pulmonary edema with severe dyspnea, crackles, and hypoxemia

  • Severe pressure with neurologic deficit, chest pain, or acute kidney injury

  • Rapid creatinine rise, hyperkalemia, or oliguria after ACE inhibitor or ARB initiation

  • Acute flank pain, hematuria, hypotension, or suspected renal infarction

Patient Education

  • Teach consistent home pressure measurement and prompt reporting of sudden breathlessness or reduced urine because recurrent flash pulmonary edema can be the key renovascular clue.

  • Explain that most narrowing is treated first with careful medical risk reduction; a procedure is considered when the clinical syndrome suggests meaningful threatened organ perfusion.

Nclex Traps

  • A bruit is neither sensitive nor specific enough to diagnose stenosis

  • Routine stenting does not benefit every atherosclerotic lesion
