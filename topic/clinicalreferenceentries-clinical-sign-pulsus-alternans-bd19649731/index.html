Definition

Pulsus alternans is a regular arterial pulse that alternates strong, weak, strong, weak from one heartbeat to the next. The amplitude change is independent of breathing.

Plain Language

The heartbeat timing stays regular, but every other pulse feels weaker.

Why It Matters

Pulsus alternans is an important warning of severe ventricular dysfunction, usually advanced left-sided systolic failure. It requires prompt cardiac evaluation and must be distinguished from an irregular rhythm, pulsus paradoxus, and electrical alternans on the ECG.

Associated Conditions

  • severe left-ventricular systolic dysfunction and decompensated heart failure, with uncommon right-sided or biventricular forms

Assessment Method

Confirm a regular rhythm, then palpate the femoral or radial artery for alternating strong and weak beats independent of respiration; correlate with simultaneous heart auscultation and ECG, and when appropriate lower a blood-pressure cuff slowly to detect alternating Korotkoff intensity before confirming ventricular function with echocardiography.

Positive Finding

Strong and weak arterial pulse amplitudes alternate from beat to beat while the timing remains regular and the pattern does not track inspiration and expiration.

Mechanism

Severely impaired myocardium can alternate contractile force because end-diastolic fiber length and intracellular calcium handling vary from one beat to the next.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Pulsus alternans response raises concern for severe left-ventricular systolic dysfunction and decompensated heart failure, with uncommon right-sided or biventricular forms because severely impaired myocardium can alternate contractile force because end-diastolic fiber length and intracellular calcium handling vary from one beat to the next. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Pulsus alternans does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Pulsus alternans to focus evaluation of severe left-ventricular systolic dysfunction and decompensated heart failure, with uncommon right-sided or biventricular forms and the reviewed alternatives: ventricular bigeminy, electrical alternans, pulsus paradoxus, pulse deficit, atrial fibrillation. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Confirm a regular rhythm, then palpate the femoral or radial artery for alternating strong and weak beats independent of respiration; correlate with simultaneous heart auscultation and ECG, and when appropriate lower a blood-pressure cuff slowly to detect alternating Korotkoff intensity before confirming ventricular function with echocardiography. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Pulsus alternans as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Subtle alternation is hard to palpate, very weak beats may disappear peripherally, tachycardia limits assessment, and bigeminy, atrial fibrillation, respiratory variation, or cuff technique can mimic the pattern. Therefore, Pulsus alternans cannot confirm or exclude severe left-ventricular systolic dysfunction and decompensated heart failure, with uncommon right-sided or biventricular forms by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • ventricular bigeminy

  • electrical alternans

  • pulsus paradoxus

  • pulse deficit

  • atrial fibrillation

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from ventricular bigeminy, electrical alternans, pulsus paradoxus, pulse deficit, atrial fibrillation, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Pulsus alternans may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current uncommon but important sign of severe ventricular dysfunction and a poor prognostic clue; objective waveform and cardiac evaluation should confirm it. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Pulsus alternans, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Confirm a regular rhythm, then palpate the femoral or radial artery for alternating strong and weak beats independent of respiration; correlate with simultaneous heart auscultation and ECG, and when appropriate lower a blood-pressure cuff slowly to detect alternating Korotkoff intensity before confirming ventricular function with echocardiography. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not confuse pulsus alternans with ECG electrical alternans or delay heart-failure evaluation to repeat bedside palpation; new alternans with dyspnea, hypoxemia, hypotension, chest pain, syncope, pulmonary edema, confusion, or low urine output requires urgent cardiac and hemodynamic assessment.
