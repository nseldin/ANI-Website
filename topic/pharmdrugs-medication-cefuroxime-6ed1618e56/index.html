Used To Treat

Susceptible respiratory, sinus, ear, skin, urinary, gonococcal, and early Lyme infections using the appropriate oral or parenteral formulation. The oral axetil product and IV cefuroxime are related but not administration substitutes.

Mechanism

Active cefuroxime binds PBPs and blocks peptidoglycan transpeptidation, causing wall failure and lysis in susceptible growing bacteria. The axetil ester temporarily improves oral lipophilicity, then nonspecific esterases release cefuroxime and metabolize the promoiety. Food raises tablet bioavailability by improving absorption; acid-reducing drugs can blunt this advantage. The suspension has a different peak and total exposure from the tablet, which is why a prescription cannot be converted by matching milligrams alone. Cefuroxime is excreted largely unchanged in urine, so reduced clearance lengthens half-life and increases beta-lactam neurotoxicity risk.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Cefuroxime has no current U.S. boxed warning. Serious beta-lactam hypersensitivity, C. difficile diarrhea, renal accumulation with encephalopathy or seizures, and formulation errors are key hazards. During early Lyme treatment, a transient Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction can occur as spirochetal components trigger cytokine release; fever and symptom worsening require assessment because the reaction can resemble allergy or treatment failure.

Contraindications

  • Known serious cefuroxime/cephalosporin hypersensitivity or a severe immediate or delayed reaction to a relevant beta-lactam.

  • Do not substitute tablet and suspension milligram-for-milligram, and do not rely on oral cefuroxime when severity, vomiting, malabsorption, resistance, or site requires parenteral therapy.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify axetil tablet, suspension, or parenteral product; give oral tablets after food when feasible to improve exposure and review acid-suppressing drugs.

  • Assess culture/site, renal function, allergy phenotype, ability to absorb oral therapy, and the clinical distinction between Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, immediate allergy, and infection progression.

Interactions

  • Probenecid raises cefuroxime exposure; proton-pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, and antacids can reduce axetil absorption by altering gastric conditions.

  • Warfarin effect may become unstable during infection or antibiotic therapy; monitor high-risk patients rather than assuming the interaction is identical for everyone.

Key Labs

  • Cultures and susceptibility, renal function, CBC/liver tests for prolonged therapy, temperature and symptom trajectory, and stool assessment for significant diarrhea.

  • In Lyme disease, trend timing and duration of fever, rigors, myalgia, rash, blood pressure, and airway findings to distinguish inflammatory die-off from anaphylaxis.

Nclex Traps

  • Cefuroxime axetil is a prodrug; the tablet and suspension are not bioequivalent and should not be swapped by equal milligrams.

  • A Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction after Lyme treatment is inflammatory and time-limited; hives, wheeze, hypotension, or airway swelling suggests allergy instead.

Description

Cefuroxime is a second-generation cephalosporin that binds bacterial penicillin-binding proteins and blocks cell-wall cross-linking; it is given as active parenteral cefuroxime or as the oral ester prodrug cefuroxime axetil. Intestinal and blood esterases convert the axetil prodrug to active cefuroxime. Compared with first-generation agents, it retains useful streptococcal and MSSA activity while adding selected respiratory gram-negative coverage such as beta-lactamase-producing Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis. It does not reliably cover MRSA, Enterococcus, Listeria, Pseudomonas, or atypicals. Oral tablets and suspension are not milligram-for-milligram bioequivalent, and food increases tablet absorption, so formulation and administration details can determine whether exposure is adequate.

Adverse Effects

  • Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, rash, candidiasis, unpleasant suspension taste, and Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction in early Lyme disease.

  • Anaphylaxis, severe cutaneous reaction, C. difficile colitis, hemolytic anemia or cytopenia, hepatitis, interstitial nephritis, encephalopathy, and seizures.
