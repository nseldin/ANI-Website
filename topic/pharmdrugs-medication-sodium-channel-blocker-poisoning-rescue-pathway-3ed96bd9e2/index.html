Used To Treat

Emergency recognition and management of toxic sodium-channel blockade causing seizure, QRS widening, hypotension, ventricular dysrhythmia, or arrest.

Description

This pathway applies when a credible sodium-channel-blocking poison such as a tricyclic antidepressant, flecainide, propafenone, diphenhydramine, or cocaine produces toxic conduction delay, seizure, hypotension, or ventricular dysrhythmia. It prioritizes airway and ventilation, benzodiazepines for seizures, serial ECGs, and IV sodium bicarbonate. AHA guidance describes adult 50 to 150 mEq or pediatric 1 to 3 mEq/kg initial sodium bicarbonate for life-threatening cardiotoxicity, with repeat treatment titrated to QRS, perfusion, sodium, and pH under toxicology guidance. It is not a generic map for every medication in a sodium-channel pharmacology class.

Mechanism

Fast sodium-channel blockade slows phase-zero myocardial depolarization, widening the QRS and promoting re-entry, while acidosis increases protonated drug binding and makes blockade stronger. Sodium bicarbonate provides both sodium mass and alkalemia, which weakens drug-channel binding and improves conduction. Seizures worsen lactic acidosis and thereby deepen cardiotoxicity, so rapid benzodiazepine treatment interrupts a harmful feedback loop. A drug may redistribute or continue absorbing after the QRS narrows, which explains the need for serial ECG and recurrence monitoring.

Administration Timing

  • Secure airway and ventilation, obtain continuous ECG, treat seizure with benzodiazepines, and give sodium bicarbonate promptly for life-threatening QRS widening, hypotension, ventricular dysrhythmia, or arrest.

  • Use AHA clinician ranges of 50-150 mEq adult or 1-3 mEq/kg pediatric initially, then repeat or infuse only through a poison-center protocol based on ECG and laboratory response.

  • Escalate early for refractory shock or dysrhythmia; toxin-specific options such as lidocaine in selected cases or VA-ECMO require expert direction.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact drug, formulation, amount, time, co-ingestants, ECG baseline, and access to sustained-release products because delayed absorption changes observation.

  • Track QRS, QT, aVR findings when relevant, rhythm, blood pressure, pH, sodium, potassium, ionized calcium, and cumulative bicarbonate because therapeutic alkalinization can become harmful.

  • Avoid class IA and IC antiarrhythmics and uncontrolled hyperventilation because additional sodium-channel blockade or extreme alkalemia can worsen instability.

Key Labs

  • Serial ECG, blood gas, sodium, potassium, chloride, ionized calcium, glucose, lactate, creatinine, drug concentrations when validated, and co-ingestant testing guide physiology rather than replace treatment.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause coma, seizure, aspiration, QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia, hypotension, and arrest; bicarbonate can cause hypernatremia, alkalemia, hypokalemia, and hypocalcemia.

Contraindications

  • Do not apply the pathway to benign therapeutic sodium-channel pharmacology or non-toxicologic hyperkalemia without evidence of poisoning; life-threatening blockade has no simple contraindication to monitored sodium loading.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent QRS widening, acidosis, seizure, hypotension, ventricular dysrhythmia, or arrest requires immediate poison-center consultation, vasopressor and toxin-specific rescue, and VA-ECMO consideration.

Interactions

  • Acidosis, hyperthermia, and seizure intensify channel blockade, while other QRS-prolonging or sodium-channel-blocking drugs add cardiotoxicity.

  • Bicarbonate can precipitate with calcium in the same line and can worsen hypokalemia from insulin, beta agonists, or diuretics.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A small pill burden can be lethal and bicarbonate is weight-based, so early Poison Help contact and independent dose verification are essential.

  • Population

    Cardiac conduction disease

    Note

    Limited conduction reserve makes additional channel blockade dangerous and can obscure whether an ECG change is toxic or baseline.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal seizure and shock threaten fetal perfusion; aggressive maternal resuscitation and obstetric monitoring take priority.

Evidence Limitations

  • Not every sodium-channel-blocking poison responds identically, and target pH, sodium, or QRS strategies vary; specialist protocols remain necessary.

Nclex Traps

  • A wide QRS plus poisoning context is different from a routine antiarrhythmic pharmacology question.

  • Treating seizures helps the heart because it reduces acidosis.

  • A temporarily narrowed QRS does not prove the toxin has cleared.

Regulatory Status

This is an educational emergency toxicology pathway. Sodium bicarbonate use for specific poisonings is guideline-supported and generally not a poison-specific FDA-labeled indication.

Boxed Warning

WIDE-QRS POISONING WARNING: seizure, hypotension, or ventricular dysrhythmia after a sodium-channel blocker requires 911/Poison Help, airway support, benzodiazepines, and monitored IV sodium bicarbonate. Household baking soda and unmonitored alkalinization are dangerous substitutes.

The visible warning names the urgent hazard, explains why it matters, and states the action that prevents delay or medication error.
