Used To Treat

Edema associated with heart failure, cirrhosis, and renal disease including nephrotic syndrome in adults and pediatric patients; adult hypertension under current oral labeling. IV formulations are used when a rapid or parenteral loop effect is clinically required.

Mechanism

Highly albumin-bound furosemide reaches the proximal tubule through blood flow and is actively secreted into the tubular lumen through organic-anion transport. From the luminal side it inhibits NKCC2, reducing sodium, potassium, and chloride reabsorption. Less ROMK potassium recycling removes the positive luminal voltage that normally drives calcium and magnesium reabsorption, so both can be lost. More sodium reaches the collecting duct; ENaC reabsorbs it, making the lumen negative and increasing potassium and hydrogen secretion. Volume contraction simultaneously activates RAAS and aldosterone. This combined distal-delivery and hormonal effect explains hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. Reduced medullary salt accumulation weakens urinary concentration. A patient with CKD may need a larger threshold dose because less active drug reaches fewer functioning nephrons, but dose escalation still increases electrolyte, pressure, renal, and hearing risk.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: The U.S. label carries a prominent warning that excessive furosemide can produce profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion. Individualize dose and schedule, and do not interpret brisk urine as automatically beneficial. Rapid IV delivery, severe renal impairment, high doses, hypoproteinemia, or concurrent aminoglycoside, cisplatin, ethacrynic acid, or other ototoxic exposure increases tinnitus and permanent or reversible hearing-loss risk.

Contraindications

  • Anuria and hypersensitivity are core label contraindications. Severe sodium, potassium, chloride, or volume depletion and progressive oliguria or azotemia require correction or reassessment before more drug.

  • Cirrhosis with ascites requires cautious titration because an abrupt fluid or electrolyte shift can precipitate hepatic encephalopathy.

  • The label contraindicates anuria and furosemide hypersensitivity. Sulfonamide-antibiotic allergy does not automatically predict cross-reaction, but a severe prior reaction requires specific review.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record weight, congestion, pressure, orthostasis, intake/output, recent response, creatinine/eGFR, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, and magnesium before aggressive dosing.

  • Reassess within the expected route-specific effect window. Teach daily weight and symptom thresholds, morning timing when feasible, fall prevention, and urgent reporting of hearing change or severe weakness.

  • For high-dose IV administration, follow the ordered controlled-infusion protocol; the label notes an adult rate not exceeding 4 mg/min has been used to reduce high peak exposure. Separate oral furosemide from sucralfate by at least two hours.

Interactions

  • NSAIDs can blunt natriuresis and worsen kidney perfusion; aminoglycosides, cisplatin, and other ototoxins increase hearing risk; lithium clearance falls with sodium depletion.

  • RAAS blockers and vasodilators add hypotension and filtration effects, while low potassium or magnesium increases digoxin and arrhythmia risk.

  • Sucralfate reduces absorption; phenytoin can reduce intestinal absorption and renal action; high-dose salicylates may become toxic at lower doses through competing renal secretion.

Key Labs

  • Daily weight, intake/output, blood pressure and orthostasis, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, and calcium or uric acid when clinically relevant.

  • Judge response by decongestion with preserved perfusion and acceptable chemistry, not urine volume alone.

Nclex Traps

  • Loop drugs increase distal sodium delivery, so potassium and hydrogen are lost together and hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis can result.

  • They increase urinary calcium and magnesium; thiazides decrease urinary calcium.

  • Approximate conversion is furosemide 40 mg orally to 20 mg IV, bumetanide 1 mg, or torsemide 20 mg, but actual response and local protocols govern conversion.

Description

Furosemide, commonly called Lasix, is a high-ceiling loop diuretic that removes sodium and water by blocking NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb. It is often the first familiar drug for pulmonary or systemic congestion, but it does not directly strengthen the heart or repair the kidney; it lowers venous filling pressure and tissue fluid while the underlying disease is treated. Oral absorption is variable, with current tablet labeling reporting mean bioavailability around 64 percent of IV exposure in fasting healthy subjects. Gut edema, kidney disease, low perfusion, NSAIDs, and hypoalbuminemia can make the response less predictable. Oral effect begins within about one hour, peaks in the first or second hour, and lasts roughly six to eight hours. That short action permits post-diuretic sodium retention later in the day, explaining why timing, dose interval, dietary sodium, and measured response matter.

Adverse Effects

  • Dose-related volume depletion, orthostasis, hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, and kidney-function change arise from potent salt loss and downstream aldosterone signaling.

  • Tinnitus or hearing loss becomes more likely with high or rapid IV exposure, kidney impairment, low albumin, or another ototoxin; severe rash, interstitial nephritis, cytopenia, and pancreatitis are less common.

  • Premature infants and young children receiving chronic therapy can develop nephrocalcinosis or nephrolithiasis; urinary retention can worsen when high urine flow meets bladder-outlet obstruction.
