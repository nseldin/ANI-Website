Class

Oral bidentate ferric-iron chelator with predominantly urinary iron-complex excretion

Used To Treat

Transfusional iron overload in adults and eligible children with thalassemia syndromes, sickle-cell disease, or other anemias under current labeling. It is not ordinary iron-deficiency treatment. Twice-daily and three-times-daily 1,000 mg tablet formulations have distinct schedules despite the same active ingredient.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING: Deferiprone can cause agranulocytosis leading to serious infection and death; neutropenia may precede it. Obtain ANC before treatment. Current labeling monitors weekly for the first 6 months, every 2 weeks for the next 6 months, and every 2 to 4 weeks after one year in appropriate adherent patients. Interrupt immediately for infection symptoms or ANC below 1.5 x 10^9/L and monitor more frequently.

Mechanism

Each deferiprone molecule donates two oxygen-binding sites to ferric iron. Three molecules form a 3:1 octahedral complex with one Fe3+ ion, reducing labile iron and allowing predominantly urinary excretion. Because deferiprone can enter cells, it can mobilize tissue iron as well as plasma iron, but the exact relationship between compartment removal and clinical outcomes requires longitudinal iron assessment. Its agranulocytosis mechanism is not fully defined and appears idiosyncratic rather than a predictable extension of iron binding. The drug is glucuronidated, and the glucuronide lacks iron-binding activity. Deferiprone can also bind zinc and other cations, explaining zinc monitoring and separation from mineral products.

Nursing Essentials

  • Teach the ANC calendar and same-day infection rule in plain language. The patient should interrupt therapy and contact the prescriber immediately for fever, sore throat, mouth ulcer, chills, or other infection symptoms.

  • Track ferritin every 2 to 3 months and reduce or interrupt when iron burden becomes low; monitor liver enzymes monthly and zinc periodically.

Contraindications

  • No label-listed absolute contraindication, but prior agranulocytosis/rechallenge requires exceptional specialist risk-benefit review

  • Avoid other agranulocytosis/neutropenia-causing drugs when possible

  • Do not use without reliable ANC monitoring and an immediate infection action plan

Interactions

  • Avoid other neutropenia/agranulocytosis drugs when possible; if unavoidable, monitor ANC closely.

  • Avoid UGT1A6 inhibitors such as diclofenac, probenecid, or milk thistle under current labeling; separate iron, aluminum, zinc, antacids, and other polyvalent cations by at least 4 hours.

Key Labs

  • ANC/CBC at the exact phase-based schedule and daily until recovery if neutropenia occurs

  • Ferritin every 2 to 3 months, liver tests monthly, zinc, transfusion burden, and organ iron assessment

Nclex Traps

  • Fever on deferiprone is an urgent agranulocytosis evaluation, not routine medication teaching.

  • The 1,000 mg twice-daily and three-times-daily tablets are different formulations; read the label rather than inferring frequency.

Description

Deferiprone is an oral bidentate chelator: each molecule grips two of ferric iron's six coordination sites, so three molecules surround one Fe3+ ion and the complex leaves mainly in urine. It is useful for transfusional iron overload, including clinically important cardiac iron contexts, but it can abruptly suppress neutrophils. Agranulocytosis can cause fatal infection, so fever, sore throat, mouth ulcers, or flu-like illness means stop the drug and obtain an urgent ANC rather than wait for the next routine visit. Iron benefit is assessed over months. Infection safety changes within hours.

Adverse Effects

  • Neutropenia, agranulocytosis, fever, and life-threatening infection

  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, arthralgia, headache, or reddish-brown urine from iron complex

  • ALT/AST elevation, zinc deficiency, and neurologic toxicity with major overdose/prolonged high exposure
