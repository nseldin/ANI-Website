Used To Treat

Known or suspected paraquat or diquat exposure. No proven antidote or cure exists; rescue depends on immediate decontamination and adsorbent, meticulous supportive care, cautious oxygen use for paraquat, and early toxicology and organ-failure planning.

Description

Paraquat and diquat are highly toxic bipyridyl herbicides that redox-cycle to generate reactive oxygen species, damaging the gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, liver, nervous system, and—most characteristically with paraquat—the lungs. Paraquat undergoes redox cycling and concentrates in lung tissue, producing corrosive GI injury, kidney and liver failure, and delayed progressive lung injury and fibrosis. Diquat more often causes severe GI, kidney, liver, and neurologic injury without the same classic progressive pulmonary fibrosis. The intervention most likely to reduce absorbed dose is immediate binding in the gut with activated charcoal, Fuller's earth, or another expert-selected adsorbent when the airway is safe. Once distributed, no therapy has proven to neutralize the molecule. Immunosuppression, hemoperfusion, antioxidants, and other proposed regimens have uncertain or conflicting evidence and must not be labeled proven antidotes.

Mechanism

Paraquat accepts and donates electrons repeatedly, generating superoxide and other reactive oxygen species while consuming cellular reducing capacity. Oxygen-rich lung tissue is especially vulnerable; excessive supplemental oxygen can provide more substrate for oxidative injury, although clinically important hypoxemia still must be treated. Kidney failure reduces elimination and worsens systemic burden. Adsorbents work only by binding material still in the gastrointestinal lumen. Skin and eye washing prevent continued external absorption. Dialysis can support kidney failure but generally cannot reverse rapid tissue distribution or established redox injury.

Administration Timing

  • Any ingestion is an emergency: call 911 and Poison Help immediately. Protect rescuers, remove contaminated clothing, wash exposed skin and hair, irrigate eyes, and begin poison-center-directed activated charcoal or Fuller's earth as soon as airway and GI safety permit; do not induce vomiting.

  • Use supplemental oxygen for documented hypoxemia and titrate to the lowest level that achieves necessary oxygenation in suspected paraquat poisoning rather than giving excessive oxygen reflexively. Airway, ventilation, shock, pain, corrosive injury, kidney failure, and seizure still require full supportive treatment.

  • Consult toxicology, nephrology, GI, pulmonary, and critical care early. Proposed immunosuppression, antioxidants, hemoperfusion, or extracorporeal approaches are center-specific and evidence uncertain; timing, product, and exposure burden matter.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain the original container, formulation concentration, product registration and safety label, amount, time, route, vomiting, and whether skin was broken because formulation, concentration, and route determine exposure burden and decontamination decisions. Never decant herbicides into drink containers, and treat vomit and contaminated clothing as secondary-exposure hazards because residual product can expose helpers.

  • Use PPE, isolate contaminated material, and avoid abrasive scrubbing. Document time to clothing removal, irrigation, and adsorbent because these are the few actions that can reduce continuing absorption.

  • Trend mouth and esophageal burns, respiratory status, oxygen requirement, urine output, creatinine, liver tests, acid-base status, hemodynamics, and neurologic findings over time; an initially stable patient can develop delayed organ failure.

Key Labs

  • Blood gas, electrolytes, bicarbonate, creatinine, liver tests, CBC, lactate, urinalysis, ECG, chest assessment, and serial oxygenation. Specialized plasma or urine paraquat testing and prognostic tools require toxicology interpretation and must not delay decontamination because results may arrive after the brief window to reduce absorption.

  • A negative early chest image does not exclude later paraquat lung injury. Diquat exposure can have severe renal and neurologic toxicity without classic fibrosis.

Adverse Effects

  • Paraquat can cause caustic oral and GI burns, vomiting, shock, kidney and liver failure, myocarditis, pulmonary edema, delayed fibrosis, respiratory failure, and death. Diquat can cause corrosive GI injury, kidney and liver failure, delirium, seizure, coma, and death.

  • Activated charcoal or clay can cause vomiting, aspiration, obstruction, constipation, or electrolyte complications. Excess oxygen may worsen paraquat oxidative injury, while undertreating true hypoxemia is also dangerous.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce emesis, give an oral adsorbent to an unprotected airway, or delay emergency transfer because vomiting and an unprotected airway increase aspiration risk while delay permits further absorption. Do not use oxygen indiscriminately in a nonhypoxemic paraquat patient because excess oxygen can amplify redox injury, but do not withhold oxygen needed for actual hypoxemia.

  • Do not call steroids, cyclophosphamide, hemoperfusion, dialysis, N-acetylcysteine, or another adjunct a proven antidote. Each is a specialist consideration with limited or inconsistent human evidence.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Any ingestion, worsening oral burns, hypotension, rising creatinine, acidosis, hypoxemia, neurologic change, or reduced urine output requires ICU and toxicology escalation. Early goals-of-care support may be appropriate in devastating exposure but must not replace immediate evidence-based stabilization and specialist prognostication.

Interactions

  • Oxygen concentration modifies paraquat redox injury; use only what is clinically necessary. Nephrotoxins and dehydration worsen reduced clearance, while emesis and GI procedures increase aspiration and perforation risks.

  • Commercial formulations contain surfactants or other ingredients that can change corrosive injury and absorption; product identification matters.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A small sip can be fatal, and exploratory contact involves both ingestion and skin exposure; emergency assessment is required regardless of early appearance.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal corrosive injury, shock, and hypoxemia threaten the fetus; no proven fetal or maternal antidote exists, so rapid decontamination and maternal stabilization are central.

  • Population

    Agricultural workers and responders

    Note

    Concentrated formulations and contaminated clothing can expose helpers; PPE and secondary-contamination control are treatment requirements.

Evidence Limitations

  • CDC's 2026 paraquat page is current and explicitly states that no proven antidote exists. Detailed EPA paraquat-diquat clinical material is substantially older and its manual notes authorship under a cooperative agreement; proposed drug regimens and extracorporeal strategies need current medical-toxicology review before local use.

Nclex Traps

  • Paraquat has no proven antidote; immediate adsorbent and decontamination are not proof of cure.

  • Excess oxygen can worsen paraquat oxidative injury, but documented hypoxemia still requires carefully titrated oxygen.

  • Diquat is related to paraquat but does not predictably cause the same classic lung fibrosis pattern.

Regulatory Status

No FDA-approved antidote for paraquat or diquat poisoning exists. Paraquat is a U.S. EPA restricted-use pesticide. Adsorbents, organ support, and proposed adjuncts are toxicology-directed; evidence and local availability vary.

Boxed Warning

PARAQUAT/DIQUAT WARNING: any swallowed amount requires 911 and Poison Help now. Decontaminate and bind unabsorbed herbicide immediately when safe, do not induce vomiting, and do not present oxygen, dialysis, steroids, or another adjunct as a cure; paraquat oxygen should be titrated for actual hypoxemia because excess can worsen oxidative injury.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
