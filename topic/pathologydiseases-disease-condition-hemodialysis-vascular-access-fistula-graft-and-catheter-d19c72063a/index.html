Definition

Hemodialysis vascular access is the route that repeatedly delivers high blood flow to the extracorporeal circuit. An arteriovenous fistula, synthetic graft, or central venous catheter is chosen within an individualized ESKD Life-Plan; no access is best for every patient or every stage of care.

Etiology

Fistulas connect native artery to vein and require maturation; grafts use prosthetic conduit and can often be used sooner but have more stenosis and infection than mature fistulas; catheters provide immediate access but have the highest bloodstream-infection and central-venous injury burden. Vascular anatomy, expected duration, urgency, heart function, prior access, transplant plans, life expectancy, and patient preference shape selection.

Pathology

Arterial pressure and flow remodel a fistula vein, producing a palpable thrill and audible bruit. Repeated cannulation and turbulent flow can create neointimal hyperplasia, stenosis, aneurysm, bleeding, infection, thrombosis, or distal steal; catheters can cause biofilm, fibrin sheath, thrombosis, and central venous stenosis.

Pathophysiology

A continuous thrill indicates flow but does not guarantee adequate dialysis or exclude infection. Stenosis raises circuit pressures, recirculation, prolonged post-needle bleeding, and thrombosis risk; thrombosis abruptly removes the access lifeline. High access flow can increase cardiac preload and rarely contribute to high-output failure, while diversion of arterial blood can cause hand ischemia. Catheter biofilm directly links skin and bloodstream, explaining bacteremia, metastatic infection, and endocarditis risk.

Risk Factors

  • Diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, small vessels, prior catheter or central stenosis

  • Hypotension, dehydration, compression, stenosis, hypercoagulability, or infection

  • Poor cannulation or exit-site technique

  • Pacemakers, prior access, mastectomy or vascular procedures affecting anatomy and planning

Signs Symptoms

  • Healthy fistula or graft has a characteristic thrill and bruit

  • Weak, changed, or absent thrill; difficult cannulation; poor flows; pressure alarms; or prolonged bleeding suggests dysfunction

  • Redness, warmth, drainage, tenderness, fever, or chills suggests infection

  • Cold painful hand, numbness, weakness, ulcers, or reduced capillary refill suggests ischemia

Diagnostics

  • Inspect, palpate, and auscultate before every use

  • Compare access pressures, flows, recirculation, adequacy, and bleeding time trends

  • Use duplex ultrasound or fistulography when stenosis or thrombosis is suspected

  • Obtain blood cultures and assess endocarditis or metastatic infection when catheter or access bacteremia is possible

Labs

  • Potassium and volume become urgent if access failure delays dialysis

  • CBC and cultures support infection evaluation

  • Hemoglobin and coagulation context matter with bleeding

  • Adequacy measures can fall with recirculation or poor access flow

Treatments

  • Create, revise, monitor, or replace access according to the ESKD Life-Plan

  • Treat stenosis with endovascular or surgical strategies and salvage thrombosis urgently when feasible

  • Treat access infection with antibiotics and source control appropriate to access type and severity

  • Use catheter lock, dressing, and connection practices according to protocol

Nursing Priorities

  • Identify the access type, ESKD life plan, maturity, prior interventions, baseline thrill and bruit, and limb circulation because fistulas, grafts, and catheters have different readiness, infection, and thrombosis risks.

  • Inspect and palpate the access before every use for thrill, bruit, redness, drainage, swelling, bleeding, aneurysmal change, distal warmth, sensation, and pulse because early dysfunction threatens dialysis adequacy and limb safety.

  • Use strict hand hygiene, skin antisepsis, catheter-hub technique, and facility cannulation protocols because repeated bloodstream access creates a direct pathway for serious infection.

  • Avoid blood pressure cuffs, venipuncture, constrictive clothing, and unapproved compression on the access limb because trauma or prolonged pressure can reduce flow and precipitate thrombosis.

  • Escalate immediately for absent thrill, uncontrolled bleeding, fever with access findings, hand ischemia, rapidly expanding swelling, or catheter air exposure because access loss, sepsis, hemorrhage, or embolism may result.

Complications

  • Stenosis, thrombosis, recirculation, aneurysm, pseudoaneurysm, rupture, and access loss

  • Cellulitis, bacteremia, endocarditis, sepsis, and metastatic infection

  • Steal syndrome, neuropathy, venous hypertension, and high-output heart failure

  • Catheter thrombosis and central venous stenosis

Contraindications

  • Do not apply a blanket fistula-first rule without individualized life planning

  • Do not use the access arm for routine BP or blood draw

  • Do not cannulate through infected, ulcerated, or dangerously thinned skin

Red Flags

  • Absent or markedly changed thrill or bruit before dialysis

  • Fever, rigors, purulent drainage, or spreading access-site erythema

  • Uncontrolled post-dialysis bleeding or rapidly expanding hematoma

  • Cold painful hand, numbness, weakness, pallor, or reduced distal pulse

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to check their thrill every day and contact the dialysis team immediately if it disappears or changes substantially.

  • Explain that catheters carry the highest bloodstream-infection risk, so dressings must stay clean and dry and hubs should only be handled using trained aseptic technique.

Nclex Traps

  • Fistula, graft, and catheter selection is individualized

  • A thrill does not exclude infection or stenosis

  • Prolonged bleeding can be a stenosis clue

  • A catheter is immediately usable but carries the highest infection burden
