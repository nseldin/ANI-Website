Definition

Alport syndrome is an inherited type IV collagen disorder caused by pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5. Abnormal basement membranes first cause persistent hematuria and later proteinuria and kidney failure; the same collagen network in the cochlea and eye explains associated sensorineural hearing loss and ocular findings.

Pathology

The glomerular basement membrane may begin abnormally thin, then develop irregular thickening, splitting, and lamellation as filtration stress continues. Podocyte injury and scarring follow, converting an initially isolated blood leak into proteinuria, glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and progressive nephron loss.

Pathophysiology

  • The alpha-3, alpha-4, and alpha-5 type IV collagen network provides durable structural support in mature glomerular, cochlear, and ocular basement membranes. A pathogenic COL4 variant weakens that network, so repeated mechanical stress produces injury across all three organs.

  • Proteinuria marks a more advanced filtration-barrier injury than hematuria alone. Reducing angiotensin-II-mediated intraglomerular pressure lowers protein leakage and mechanical stress, which is why early renin-angiotensin-system blockade can delay kidney failure even though it does not repair the gene.

Etiology

X-linked disease results from COL4A5 variants, while autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant forms usually involve COL4A3 or COL4A4; uncommon digenic inheritance also occurs. Risk and age of kidney failure vary with gene, variant, inheritance, sex, proteinuria, blood pressure, and baseline kidney function.

Risk Factors

  • Pathogenic COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5 variant

  • Affected relatives with hematuria, kidney failure, hearing loss, or characteristic ocular disease

  • Early or increasing albuminuria or proteinuria and hypertension

  • A genotype and inheritance pattern associated with more severe collagen-network disruption

Signs Symptoms

  • Persistent microscopic hematuria, sometimes with episodic gross hematuria during illness

  • Increasing albuminuria or proteinuria, hypertension, edema, and later chronic kidney disease

  • Progressive high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss

  • Anterior lenticonus, fleck retinopathy, recurrent corneal erosion, or selected cataract findings

  • A family history that may appear milder in some relatives despite clinically important lifetime risk

Diagnostics

  • Use urinalysis, urine albumin or protein quantification, blood pressure, serum creatinine and eGFR, and a three-generation renal, hearing, and ocular family history.

  • Joint genetic analysis of COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 is preferred because it establishes inheritance, guides family screening, and can prevent an incorrect label of benign familial hematuria.

  • When genetics are unavailable or inconclusive, kidney biopsy with electron microscopy and selected type IV collagen studies can support diagnosis and evaluate competing glomerular disease.

  • Arrange audiology and ophthalmology assessment and test at-risk relatives through genetic counseling rather than relying only on symptoms.

Labs

  • Hematuria is often the earliest laboratory clue and can precede kidney-function decline by years.

  • Urine albumin or protein increases as podocyte and glomerular injury progresses and is a key treatment and prognostic marker.

  • Serum creatinine rises and eGFR falls later; potassium and creatinine must be checked after starting or changing ACE-inhibitor or ARB therapy.

  • Normal hearing or eye findings early in life do not exclude the renal disease.

Assessment

Assess blood pressure, urine color and output, edema, weight, urine albumin or protein trend, creatinine or eGFR, potassium, hearing, vision, pregnancy plans, nephrotoxin exposure, and the family pattern. Explain that persistent hematuria can be genetically important even before pain, edema, or creatinine change appears.

Differential

Differentiate Alport syndrome from IgA nephropathy, other COL4-related thin-basement-membrane phenotypes, C3 glomerulopathy, MYH9-related disease, branchio-oto-renal disorders, and other inherited causes of hearing loss with kidney disease. Do not use benign familial hematuria as a reassuring endpoint without appropriate COL4 evaluation.

Treatments

  • Begin ACE-inhibitor or angiotensin-receptor-blocker nephroprotection at the guideline-indicated stage to reduce intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria and delay kidney failure.

  • Control blood pressure and other CKD risks, avoid nephrotoxins, maintain individualized hydration and sodium guidance, and manage CKD complications as they arise.

  • Use hearing aids, educational accommodations, and ophthalmic treatment to preserve communication, learning, safety, and vision when those complications develop.

  • Plan dialysis or kidney transplantation for advanced disease. Kidney transplantation treats kidney failure but does not change the inherited hearing or ocular risk.

  • Selected adults with proteinuric CKD may receive an SGLT2 inhibitor under current nephrology guidance; it is not a gene-specific cure.

Contraindications

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated during pregnancy because fetal renal injury can occur; establish a preconception and alternative blood-pressure plan.

  • Do not call inherited hematuria benign solely because kidney function is currently normal.

  • A potentially related living kidney donor requires genotype and full renal assessment; a relative with a pathogenic variant or clinically relevant COL4 disease is generally an unsuitable donor.

  • Avoid NSAIDs and unreviewed nephrotoxins, especially during dehydration or declining kidney function.

  • Do not assume absence of hearing loss excludes Alport syndrome in a child or mildly affected adult.

Safety Considerations

  • RAAS blockade can cause hypotension, acute kidney injury, or hyperkalemia during dehydration, intercurrent illness, or interacting therapy.

  • Gross hematuria during infection may be familiar, but oliguria, rapidly rising creatinine, severe edema, or hypertension requires urgent evaluation.

  • Females with X-linked disease can develop substantial proteinuria and kidney failure and require lifelong follow-up.

  • Rare post-transplant anti-GBM nephritis can cause abrupt graft dysfunction and must be distinguished from ordinary rejection or infection.

  • Hearing and vision change affect medication teaching, fall risk, education, and self-management and should be addressed proactively.

Nursing Priorities

  • Trend blood pressure, urine albumin or protein, creatinine or eGFR, potassium, weight, edema, and urine output and document the pattern rather than isolated values.

  • Assess dizziness, dehydration, OTC NSAID use, supplements, and pregnancy potential before and during ACE-inhibitor or ARB therapy.

  • Screen for hearing, school, communication, visual, and fall-related consequences and coordinate audiology, ophthalmology, and accessibility support.

  • Use teach-back for genetic counseling, cascade testing, donor implications, kidney-protection choices, and the fact that relatives may have different severity.

  • Escalate pregnancy-associated hypertension, headache, visual change, rapidly increasing proteinuria, or kidney decline to nephrology and maternal-fetal medicine.

Red Flags

  • Oliguria, rapidly rising creatinine, severe hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, or hypoxemia

  • Severe hypertension, neurologic symptoms, or acute volume overload

  • Pregnancy-associated hypertension, headache, visual symptoms, or rapidly worsening proteinuria

  • Sudden graft dysfunction, hematuria, or proteinuria after kidney transplantation

  • Abrupt hearing or vision change requiring urgent specialty assessment

Complications

  • Progressive chronic kidney disease and kidney failure

  • Sensorineural hearing impairment and communication or educational effects

  • Anterior lenticonus, corneal erosion, retinal changes, or cataract

  • Pregnancy-associated worsening hypertension, proteinuria, preeclampsia, or kidney function

  • Rare post-transplant anti-GBM glomerulonephritis

Prognosis

Prognosis varies greatly by genotype, inheritance, sex, proteinuria, blood pressure, and treatment timing. Early identification and renin-angiotensin-system blockade can materially delay kidney failure. A mildly affected relative does not guarantee a mild course for every family member.

Prevention

The genetic disorder cannot be prevented. Secondary injury can be reduced through early family identification, timely nephroprotection, blood-pressure control, hydration during illness, avoidance of nephrotoxins and smoking, hearing protection, and lifelong renal, auditory, and ocular surveillance.

Patient Education

  • Blood in the urine can be the earliest sign of a collagen disorder even when kidney function feels normal.

  • Take the kidney-protective medicine consistently and follow the sick-day, potassium, and pregnancy instructions provided by the care team.

  • Relatives should seek genetic counseling and testing rather than assuming that mild hematuria is harmless.

  • Report reduced urine, rapid swelling, breathing difficulty, severe dizziness, pregnancy, or new hearing or visual change promptly.

Special Populations

  • Children need early nephrology follow-up, blood-pressure and protein screening, and repeated hearing and eye assessment.

  • Females with X-linked Alport syndrome remain at lifelong kidney risk and should not be dismissed as unaffected carriers.

  • Pregnancy requires preconception medication transition and coordinated nephrology and maternal-fetal monitoring.

  • Transplant candidates and related potential donors need genotype-informed counseling and renal assessment.

Nclex Traps

  • Familial hematuria is not automatically benign; increasing proteinuria signals progression risk.

  • Absence of deafness does not exclude early Alport syndrome.

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs slow kidney damage but do not correct collagen genetics and are contraindicated in pregnancy.

  • Family members can have different severity, so screen each at-risk person rather than predicting from one relative.
