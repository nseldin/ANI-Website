Definition

The nephron is the kidney's microscopic filtering and processing unit: a glomerulus creates filtrate, and a connected tubule changes that filtrate until it becomes urine. Segment specialization explains why a defect or medication at one site produces a predictable combination of volume, electrolyte, and acid-base effects.

Etiology

Normal segmental transport depends on adequate perfusion, epithelial energy, intact transport proteins, hormonal signals, and unobstructed flow. Ischemia, toxins, inherited transporter defects, inflammation, or drugs can selectively disturb one or several segments.

Pathology

The proximal tubule performs bulk reclamation; the thin descending limb is highly water permeable; the thick ascending limb reabsorbs salt without water; the distal convoluted tubule refines sodium and calcium handling; and the collecting system uses aldosterone and ADH to make final potassium, acid, sodium, and water adjustments.

Pathophysiology

Proximal sodium transport drives reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, phosphate, bicarbonate, and water, so proximal injury can cause Fanconi-type solute wasting and bicarbonate loss. The thick ascending limb's NKCC2 transport both dilutes tubular fluid and builds the medullary gradient; blocking it weakens urine concentration and increases distal sodium delivery. Distal NCC transport and collecting-duct ENaC create later sodium reclamation, while ROMK, intercalated cells, and aquaporin-2 link distal delivery and hormones to potassium, acid, and water balance. Because downstream segments can compensate, chronic blockade at one site can produce hypertrophy and diuretic resistance.

Risk Factors

  • Ischemia or sepsis that deprives high-energy tubular cells of oxygen

  • Aminoglycosides, cisplatin, amphotericin, pigments, or other tubular toxins

  • Inherited transporter disorders such as Bartter, Gitelman, Liddle, or cystinuria

  • Diuretics, SGLT2 inhibitors, RAAS-active drugs, and disorders of ADH or aldosterone

Signs Symptoms

  • Polyuria or poor concentrating ability with loop or collecting-duct dysfunction

  • Salt wasting, dehydration, edema, hypertension, or resistant volume overload depending on the affected pathway

  • Predictable potassium, magnesium, calcium, bicarbonate, and pH patterns

  • Glycosuria, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia, or bicarbonaturia with generalized proximal dysfunction

Diagnostics

  • Map urine and serum abnormalities to the likely nephron segment

  • Review medication targets and timing relative to the electrolyte change

  • Use urinalysis, urine electrolytes, osmolality, acid-base testing, and kidney function trends

  • Consider inherited or acquired tubular disorders when patterns persist without an obvious drug or systemic cause

Labs

  • Proximal dysfunction can cause normal-anion-gap acidosis, glycosuria without marked hyperglycemia, hypophosphatemia, and other solute losses

  • Loop dysfunction commonly promotes hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, impaired concentration, and calcium wasting

  • Distal NCC dysfunction favors hypokalemic alkalosis with reduced urinary calcium

  • Collecting-duct defects can produce potassium disorders, distal renal tubular acidosis, or water-balance disorders

Treatments

  • Correct the cause and replace water or electrolytes at a rate matched to severity and ongoing loss

  • Stop or adjust the responsible toxin or medication when clinically appropriate

  • Use segment-specific pharmacology when the therapeutic goal is natriuresis, potassium conservation, aquaresis, or acid-base manipulation

  • Treat obstruction, ischemia, inflammation, or inherited disease rather than treating the laboratory pattern alone

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess renal perfusion, urine volume and concentration, creatinine, edema, acid-base status, and medication exposures because nephron filtration and segmental transport work as an integrated sequence.

  • Trend sodium, potassium, magnesium, bicarbonate, glucose, phosphate, osmolality, and urine findings because proximal, loop, distal, and collecting segments handle different solutes and water.

  • Review where diuretics, sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitors, acid-base drugs, and nephrotoxins act because segment-specific blockade predicts electrolyte loss, retention, or altered concentrating ability.

  • Maintain individualized perfusion and hydration while avoiding unnecessary nephrotoxins because tubular transport requires oxygen and can fail when ischemia or toxic exposure injures nephron cells.

  • Escalate for anuria, rapidly rising creatinine, severe potassium or sodium disturbance, pulmonary edema, profound acidosis, or uremic neurologic change because multiple nephron functions are failing.

Complications

  • Volume depletion or overload

  • Dysrhythmia and neuromuscular dysfunction from electrolyte shifts

  • Metabolic acidosis or alkalosis

  • Progressive tubular injury, acute kidney injury, stones, or medication toxicity

Contraindications

  • Do not assign a transporter diagnosis from one urine electrolyte value during rapidly changing illness

  • Avoid assuming all diuretics act at the same site or create the same calcium and acid-base pattern

Red Flags

  • Anuria or rapidly falling urine output with rising creatinine

  • Severe hyperkalemia or hypokalemia with weakness or ECG changes

  • Symptomatic sodium disturbance, seizure, confusion, or rapid osmotic shift

  • Pulmonary edema, refractory acidosis, pericarditic pain, or uremic encephalopathy

Patient Education

  • Explain that a nephron is a connected processing line: the glomerulus filters plasma, then successive tubular segments selectively reclaim or secrete water, electrolytes, nutrients, and acid.

  • Teach that a normal-looking urine volume does not guarantee normal nephron function, because filtration, concentration, electrolyte control, and acid removal can fail in different combinations.

Nclex Traps

  • The thick ascending limb reabsorbs salt but is relatively impermeable to water

  • ADH acts mainly on collecting-duct water permeability rather than glomerular filtration

  • Distal sodium delivery helps determine potassium and hydrogen secretion

  • A diuretic's electrolyte pattern follows its transport target

Causal Links

  • Segment-specific transporter activity -> characteristic solute movement -> predictable serum and urine pattern

  • More distal sodium delivery -> greater ENaC uptake -> greater potassium and hydrogen secretion

  • TAL salt reabsorption without water -> medullary gradient -> ability to concentrate urine downstream

  • Tubular epithelial injury -> failed reabsorption plus backleak and obstruction -> reduced effective filtration
