Used To Treat

Hypertension, heart failure, selected post-MI left-ventricular dysfunction, and diabetic nephropathy according to labeling. Its short action and evidence history make it important, but frequent dosing and food-sensitive absorption distinguish it from lisinopril.

Mechanism

Captopril's sulfhydryl group coordinates zinc in the ACE active site, competitively inhibiting conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II and degradation of bradykinin. Less AT1 signaling reduces arteriolar tone, aldosterone, sodium retention, sympathetic reinforcement, and remodeling. Higher bradykinin increases endothelial vasodilator signaling while causing cough or angioedema. Efferent-arteriole dilation lowers intraglomerular pressure and albumin leakage, which explains diabetic-nephropathy benefit but also creatinine rise during low renal inflow. Captopril is active without hepatic conversion and is largely renally eliminated. Food reduces absorption substantially, so inconsistent meal timing creates inconsistent peak effect. Its relatively short half-life makes missed doses or timing variation more visible than with long-acting agents.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. Captopril directly suppresses fetal RAAS signaling. Stop as soon as pregnancy is detected despite its short half-life.

Contraindications

  • Previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, hereditary or idiopathic angioedema, pregnancy, and serious captopril hypersensitivity.

  • Do not use with sacubitril/valsartan or within 36 hours, or with aliskiren in diabetes.

  • Pregnancy, previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, and hereditary or idiopathic angioedema where specified by labeling.

  • Do not combine with sacubitril/valsartan or administer within 36 hours; do not combine aliskiren with an ACE inhibitor in diabetes.

Nursing Essentials

  • Give consistently one hour before meals because food reduces absorption about 30% to 40%; this instruction protects predictable exposure, not stomach comfort.

  • Assess CBC and urine protein when renal impairment, collagen-vascular disease, high dose, or infection/proteinuria symptoms make captopril's hematologic or renal adverse effects more relevant.

  • Check pressure and orthostasis, volume, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, pregnancy status, cough, facial or tongue swelling, and every NSAID, diuretic, potassium product, lithium drug, or duplicate RAAS blocker.

  • Teach that persistent dry cough should be evaluated, while lip, tongue, throat, voice, or swallowing change is an emergency because bradykinin swelling can obstruct the airway.

Interactions

  • Potassium, NSAID, diuretic, lithium, and duplicate RAAS interactions follow the class; allopurinol, procainamide, immunosuppressants, or cytotoxic therapy can compound leukopenia concern.

  • Antacids or inconsistent meal timing can alter absorption and apparent response.

  • Potassium supplements or salt substitutes, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, diuretics, lithium, and duplicate RAAS blockers can produce hyperkalemia, AKI, hypotension, or lithium toxicity.

  • ACE inhibitor plus sacubitril/valsartan is contraindicated because overlapping peptide-degradation blockade increases angioedema risk.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR, potassium, pressure, orthostasis, urine protein, and CBC when neutropenia risk is present.

  • Assess taste, rash, fever, sore throat, oral lesions, edema, and urine changes rather than relying only on routine chemistry.

  • Baseline and follow-up potassium and creatinine/eGFR, blood pressure/orthostasis, volume, and urine albumin when kidney protection is the goal.

  • Investigate a major or progressive renal change for dehydration, NSAIDs, diuretics, sepsis, obstruction, hypotension, or renal-artery disease.

Nclex Traps

  • Captopril is the ACE inhibitor classically taken one hour before meals because food lowers absorption. Do not apply that rule automatically to every -pril.

  • Its short action does not make unsupervised sublingual treatment of severe hypertension safe.

  • ACE inhibition lowers angiotensin II and raises bradykinin; those are separate consequences of inhibiting the same enzyme.

  • A modest early creatinine rise can reflect lower intraglomerular pressure, but oliguria, severe hyperkalemia, symptoms, or a large progressive decline is not routine adaptation.

Description

Captopril is an active, short-acting ACE inhibitor with a sulfhydryl group. It lowers angiotensin II and aldosterone and raises bradykinin, but its practical identity comes from fast oral absorption, a short duration requiring multiple daily doses in many regimens, and a 30% to 40% absorption reduction when taken with food. The label therefore directs administration one hour before meals. Its sulfhydryl chemistry and older high-dose experience help explain stronger associations with taste disturbance, rash, proteinuria, and neutropenia, especially in renal impairment or collagen-vascular disease. Captopril has labeled roles in hypertension, heart failure, post-MI ventricular dysfunction, and diabetic nephropathy. It is not simply a sublingual rescue tablet: abrupt unmonitored pressure reduction can impair coronary, cerebral, or renal perfusion.

Adverse Effects

  • Taste loss or metallic taste, rash, mouth irritation, proteinuria, and neutropenia are more recognizable captopril-specific clues than with many later ACE inhibitors.

  • Neutropenia or agranulocytosis risk rises with renal impairment and collagen-vascular disease; infection symptoms require prompt CBC assessment.

  • Class effects include dry cough, dizziness, hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine increase, and less commonly rash or taste change.

  • Airway angioedema, severe AKI, dangerous potassium elevation, neutropenia in susceptible patients, hepatic injury, and fetal toxicity are high-consequence events.
