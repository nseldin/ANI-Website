Definition

Campylobacter jejuni is a clinically important bacterium associated with acute inflammatory gastroenteritis, bacteremia, reactive arthritis, and Guillain-Barre syndrome. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Campylobacter jejuni is a Gram-negative curved rod with a microaerophile oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, motile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Thorough cooking, avoidance of unpasteurized milk, prevention of kitchen cross-contamination, safe water, and hand hygiene reduce infection. The reviewed route description is fecal oral transmission. The reviewed incubation estimate is 2 to 5 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Motility, epithelial adherence and invasion, and cytolethal distending toxin contribute to inflammatory diarrhea; molecular mimicry can trigger postinfectious neuropathy. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Campylobacter jejuni is associated with acute inflammatory gastroenteritis, bacteremia, reactive arthritis, and Guillain-Barre syndrome. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Stool NAAT or culture confirms enteric infection, while blood culture evaluates suspected invasive disease. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
