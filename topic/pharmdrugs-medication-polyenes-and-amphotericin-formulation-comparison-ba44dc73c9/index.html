Used To Treat

Severe invasive mycoses with amphotericin formulations and local mucocutaneous Candida with nystatin, while preventing the dangerous error of substituting one polyene product or route for another.

Description

Polyenes are large macrolide antifungals that bind ergosterol and organize into membrane channels through which potassium, magnesium, protons, and other small solutes leak. Amphotericin B reaches systemic infection by IV administration and covers many severe yeasts, molds, and endemic fungi. Nystatin uses the same broad membrane principle but is negligibly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and is therefore a local oral, topical, or vaginal Candida drug rather than systemic therapy. Amphotericin deoxycholate exposes kidneys and infusion-reactive host tissues more directly; liposomal amphotericin packages drug in phospholipid vesicles that alter distribution and generally reduce nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions. The formulations have different pharmacokinetics and doses, so the same milligram number does not represent equivalent treatment.

Mechanism

Fungal selectivity is relative, not absolute. Amphotericin binds ergosterol more avidly than human cholesterol, but some cholesterol interaction and host-membrane or inflammatory signaling remains, explaining toxicity. In the kidney, afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction lowers renal blood flow and direct distal tubular injury wastes potassium and magnesium; declining filtration then raises creatinine. Cytokine release during infusion produces fever, rigors, hypotension, nausea, and tachypnea. Liposomes preferentially deliver amphotericin into reticuloendothelial and infected tissues while reducing freely available drug at renal membranes, which explains lower but not zero kidney and infusion toxicity. Sodium loading can reduce deoxycholate nephrotoxicity in selected protocols, while hydration and electrolyte replacement do not make monitoring optional.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Amphotericin formulations are not interchangeable by name or dose. Verify exact product, indication, mg/kg dose, diluent, filter, infusion rate, compatibility, renal function, potassium, and magnesium before administration.

Adverse Effects

  • Amphotericin can cause acute infusion reactions, anaphylaxis, AKI, distal tubular dysfunction, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, anemia from reduced erythropoietin, and phlebitis.

  • Nystatin usually causes local irritation, unpleasant taste, nausea, or diarrhea because systemic absorption is minimal; treatment failure in systemic disease is the major danger of using the wrong route.

Contraindications

  • Do not use oral nystatin for candidemia or invasive candidiasis: drug remaining in the lumen cannot reach blood, organs, or deep tissue.

  • Do not substitute amphotericin deoxycholate, liposomal amphotericin, or lipid complex without recalculating and confirming the product-specific regimen.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain baseline and serial creatinine, urine output, potassium, magnesium, CBC, infusion vitals, line assessment, and concomitant nephrotoxin review. Replace electrolytes proactively under protocol because losses may precede severe symptoms.

  • If an infusion reaction occurs, stop or slow according to severity, assess airway and hemodynamics, distinguish expected cytokine symptoms from anaphylaxis, and follow the product-specific premedication or rechallenge plan.

Interactions

  • Aminoglycosides, calcineurin inhibitors, cisplatin, IV contrast, foscarnet, and other nephrotoxins can compound kidney injury. Loop or thiazide diuretics and corticosteroids can worsen potassium loss.

  • Hypokalemia can magnify digoxin toxicity and QT-mediated dysrhythmia risk even when amphotericin is not the primary electrophysiologic drug.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR, urine output, potassium, magnesium, bicarbonate, CBC, liver tests when indicated, and organism/site response. Check ECG when electrolyte loss or other QT-active drugs make dysrhythmia plausible.

  • A rising creatinine and falling potassium or magnesium are mechanistically linked signals of renal vascular and tubular toxicity, not unrelated laboratory abnormalities.

Nclex Traps

  • Liposomal amphotericin is less nephrotoxic, not non-nephrotoxic.

  • Nystatin and amphotericin share a polyene mechanism but not systemic bioavailability or clinical role.

Source Note

Current U.S. amphotericin B liposome label updated April 2026 and current nystatin oral-suspension label updated February 2026.
