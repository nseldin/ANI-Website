Used To Treat

Acute or chronic theophylline, aminophylline, or substantial caffeine toxicity causing vomiting, tremor, tachycardia, hypotension, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, dysrhythmia, or seizure.

Description

Theophylline and caffeine are methylxanthines that can produce a self-amplifying hyperadrenergic emergency. Persistent vomiting, tremor, agitation, tachycardia, hypotension, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, dysrhythmia, and seizure reflect adenosine-receptor blockade, phosphodiesterase inhibition, and catecholamine excess. Chronic theophylline toxicity is especially dangerous because older age, heart or liver disease, fever, and drug interactions reduce clearance; severe dysrhythmia or seizure may occur at a lower concentration than after one acute ingestion. Sustained-release absorption and ongoing enteric or hepatic cycling make serial concentrations essential. Seizures can be sudden and difficult to control. Rescue uses airway support, benzodiazepines, careful electrolyte and volume correction, antiemetics, selected activated charcoal or multiple-dose charcoal, mechanism-aware control of severe tachydysrhythmia, and early hemodialysis for EXTRIP severe criteria. Caffeine shares the physiology, but theophylline numeric thresholds must not be copied blindly to caffeine because assays, kinetics, and evidence differ.

Mechanism

Adenosine normally restrains neuronal firing, heart rate, and catecholamine release. Methylxanthine antagonism removes that brake; phosphodiesterase inhibition raises cyclic AMP, and endogenous epinephrine and norepinephrine rise. The result is tachycardia, beta-2-mediated intracellular potassium shift and hyperglycemia, lipolysis, lactate production, vomiting, and CNS excitation. Potassium can be low from redistribution rather than complete body depletion, so replacement must anticipate rebound. Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A inhibition and treat seizures but do not lower the poison burden. Activated charcoal reduces absorption and, when repeated for theophylline, can enhance elimination through gastrointestinal dialysis. Intermittent hemodialysis rapidly removes theophylline and corrects acid-base and electrolyte abnormalities when seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, shock, rising levels, or other EXTRIP criteria indicate that supportive clearance is too slow.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for seizure, chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, confusion, shock, or a large concentrated-caffeine ingestion. Stop methylxanthines, support airway, obtain continuous ECG, glucose, electrolytes, blood gas, and serial concentrations, and call Poison Help early.

  • Treat seizures promptly with benzodiazepines, correct volume and electrolytes carefully, control vomiting, and use toxicology-directed short-acting beta blockade or other rhythm therapy only when hemodynamics and mechanism support it. Avoid reflex antiarrhythmics that may worsen hypotension or conduction.

  • Use activated charcoal or multiple-dose charcoal only with a protected airway and functioning bowel. Arrange early hemodialysis for theophylline seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, shock, rising concentration or deterioration despite optimal care, and other acute or chronic EXTRIP criteria; individualize caffeine dialysis decisions.

Nursing Essentials

  • Differentiate acute ingestion from chronic accumulation; identify immediate versus sustained-release formulation, caffeine powder or concentrate, total amount, chronic dose, fever, smoking change, heart or liver disease, age, and interacting antibiotics or antiseizure drugs.

  • Continuously monitor rhythm, blood pressure, temperature, respiratory effort, mental status, tremor, vomiting, urine output, and seizure activity because catecholamine excess can abruptly produce dysrhythmia, shock, hyperthermia, or seizure. Trend potassium, magnesium, glucose, lactate, and acid-base status frequently because beta-2-driven potassium shift and lactate production can change rapidly and guide safe correction during severe hyperadrenergic toxicity.

  • Before repeated charcoal, reassess airway, bowel sounds, distention, vomiting, aspiration risk, and stool passage. Coordinate serial concentration timing around charcoal and dialysis, and keep resuscitation equipment ready because deterioration can be abrupt.

Key Labs

  • Serial theophylline concentrations until a definite peak and decline, or a targeted caffeine concentration when available; glucose, potassium, magnesium, phosphate, bicarbonate, blood gas, lactate, creatinine, liver studies, CK, pregnancy testing, and serial ECG.

  • Concentration-risk relationships differ between acute and chronic theophylline exposure, and caffeine may not be measured rapidly. Clinical seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, acid-base trend, and clearance trajectory can outweigh a single number.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe toxicity causes intractable vomiting, aspiration, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, supraventricular or ventricular dysrhythmia, hypotension, seizure, rhabdomyolysis, kidney injury, cerebral injury, and death.

  • Beta blockade can cause bradycardia, bronchospasm, or shock if poorly selected; potassium overreplacement can rebound; charcoal can aspirate or obstruct; and dialysis carries hemodynamic and access risks.

Contraindications

  • Do not use one apparently therapeutic theophylline concentration to dismiss a symptomatic chronic patient because reduced clearance and tissue exposure can cause severe chronic toxicity at concentrations tolerated after an acute ingestion. Do not wait for the level to rise further after seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, or shock because these clinical endpoints already show that supportive clearance is too slow.

  • Do not give repeated charcoal with an unprotected airway, uncontrolled vomiting, ileus, or obstruction. Do not apply EXTRIP theophylline concentration thresholds directly to caffeine without specialist interpretation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Any seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, shock, rising concentration, clinical deterioration, severe chronic toxicity, inability to give charcoal, or persistent metabolic derangement requires ICU, toxicology, nephrology, and early hemodialysis-capable transfer.

Interactions

  • Macrolides, fluoroquinolones, cimetidine, liver disease, heart failure, fever, and smoking cessation can reduce theophylline clearance; enzyme inducers and active smoking can increase clearance and complicate chronic dosing history.

  • Other stimulants, beta agonists, decongestants, thyroid hormone, and sympathomimetics intensify catecholamine effects, while sedatives can obscure early neurologic warning signs without preventing sudden seizure.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Infants and older adults

    Note

    EXTRIP recognizes greater chronic-toxicity risk at lower concentrations in the youngest infants and adults older than sixty.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal seizure, dysrhythmia, and shock require immediate rescue, with fetal monitoring after maternal stabilization and careful interpretation of changed clearance.

  • Population

    Heart, liver, or lung disease

    Note

    Reduced clearance and limited cardiopulmonary reserve increase severe chronic toxicity and complicate beta-blocker or fluid decisions.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP theophylline recommendations include low- to very-low-quality evidence despite consistent dialyzability. Caffeine rescue and dialysis rely more heavily on case reports, so specialists must translate shared mechanism without assuming identical numeric cutoffs.

Nclex Traps

  • Chronic theophylline toxicity can cause severe seizure or dysrhythmia at lower levels than acute ingestion.

  • Hypokalemia often reflects catecholamine-driven intracellular shift, so aggressive replacement can rebound.

  • Multiple-dose charcoal enhances theophylline elimination but requires a protected airway and functioning bowel.

Regulatory Status

No specific antidote exists. Anticonvulsants, beta-blockade, repeated charcoal, and hemodialysis are emergency or toxicology uses; EXTRIP recommendations apply specifically to theophylline, not automatically to caffeine.

Boxed Warning

METHYLXANTHINE WARNING: call 911 for seizure, chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, or shock. Chronic theophylline toxicity can be lethal at a lower level than acute overdose because reduced clearance permits dangerous tissue exposure; obtain serial levels and ECGs because sustained release or ongoing accumulation can make one concentration falsely reassuring, treat seizures promptly, and arrange early hemodialysis for EXTRIP severe criteria.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
