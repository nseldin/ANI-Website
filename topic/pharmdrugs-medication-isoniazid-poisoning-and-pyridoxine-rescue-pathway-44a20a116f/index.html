Used To Treat

Acute isoniazid poisoning with seizure, coma, or severe metabolic acidosis. IV pyridoxine is the specific cofactor-replacement antidote, given with benzodiazepines, airway support, and correction of acid-base and metabolic complications.

Description

Isoniazid is an antimycobacterial antibiotic that, in overdose, depletes pyridoxal-5-phosphate, lowering GABA synthesis and causing the classic severe triad of refractory seizure, profound anion-gap metabolic acidosis, and coma. IV pyridoxine is the specific cofactor-replacement antidote because it restores GABA production; benzodiazepines strengthen the effect of available GABA, so both treatments address complementary parts of the seizure mechanism. Symptoms can begin within 30 minutes to three hours, so a patient can deteriorate before a routine drug level is available. The FDA label gives gram-for-gram pyridoxine replacement when the ingested isoniazid amount is known and an age- and weight-specific initial approach when it is unknown, but exact administration must follow the verified IV product and emergency protocol.

Mechanism

Isoniazid metabolites bind pyridoxal-5-phosphate and inhibit pyridoxine phosphokinase, reducing the cofactor available to glutamate decarboxylase. GABA synthesis falls, excitatory signaling becomes unopposed, and sustained muscle activity produces lactate and severe acidosis. IV pyridoxine replenishes the cofactor, restores GABA synthesis, and can terminate seizures that resist ordinary anticonvulsants. Bicarbonate and ventilation address the acidosis but do not repair GABA synthesis; decontamination can reduce unabsorbed drug but does not replace antidote.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 and Poison Help immediately for suspected large ingestion, seizure, coma, or severe acidosis. Secure the airway, give benzodiazepines for seizure, draw emergency studies, and obtain IV pyridoxine without waiting for an isoniazid concentration.

  • The 2025 FDA label directs initial pyridoxine on a gram-for-gram basis when the isoniazid amount is known; when unknown it provides 5 g in adults or 80 mg/kg in children as an initial approach. Apply the label's rate, pediatric maximum, repeat assessment, and available-concentration instructions rather than extrapolating a routine vitamin dose.

  • Consider activated charcoal only when timing and airway safety support it. Continue critical monitoring and toxicology guidance for recurrent seizure, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, or severe exposure; selected refractory cases may prompt extracorporeal consultation.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify single-agent isoniazid versus combination tuberculosis products and ask about rifampin, pyrazinamide, acetaminophen, alcohol, and intentional co-ingestants. Locate the medication bottle to estimate grams.

  • Pyridoxine rescue can require gram quantities far above nutritional dosing. Inventory enough IV product, calculate total grams and volume independently, verify the formulation, and avoid confusion between milligrams and grams or oral supplements and injectable antidote because an error can leave seizures undertreated or deliver an unintended massive dose.

  • Track seizure duration, airway, glucose, temperature, ECG, blood pressure, urine output, CK, potassium, blood gas, lactate, and neurologic recovery because serial changes reveal persistent seizure injury, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and impending organ failure. Treat hyperthermia and rhabdomyolysis generated by status epilepticus.

Key Labs

  • Blood gas, bicarbonate, anion gap, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, CK, liver tests, ECG, urinalysis, and co-ingestion studies. Isoniazid levels are not usually available soon enough to guide initial rescue.

  • Acetonuria and hyperglycemia can occur, but neither is required. A severe lactic acidosis after sustained seizure should improve as seizures stop; persistent acidosis demands reassessment for shock or co-ingestion.

Adverse Effects

  • Isoniazid poisoning can cause relentless seizures, aspiration, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, severe acidosis, coma, respiratory failure, liver injury, and death.

  • Large rapid pyridoxine exposure can cause sensory neuropathy with prolonged use, infusion effects, or sedation, but acute lifesaving replacement is favored over the immediate neurologic catastrophe of untreated overdose.

Contraindications

  • Do not manage INH status epilepticus with phenytoin as a substitute for pyridoxine; it does not restore depleted GABA synthesis and may not stop the mechanism.

  • Do not delay antidote because a drug concentration, exact pill count, or confirmatory toxicology screen is missing in a compatible severe presentation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent or recurrent seizure after initial pyridoxine and benzodiazepines, profound acidosis, shock, coma, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, or organ failure requires ICU, toxicology, continuous EEG, and rescue escalation. Review whether the initial pyridoxine amount matched the known dose and whether another proconvulsant was ingested.

Interactions

  • Alcohol, malnutrition, pregnancy, HIV, diabetes, kidney disease, and other neurotoxic drugs can increase therapeutic pyridoxine requirements or neuropathy risk, but routine supplementation dosing is not the overdose regimen.

  • Rifampin and pyrazinamide in combination products add hepatic toxicity; bupropion, stimulants, tricyclics, or withdrawal can add seizure mechanisms.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Use the FDA label's weight-based unknown-dose approach and pediatric maximum because a gram-versus-milligram error can be catastrophic in a small child.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Benzodiazepine and pyridoxine rescue should not be delayed because untreated maternal seizure and acidosis threaten both pregnant patient and fetus; arrange obstetric monitoring in parallel.

  • Population

    Malnutrition, alcohol-use disorder, or chronic INH therapy

    Note

    Baseline B6 depletion and neuropathy can coexist, but acute seizures still require overdose-scale IV rescue under protocol.

Evidence Limitations

  • The labeled overdose regimen is based on clinical experience and mechanistic evidence rather than modern randomized trials. Availability of enough IV pyridoxine can be a practical limitation, making pharmacy inventory and poison-center coordination essential.

Nclex Traps

  • The antidote can be dosed in grams, unlike routine vitamin B6 supplementation.

  • Benzodiazepines and pyridoxine are complementary; neither airway support nor cofactor replacement should be delayed.

  • Severe anion-gap acidosis can be generated by persistent seizure and improve only when the seizure mechanism is stopped.

Regulatory Status

The FDA isoniazid label provides pyridoxine rescue instructions for overdose. Exact IV product concentration, rate, repeat dosing, and pediatric maximum must follow current labeling, pharmacy verification, and toxicology guidance.

Boxed Warning

ISONIAZID WARNING: refractory seizure plus severe acidosis or coma is a time-critical antidote emergency. Call 911 and Poison Help, support the airway, give benzodiazepines, and deliver IV pyridoxine using the FDA label's known-dose or unknown-dose pathway; do not wait for a drug level or substitute routine vitamin tablets.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
