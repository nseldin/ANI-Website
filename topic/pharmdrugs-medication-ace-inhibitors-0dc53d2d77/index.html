Description

ACE inhibitors block the enzyme that converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II and degrades bradykinin. Less angiotensin II means less AT1-mediated vasoconstriction, aldosterone release, sodium retention, sympathetic reinforcement, efferent-arteriole constriction, and maladaptive cardiac or renal remodeling. More bradykinin promotes vasodilation but also explains the signature dry cough and part of the angioedema risk. The class can lower systemic and intraglomerular pressure, reduce albuminuria, and improve outcomes in selected heart-failure or post-MI populations. Those benefits do not make every creatinine rise harmless: marked decline can reveal volume depletion, bilateral renal-artery stenosis, NSAID injury, sepsis, or pressure that has fallen below autoregulatory reserve. Agent differences matter: captopril is short acting and food sensitive, enalapril is a prodrug with an IV active form, lisinopril is active and renally excreted unchanged, and fosinopril has compensatory hepatic and renal elimination.

Used To Treat

Hypertension, selected chronic kidney disease with albuminuria, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction when ARNI is not feasible, post-myocardial-infarction remodeling or survival indications for selected agents, and agent-specific cardiovascular-risk indications. The exact indication belongs to the individual drug, not every -pril.

Mechanism

ACE is also called kininase II. Inhibiting it lowers formation of angiotensin II and slows bradykinin degradation. Arteriolar relaxation lowers afterload; reduced aldosterone lowers distal sodium retention and potassium excretion; reduced efferent tone lowers intraglomerular pressure and albumin leakage. In the myocardium and vasculature, less AT1 signaling reduces hypertrophy, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and adverse remodeling. Bradykinin stimulates endothelial nitric oxide and prostacyclin, adding vasodilation. It also increases vascular permeability and sensory-nerve signaling, which helps explain cough and nonhistaminergic angioedema. Because the glomerulus may rely on angiotensin-II-mediated efferent constriction when inflow is poor, ACE inhibition can abruptly reduce filtration in bilateral renal-artery stenosis, severe dehydration, decompensated heart failure, or NSAID-associated afferent constriction. The same causal chain explains why the nurse monitors pressure, creatinine, potassium, and volume together.

Contraindications

  • Pregnancy; previous ACE-inhibitor-related angioedema; hereditary or idiopathic angioedema for many labels; and use with sacubitril/valsartan or within its required 36-hour separation.

  • Do not combine aliskiren with an ACE inhibitor in diabetes. Clarify severe hyperkalemia, symptomatic hypotension, evolving AKI, or suspected bilateral renal-artery stenosis before giving.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the specific ACE inhibitor, indication, formulation, and renal dosing. Check pressure, orthostasis, volume, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, cough, facial/tongue swelling, pregnancy status, and interacting drugs.

  • Teach that lip, tongue, throat, or facial swelling is an airway emergency and can occur even after long exposure. A dry cough is not an allergy, but it may require a class change after other causes are considered.

Interactions

  • Sacubitril/valsartan plus ACE inhibition is contraindicated because overlapping bradykinin-related effects increase angioedema risk; maintain the required 36-hour separation in both directions.

  • Potassium products, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, diuretics, lithium, and duplicate RAAS blockers can convert expected pharmacology into hyperkalemia, AKI, hypotension, or lithium toxicity.

Key Labs

  • Baseline and follow-up creatinine/eGFR and potassium, blood pressure/orthostasis, volume status, urine albumin when kidney protection is the target, and CBC when collagen-vascular disease or neutropenia risk is relevant.

  • Evaluate a major creatinine rise by checking volume loss, pressure, NSAIDs, diuretics, sepsis, obstruction, and renal-artery disease rather than reflexively calling all changes nephrotoxicity.

Patient Education

  • Rise slowly and report dizziness, fainting, swelling of the face/lips/tongue/throat, persistent cough, or decreased urination.

  • Avoid potassium supplements or potassium-based salt substitutes unless prescribed.

Nclex Traps

  • The -pril ending identifies the class, but not identical dosing or indications. Captopril food timing, enalaprilat IV use, and fosinopril elimination are agent-specific.

  • ACE-inhibitor angioedema is often bradykinin mediated, so absence of hives does not make tongue swelling safe and ordinary allergy treatment may not rapidly reverse the airway threat.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. All systemic ACE inhibitors carry this boxed warning. Do not substitute an ARB or ARNI without checking its own fetal warning, because those classes also block fetal RAAS signaling.

Class Pathway

  • RAAS blocker

  • ACE or kininase II inhibition

  • Less angiotensin II and aldosterone plus more bradykinin

Adverse Effects

  • Dry cough, dizziness, first-dose hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine increase, fatigue, headache, and rash or taste disturbance with some agents.

  • Airway angioedema, acute kidney injury, severe hypotension, neutropenia/agranulocytosis in susceptible patients, cholestatic or hepatic injury, and fetal toxicity are high-consequence effects.
