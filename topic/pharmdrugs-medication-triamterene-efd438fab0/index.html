Used To Treat

Edema associated with heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, corticosteroid excess, and selected hyperaldosteronism states under current single-agent labeling; more commonly encountered in fixed combinations with hydrochlorothiazide to limit potassium loss.

Mechanism

Triamterene is secreted into the distal tubular lumen and directly inhibits ENaC in principal cells. Reduced sodium entry makes the lumen less negative, decreasing ROMK-mediated potassium secretion and intercalated-cell hydrogen secretion; hyperkalemia and normal-anion-gap acidosis can result. Because ENaC handles a small sodium fraction, natriuresis is modest unless another upstream diuretic increases distal sodium delivery. Hepatic metabolism forms active p-hydroxytriamterene sulfate, and both parent and metabolite depend on kidney elimination. Poor solubility allows crystalluria and calculi and can contribute to tubular injury. Its pteridine structure interferes with folate pathways, which explains megaloblastic anemia risk in patients whose folate reserve is already low. Direct channel blockade begins without aldosterone signaling, distinguishing it from spironolactone and eplerenone.

Contraindications

  • Hyperkalemia, anuria, acute or severe chronic renal impairment, another potassium-sparing drug, and routine potassium supplementation are central contraindication patterns.

  • A diabetic patient or older adult with reduced renal reserve needs especially careful potassium and kidney assessment.

  • The label contraindicates severe progressive kidney disease, severe hepatic disease, and preexisting elevated potassium. Prior stones require caution rather than dismissal.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check potassium, bicarbonate, creatinine/eGFR, and blood pressure before therapy, early after a start or dose change, and during dehydration or acute illness.

  • Teach avoidance of unreviewed potassium salt substitutes or supplements and urgent reporting of weakness, palpitations, or reduced urine.

  • Ask about kidney stones, hematuria, cirrhosis, nutrition, pregnancy, and macrocytosis. Monitor urine findings and folate-related blood counts when risk or prolonged use warrants.

Interactions

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, potassium products, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, and heparin magnify hyperkalemia.

  • Other diuretics may be intentionally combined for potassium balance, but chemistry and kidney function determine whether the combination remains safe.

  • Indomethacin and other NSAIDs have been associated with acute kidney injury when combined; folate antagonists can compound megaloblastic risk.

Key Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, sodium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, blood pressure, volume, and ECG when hyperkalemia is meaningful.

  • Trend after acute illness or interaction changes even when the chronic dose was previously stable.

  • Add urinalysis, stone symptoms, CBC, and folate assessment when clinically indicated by prolonged exposure, cirrhosis, pregnancy, or macrocytosis.

Nclex Traps

  • ENaC blockers act directly on a luminal channel and do not require aldosterone receptor blockade.

  • Their weak natriuresis does not imply weak hyperkalemia risk.

  • Triamterene is not merely 'like amiloride.' Crystal or stone risk and folate antagonism are distinctive high-yield differences.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Fatal hyperkalemia can occur, especially with renal impairment, diabetes, older age, acute illness, potassium products, RAAS blockers, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, or duplicate potassium-sparing therapy. Triamterene can also crystallize and contribute to nephrolithiasis or renal injury; falling urine output, flank pain, hematuria, or rising creatinine requires prompt review.

Description

Triamterene, historically branded Dyrenium, is a direct ENaC blocker and weak potassium-sparing diuretic. It is often paired with hydrochlorothiazide because the thiazide wastes potassium while triamterene reduces distal potassium secretion. It does not block the mineralocorticoid receptor and does not reproduce spironolactone's anti-remodeling evidence. Oral absorption and response are variable, and an active sulfate metabolite contributes to effect. Triamterene and its metabolites are poorly soluble in urine and can form crystals or kidney stones; prior nephrolithiasis, low urine flow, and renal impairment therefore matter. It also has folate-antagonist properties and can contribute to megaloblastic anemia in patients with depleted folate stores, especially cirrhosis or pregnancy. The combination may normalize potassium on average, but either hypokalemia or hyperkalemia can still occur.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyperkalemia, normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, nausea, diarrhea, headache, and dizziness follow reduced ENaC-mediated sodium entry and potassium or hydrogen secretion.

  • Dangerous hyperkalemia can cause weakness, paresthesia, bradycardia, conduction block, ventricular arrhythmia, or arrest.

  • Crystalluria, kidney stones, interstitial nephritis, acute kidney injury, photosensitivity, hepatic injury, thrombocytopenia, and megaloblastic anemia in folate deficiency are distinctive concerns.
