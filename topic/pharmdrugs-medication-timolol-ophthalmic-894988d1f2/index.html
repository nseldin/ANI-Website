Used To Treat

Elevated intraocular pressure in open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension, often with another pathway when one agent is insufficient.

Mechanism

Blocking beta receptors on ciliary epithelium lowers cAMP-dependent ion and fluid transport into the posterior chamber, reducing aqueous formation and intraocular pressure. Drug draining through the nasolacrimal duct reaches vascular nasal mucosa without first-pass metabolism, so a small eye dose can produce systemic beta-1 cardiac and beta-2 bronchial blockade. Punctal occlusion reduces that exposure.

Contraindications

  • Bronchial asthma or history of asthma, severe COPD, sinus bradycardia, second- or third-degree AV block, overt cardiac failure, cardiogenic shock and hypersensitivity.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check apical pulse, rhythm, blood pressure, asthma/COPD history, heart-failure symptoms and concurrent beta/AV-node-slowing drugs before use. Teach the patient to report wheeze, syncope, marked fatigue or edema.

  • Verify the exact eye, formulation, concentration, frequency, contact-lens instructions, and whether other drops must be separated by at least 5 minutes. Never let the bottle tip touch the eye, lashes, fingers, or another surface.

  • After the drop, gently close the eyelid and press the nasolacrimal duct for 1 to 2 minutes when appropriate. This reduces drainage into the nose and systemic exposure; wiping excess from the skin also limits local irritation.

Interactions

  • Oral beta blockers add systemic blockade. Verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin, amiodarone and other AV-node-slowing drugs increase bradycardia/heart-block risk. CYP2D6 inhibitors can raise systemic timolol exposure. Beta blockade can blunt epinephrine response and mask hypoglycemia tachycardia.

Key Labs

  • Intraocular pressure, optic nerve and visual fields

  • Apical pulse, blood pressure, ECG when conduction risk exists, respiratory assessment/peak flow when clinically indicated

  • Glucose awareness in insulin or sulfonylurea users

Nclex Traps

  • Eye-drop timolol can still trigger fatal bronchospasm.

  • Punctal occlusion directly reduces systemic cardiac and pulmonary exposure.

  • Do not assume a normal eye exam means the pulse and lungs can be ignored.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Systemic absorption can reproduce severe systemic beta-blocker reactions, including death from bronchospasm in asthma and cardiac failure. Contraindications include asthma/history of asthma, severe COPD, sinus bradycardia, second- or third-degree AV block without appropriate pacing, overt heart failure and cardiogenic shock.

Description

Timolol ophthalmic is a nonselective beta blocker placed in the eye to reduce aqueous-humor production by ciliary epithelium. It has no meaningful membrane-stabilizing or intrinsic sympathomimetic activity. The critical lesson is that topical does not mean local-only: nasolacrimal absorption can cause systemic bradycardia, heart block, heart-failure worsening and life-threatening bronchospasm.

Adverse Effects

  • Ocular burning/dryness, blurred vision, bradycardia, hypotension, heart block, heart-failure worsening, bronchospasm, fatigue, depression, sexual dysfunction and masking of adrenergic hypoglycemia signs.
