Used To Treat

Rhino-orbital-cerebral, pulmonary, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, or disseminated mucormycosis in patients with diabetes/ketoacidosis, neutropenia, transplantation, corticosteroid exposure, trauma, burns, iron overload, or other major risk.

Description

Mucormycosis is a rapidly progressive infection caused by Mucorales molds whose broad hyphae invade blood vessels, causing thrombosis, ischemia, and tissue necrosis. Nasal congestion, facial pain, orbital swelling, cranial neuropathy, black eschar, pulmonary infarction/hemoptysis, or necrotic wounds in a high-risk patient are emergency clues. High-dose liposomal amphotericin B is strongly recommended first-line in global guidance; IV isavuconazole or IV/delayed-release posaconazole are important alternatives or salvage options based on patient and disease. Surgery is often indispensable because thrombosed vessels cannot deliver adequate drug into dead tissue. Correcting ketoacidosis, reducing immunosuppression when feasible, and restoring neutrophils remove conditions that allow continued invasion.

Mechanism

Mucorales use angioinvasion to obtain tissue access, while uncontrolled hyperglycemia, acidosis, available iron, and impaired phagocytes weaken host defense. Acidosis reduces transferrin iron binding, increasing available iron; hyperglycemia and ketoacidosis also impair neutrophil function. Once vessels thrombose, ischemic tissue becomes both a fungal reservoir and a pharmacologic sanctuary. Debridement physically removes that reservoir and reopens a margin where perfused tissue can receive antifungal. Voriconazole lacks Mucorales activity, so prior voriconazole exposure does not provide protection and can create false reassurance. Repeated imaging and surgery may be necessary because visible surface necrosis can underestimate deeper spread.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not wait for a black eschar; it is a late and inconsistent sign. High-risk host plus compatible orbital, sinus, pulmonary, or necrotic-tissue findings warrants urgent ENT, ophthalmology, surgery, infectious-disease, imaging, and tissue diagnosis.

Adverse Effects

  • Untreated disease can cause orbital apex syndrome, blindness, cavernous-sinus thrombosis, stroke, massive hemoptysis, disseminated infarction, and death.

  • Therapy adds amphotericin renal/electrolyte toxicity or azole hepatic/interaction toxicity, but toxicity monitoring must occur alongside rather than delay source control.

Contraindications

  • Do not use voriconazole monotherapy for mucormycosis.

  • Do not rely on a negative superficial swab. Deep tissue histopathology and culture are needed because devitalized surface material may miss the invasive front.

Nursing Essentials

  • Escalate facial numbness, eye pain, ophthalmoplegia, vision loss, black/gray tissue, cranial-nerve change, hemoptysis, or rapidly spreading wound necrosis immediately.

  • Coordinate time-critical imaging, operating-room preparation, tissue transport for pathology/culture, amphotericin hydration/electrolytes, glycemic and acid-base correction, and serial wound/neurologic assessment.

Interactions

  • Corticosteroids, uncontrolled glucose, acidosis, neutropenia, deferoxamine exposure, and excessive immunosuppression are modifiable disease drivers when clinically feasible.

  • Posaconazole and isavuconazole have major CYP3A interactions with transplant and oncology drugs; levels and preemptive dose changes may be required.

Key Labs

  • Glucose, ketones, pH/bicarbonate, CBC/neutrophils, creatinine, potassium, magnesium, liver tests, tissue histopathology/culture, species/susceptibility when available, and serial site-specific imaging.

  • Serum fungal biomarkers may be negative because common galactomannan and beta-D-glucan assays do not reliably detect Mucorales; a negative result does not exclude disease.

Nclex Traps

  • The black eschar is memorable but not required. Earlier pain, edema, cranial-nerve or orbital findings may be more actionable.

  • Drug plus debridement is not redundant: necrotic avascular tissue is precisely where systemic drug delivery is weakest.

Source Note

ECMM/MSG global mucormycosis guideline and current U.S. liposomal amphotericin, Cresemba, and posaconazole labels.
