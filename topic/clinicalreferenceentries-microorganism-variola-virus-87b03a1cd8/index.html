Definition

Variola virus is a clinically important virus associated with smallpox, variola major, and variola minor. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

  • Content

    Variola virus is an acellular, obligately intracellular virus with a dsdna genome and a enveloped particle. Envelope status helps explain environmental stability and susceptibility to routine controls, while genome type shapes replication and diagnostic targets. Capsid symmetry is left explicitly unknown in this bounded record rather than inferred without a reviewed taxonomic source.

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Global vaccination eradicated naturally occurring smallpox; preparedness relies on surveillance, secure containment, exposure vaccination, and public-health response plans. The reviewed route description is respiratory particle transmission. The reviewed incubation estimate is 7 to 19 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Variola virus spreads from the respiratory tract through lymphatic and bloodstream phases before producing systemic toxicity and a deep, synchronous pustular rash. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Variola virus is associated with smallpox, variola major, and variola minor. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.
