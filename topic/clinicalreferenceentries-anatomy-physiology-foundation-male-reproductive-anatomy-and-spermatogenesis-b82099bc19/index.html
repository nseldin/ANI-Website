Summary

The male reproductive system makes sperm and androgens, matures and transports sperm, adds glandular secretions to form semen, and delivers it through the urethra. Spermatogenesis occurs inside seminiferous tubules under coordinated FSH, testosterone, Sertoli-cell, and local control; Leydig cells make testosterone in response to LH. Production takes weeks and requires a temperature below core body temperature, while epididymal transit adds motility and functional maturation.

Quick Answer

Testes lie in the scrotum. Seminiferous tubules contain developing germ cells supported by Sertoli cells; interstitial Leydig cells make testosterone. Sperm leave through the rete testis and efferent ducts, mature in the epididymis, travel through the vas deferens and ejaculatory ducts, mix with seminal-vesicle and prostate secretions, and exit through the urethra. Pulsatile GnRH stimulates pituitary LH and FSH. LH drives Leydig testosterone, while FSH and high local testosterone support Sertoli cells, the blood-testis barrier, germ-cell development, androgen-binding protein, and inhibin B. Exogenous testosterone can improve serum androgen symptoms yet suppress LH and FSH, lower intratesticular testosterone, and markedly reduce sperm production—an essential fertility counseling point.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    Each testis contains lobules of coiled seminiferous tubules that drain through the rete testis and efferent ductules into the epididymis. The epididymis lies along the posterior testis and continues as the muscular vas deferens, which travels within the spermatic cord through the inguinal canal and joins the seminal-vesicle duct. Ejaculatory ducts pass through the prostate into the urethra. Seminal vesicles contribute much of semen volume and nutrient-rich alkaline fluid; the prostate adds enzymes and fluid; bulbourethral glands add lubrication. The penis contains erectile tissues surrounding vascular spaces, and the scrotum, pampiniform venous plexus, and cremasteric mechanisms regulate testicular temperature.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: spermatogenesis

    Text

    Stem spermatogonia divide, selected cells enter meiosis as primary spermatocytes, and two meiotic divisions reduce chromosome number to form haploid spermatids. Spermiogenesis reshapes spermatids into spermatozoa with a condensed nucleus, acrosome, mitochondrial midpiece, and flagellum. Sertoli cells nourish and organize developing cells, form a blood-testis barrier that protects later-stage germ cells from inappropriate immune exposure, remove residual material, and secrete regulatory proteins. Newly released sperm are not fully motile or fertilization-capable; epididymal transit modifies them. The process is continuous after puberty but slow, so fever, toxin, chemotherapy, or hormonal suppression may affect semen weeks to months after exposure and recovery also takes time.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: hormonal control and sexual function

    Text

    Pulsatile GnRH stimulates LH and FSH. LH activates Leydig cells to produce testosterone; FSH acts on Sertoli cells, and high intratesticular testosterone supports sperm formation. Testosterone and estradiol provide negative feedback, while inhibin B selectively restrains FSH. Testosterone supports reproductive tissues, muscle, bone, erythropoiesis, libido, and secondary sex traits; conversion to DHT strengthens androgen action in selected tissues. Parasympathetic signaling and nitric oxide promote arterial dilation and erection, while sympathetic and somatic coordination support emission, ejaculation, and closure patterns. Erectile function, orgasm, ejaculation, sperm count, and fertility overlap but are not interchangeable outcomes.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Scrotal position, countercurrent heat exchange in the pampiniform plexus, and muscle reflexes keep testes cooler than core temperature. Cryptorchidism, sustained heat, varicocele, severe fever, systemic illness, undernutrition, toxins, radiation, and medicines can impair production. The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis changes with sleep, age, obesity, stress, and chronic disease. Accessory-gland fluid affects semen volume and sperm environment but most ejaculate volume is not sperm. The urethra is shared by urinary and reproductive systems, although coordinated sphincter and autonomic activity normally separates functions. Prostate enlargement may obstruct urine without directly proving cancer or loss of spermatogenesis.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Primary testicular failure lowers sperm or testosterone output and removes feedback, so LH or FSH may rise. Central hypothalamic or pituitary failure can leave testosterone and gonadotropins inappropriately low. Obstruction may produce absent sperm despite preserved hormone production. Varicocele can alter temperature and local environment. Testicular torsion twists the spermatic cord, first compromising venous drainage and then arterial perfusion, risking infarction. Infection or inflammation can affect epididymis, testis, or prostate. Erectile dysfunction can arise from vascular, neurologic, endocrine, medication, or psychologic factors and may signal cardiovascular disease. Infertility is a couple-level outcome; one semen sample or one testosterone level rarely explains it fully.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Exogenous testosterone and anabolic steroids suppress GnRH, LH, and FSH through feedback, reducing intratesticular testosterone and sperm even when blood testosterone is high. Alkylating chemotherapy and radiation can damage rapidly dividing germ cells; fertility preservation should be discussed before exposure when feasible. Alpha blockers relax prostate and bladder-neck smooth muscle, while 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors reduce DHT-related prostate growth and alter PSA interpretation and sexual effects. Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors enhance nitric-oxide signaling for erection but require cardiovascular and nitrate safety review. Antibiotics treat susceptible infection, not torsion. Hormonal fertility therapies depend on whether failure is central, testicular, or obstructive; empiric testosterone is not a fertility treatment.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    Semen analysis evaluates volume, concentration, motility, morphology, and other features after standardized collection; abnormal results often require confirmation because fever, abstinence interval, collection, and time to analysis matter. Morning testosterone measurement and repeat confirmation may be appropriate because secretion varies; LH and FSH help distinguish testicular from central causes. Prolactin, thyroid, genetic, or other tests answer selected patterns. Scrotal Doppler ultrasound evaluates blood flow, mass, hydrocele, or varicocele, but urgent torsion decisions should not be delayed when clinical concern is high. PSA is prostate-derived and can rise for benign, inflammatory, or malignant reasons; it is not a stand-alone cancer diagnosis or a measure of sperm production.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess pain onset and location, swelling, trauma, fever, urinary symptoms, discharge, sexual exposure, erectile and ejaculatory function, fertility goals, medications and supplements, prior surgery, cancer therapy, and endocrine symptoms. Preserve privacy, use consent and a chaperone according to policy, and avoid assumptions about identity, partners, anatomy, or goals. Sudden testicular pain requires rapid escalation and NPO preparation because surgical treatment may be time-critical. Teach monthly familiarity or disease-specific self-assessment without implying that self-examination replaces evaluation. Before testosterone or gonadotoxic treatment, ask explicitly about future fertility. Explain specimen collection steps precisely because incomplete collection can falsely lower semen volume or count.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Sudden severe unilateral testicular pain, high-riding or horizontal testis, nausea, or absent cremasteric response suggests torsion and needs emergency surgical evaluation. Do not wait for fever or assume relief after intermittent pain means safety. Scrotal trauma with severe swelling, rapidly spreading perineal pain or skin change, systemic toxicity, urinary retention with severe distress, or a painful erection lasting several hours requires urgent care. A painless testicular mass also needs prompt evaluation even though it is not usually an immediate resuscitation emergency. Fever and scrotal pain may be infection, but torsion must be addressed first when the presentation fits because antibiotics cannot restore twisted blood flow.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Sperm are made in seminiferous tubules, not the prostate, and they mature mainly in the epididymis. Most semen volume comes from accessory glands, so normal volume does not guarantee sperm are present. Testosterone therapy can suppress fertility rather than enhance it. A vasectomy interrupts sperm transport but does not immediately sterilize stored distal semen, stop testosterone production, or prevent ejaculation. Erectile ability does not prove normal sperm count, and infertility does not equal impotence. Torsion can occur without infection signs and is not safely excluded by age alone. A high PSA does not equal cancer, and a normal PSA does not evaluate testicular disease.
