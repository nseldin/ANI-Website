Used To Treat

Selected severe systemic fungal infections when conventional amphotericin is specifically indicated and its toxicity can be managed. Many modern protocols prefer a lipid formulation when equivalent efficacy and access permit, especially with renal risk or prolonged therapy.

Description

Amphotericin B deoxycholate is the conventional IV formulation of an ergosterol-binding, membrane-pore-forming fungicidal polyene antifungal solubilized with deoxycholate. Compared with liposomal amphotericin, it exposes host tissues to more free drug and causes more infusion reactions, renal vasoconstriction, tubular injury, potassium/magnesium wasting, and anemia. This formulation can still be appropriate in selected infections and resource settings, but its dose is substantially lower than typical liposomal mg/kg dosing. The danger is not merely that it is an 'older drug'; it is that formulation pharmacology changes both dose and toxicity.

Mechanism

Amphotericin binds fungal ergosterol and forms membrane channels. Deoxycholate micelles release active drug in plasma without the same liposomal sequestration, increasing contact with renal and immune cells. Renal afferent vasoconstriction reduces filtration, and direct distal tubular injury wastes electrolytes and can cause type 1 renal tubular acidosis. Inflammatory cytokine release produces the characteristic infusion syndrome. Sodium loading before selected doses can reduce renal vasoconstrictive injury in some protocols, but it cannot prevent direct tubular toxicity or substitute for monitoring.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: High-alert formulation: deoxycholate is not interchangeable with liposomal amphotericin. A liposomal dose accidentally given as deoxycholate can be catastrophically toxic.

Adverse Effects

  • Frequent fever, rigors, nausea/vomiting, hypotension, headache, tachypnea, phlebitis, and rare anaphylaxis during infusion.

  • Dose-limiting AKI, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, renal tubular acidosis, anemia, and less common hepatic, platelet, or leukocyte abnormalities.

Contraindications

  • Serious product hypersensitivity is a major contraindication unless life-saving benefit outweighs risk.

  • Avoid casual use when a less toxic active alternative exists, particularly with baseline kidney injury, severe electrolyte instability, or unavoidable nephrotoxin exposure.

Nursing Essentials

  • Double-check that the calculated mg/kg dose belongs to deoxycholate. Verify product, diluent, test-dose policy, infusion time, premedication, hydration, and line compatibility.

  • Check creatinine, urine output, potassium, magnesium, bicarbonate, CBC, weight, and fluid status frequently. Anticipate replacement rather than waiting for dysrhythmia or weakness.

Interactions

  • Calcineurin inhibitors, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, foscarnet, contrast, and other nephrotoxins compound renal injury.

  • Diuretics/corticosteroids worsen electrolyte loss; low potassium increases digoxin and ventricular dysrhythmia risk.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR, urine output, potassium, magnesium, bicarbonate, CBC/hemoglobin, liver tests when indicated, and infection-specific cultures/imaging.

  • Falling bicarbonate with urine pH that remains inappropriately high can reflect distal tubular acidification injury.

Nclex Traps

  • Conventional amphotericin uses lower mg/kg doses than liposomal products; more milligrams does not mean a stronger formulation.

  • Premedication can reduce symptoms but does not prevent nephrotoxicity.
