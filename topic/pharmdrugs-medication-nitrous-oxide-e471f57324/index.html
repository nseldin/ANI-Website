Class

Inhaled NMDA-antagonist analgesic/anxiolytic and weak general-anesthetic gas

Used To Treat

Adjunct analgesia and anxiolysis for dental, obstetric and procedural care and as part of balanced general anesthesia; it is too weak to provide surgical anesthesia alone at normobaric pressure.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Use only with an oxygen-delivery fail-safe, adequate ventilation, scavenging and personnel trained in inhaled sedation. Avoid when expansion of a closed gas space could harm the patient, including untreated pneumothorax, air embolus, recent intraocular gas, trapped intracranial air or severe bowel distention. Give oxygen during washout to prevent diffusion hypoxia. Repeated/prolonged exposure can cause functional B12 deficiency, marrow suppression, neuropathy and myelopathy.

Mechanism

Analgesia is driven largely by NMDA receptor antagonism plus endogenous opioid and descending noradrenergic pathways; GABAergic and other targets contribute sedation. Its low blood-gas solubility produces rapid onset and offset. Movement down its steep partial-pressure gradient into closed gas spaces increases volume or pressure. Oxidation of vitamin B12 cobalt inactivates methionine synthase, impairing methionine and DNA/myelin metabolism.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify oxygen fail-safe function, suction, airway readiness, scavenging and fire/oxidizer precautions. Continuously observe consciousness, ventilation and oxygenation; nitrous oxide supports combustion even though it is not itself flammable.

  • Ask specifically about pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle-ear surgery, intracranial air and retinal gas-bubble procedures. Administer oxygen during recovery and monitor until cognition, gait and protective reflexes return.

Contraindications

  • Any clinically important closed gas space that cannot safely expand, inability to cooperate or protect the airway, severe hypoxia without adequate oxygen delivery, and known/suspected severe vitamin B12 or methionine-synthase impairment when exposure would worsen it. Clarify recent retinal surgery with gas bubble.

Interactions

  • Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol and other anesthetics increase sedation and respiratory risk and lower MAC requirements. Other folate/B12 pathway problems or poor nutrition increase toxicity from repeated exposure.

Key Labs

  • Continuous oxygenation, ventilation and clinical sedation monitoring

  • CBC, vitamin B12 with methylmalonic acid/homocysteine and neurologic examination when chronic exposure or deficiency is suspected

  • Imaging/clinical confirmation of closed-space pathology when relevant

Nclex Traps

  • Nitrous oxide expands trapped gas; a pneumothorax can worsen even while the patient seems comfortably sedated.

  • It supplies analgesia but is not potent enough for surgical anesthesia alone.

  • Give oxygen during washout because rapid gas exit can transiently dilute alveolar oxygen.

Description

Nitrous oxide is a rapidly equilibrating inhaled gas that provides meaningful analgesia and anxiolysis with weak hypnotic potency. It diffuses into air-filled spaces faster than nitrogen leaves, so it can expand a pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, intracranial air, middle-ear gas or intraocular gas bubble. Abrupt discontinuation can dilute alveolar oxygen and cause diffusion hypoxia. Prolonged or repeated exposure oxidizes cobalamin and inhibits methionine synthase, risking megaloblastic change and neurologic injury.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea/vomiting, dizziness, dysphoria, diffusion hypoxia, expansion of closed gas spaces, impaired judgment, pressure injury, and with prolonged/repeated exposure megaloblastic anemia, leukopenia, neuropathy, myelopathy, reproductive/occupational concerns and abuse-related neurologic injury.
