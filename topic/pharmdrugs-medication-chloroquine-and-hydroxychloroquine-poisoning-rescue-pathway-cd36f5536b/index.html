Used To Treat

Acute chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine poisoning with rapidly developing hypotension, conduction delay, ventricular dysrhythmia, hypokalemia, seizure, coma, or arrest. No neutralizing antidote exists; expert resuscitation may use early airway control, epinephrine, diazepam, bicarbonate, cautious potassium replacement, and extracorporeal support in refractory shock.

Description

Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine are aminoquinoline antimalarials whose sodium- and potassium-channel blockade can produce life-threatening conduction delay, dysrhythmia, hypotension, and arrest within one to three hours. Sodium- and potassium-channel blockade and direct myocardial depression produce QRS and QT prolongation, ventricular dysrhythmia, heart block, hypotension, and arrest. Severe hypokalemia often reflects intracellular shift rather than total-body depletion, so aggressive normalization can cause rebound hyperkalemia as toxicity resolves. Seizures and rapid loss of consciousness can make early airway control safer than waiting for collapse. Chloroquine experience supports epinephrine and high-dose diazepam in severe poisoning, but protocols vary, evidence is largely observational, and hydroxychloroquine is often extrapolated rather than equivalently studied. Hemodialysis does not meaningfully remove these highly distributed drugs.

Mechanism

Fast sodium-channel blockade slows phase-zero depolarization and widens the QRS; potassium-channel blockade prolongs repolarization and QT, while myocardial and vascular effects drive shock. Catecholamine-stimulated intracellular potassium uptake contributes to profound serum hypokalemia. Epinephrine supports vascular tone, heart rate, and contractility. Diazepam treats seizure and has been used in severe chloroquine poisoning for proposed cardioprotective effects, but it is not a molecular antidote. Sodium bicarbonate may improve sodium-channel conduction in selected patients, while potassium replacement treats dangerous deficiency but must anticipate redistribution back out of cells.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 and Poison Help immediately for any significant intentional or symptomatic exposure. Place on continuous ECG, obtain IV access and frequent potassium and blood gases, consider early controlled intubation before abrupt CNS or cardiovascular collapse, and use activated charcoal only when early and airway safe.

  • Use epinephrine, diazepam, sodium bicarbonate, magnesium, potassium, defibrillation, and other resuscitation elements under a medical-toxicology and critical-care protocol matched to shock, seizure, QRS, QT, and rhythm. Exact high-dose diazepam and epinephrine regimens are not universal and should not be improvised from an old chloroquine label.

  • For refractory cardiogenic shock or arrest despite maximal pharmacologic support, activate a VA-ECMO-capable center early. Do not delay transfer while attempting dialysis as a toxin-removal strategy.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify hydroxychloroquine versus chloroquine, tablet strength, amount, time, immediate- or delayed vomiting, co-ingestants, and patient weight. Chronic retinal-toxicity counseling is a different search intent from acute overdose.

  • Use continuous ECG and invasive hemodynamic monitoring when severe. Recheck potassium frequently during replacement and recovery, document total administered, and avoid chasing a normal value because the low serum potassium often reflects intracellular shift and can rebound to dangerous hyperkalemia as toxicity resolves.

  • Prepare for simultaneous airway, vasopressor, antiarrhythmic, seizure, and electrolyte tasks. Independently verify concentrated potassium and bicarbonate because resuscitation urgency increases medication-error risk.

Key Labs

  • Serial ECG with QRS and QTc, potassium, magnesium, calcium, glucose, blood gas, bicarbonate, lactate, creatinine, hepatic studies, and co-ingestion tests. Drug concentrations are not usually available rapidly enough to guide resuscitation.

  • Severe hypokalemia is a prognostic marker and treatment target, but the total-body potassium deficit may be modest; monitor for rebound hyperkalemia as channels and catecholamine effects recover.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause vomiting, visual symptoms, profound hypotension, severe hypokalemia, seizure, coma, QRS or QT prolongation, torsades, ventricular fibrillation, cardiac arrest, and death.

  • Epinephrine can worsen tachydysrhythmia and lactate; large diazepam regimens require airway and ventilation; bicarbonate can cause alkalemia and sodium load; potassium can cause fatal hyperkalemia if over-replaced.

Contraindications

  • Do not delay airway and vasopressor planning in a rapidly deteriorating patient, and do not treat hypokalemia as ordinary total-body depletion with a fixed replacement recipe.

  • Do not claim dialysis is effective toxin removal, and do not present diazepam, epinephrine, bicarbonate, insulin, or lipid emulsion as a universally proven antidote. Each is a physiology-directed or evidence-limited rescue intervention.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Widening QRS, prolonged QT with ventricular ectopy, rising vasopressor need, seizure, coma, acidosis, ventricular dysrhythmia, or arrest requires ICU, medical toxicology, and early ECMO-center activation. Continue ECG and potassium surveillance after initial stability because redistribution and long half-lives can prolong risk.

Interactions

  • Other QT-prolonging, sodium-channel-blocking, potassium-lowering, or cardiodepressant drugs increase dysrhythmia and shock. Beta blockers can oppose catecholamine rescue, while diuretics can add true potassium depletion.

  • Strong CYP inhibitors affect therapeutic exposure but acute overdose severity is driven more by dose, rapid absorption, and distribution; do not delay resuscitation for interaction analysis.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A small tablet count can be rapidly fatal; exact strength and weight are urgent, and airway and potassium errors are magnified.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal shock and dysrhythmia are immediate fetal threats; stabilize aggressively with obstetric support, recognizing that evidence for adjunct regimens in pregnancy is limited.

  • Population

    Cardiac disease or baseline long QT

    Note

    Lower reserve and concomitant QT drugs increase deterioration risk and justify early intensive monitoring.

Evidence Limitations

  • The 2025 AHA toxicology resuscitation guideline explicitly places chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine management outside its scope. The cited hydroxychloroquine label is from 2023 and the chloroquine label is from 2009; diazepam, epinephrine, bicarbonate, lipid, insulin, and ECMO strategies rely on mixed mechanistic, animal, observational, and case evidence. Current poison-center review is mandatory.

Nclex Traps

  • Severe hypokalemia is largely a shift and can rebound during recovery.

  • High-dose diazepam in chloroquine protocols is more than ordinary seizure dosing but remains specialist directed and is not a neutralizing antidote.

  • Hydroxychloroquine chronic retinal monitoring and acute overdose resuscitation are separate pathways.

Regulatory Status

No FDA-approved specific antidote exists for chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine overdose. Label overdose sections describe supportive interventions, but modern dosing and escalation are off-label, center-specific, and medical-toxicology directed; source age and extrapolation are explicit limitations.

Boxed Warning

ANTIMALARIAL-OVERDOSE WARNING: chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine can cause fatal shock and dysrhythmia within hours. Call 911 and Poison Help, obtain immediate ECG and potassium, prepare early airway and epinephrine-based support, and replace potassium only with continuous reassessment because rebound hyperkalemia can follow the initial intracellular shift.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
