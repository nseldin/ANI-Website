Used To Treat

Thick respiratory secretions by inhalation and acetaminophen poisoning by oral or intravenous protocols. These roles use the same sulfur-containing molecule but different routes, formulations, doses, monitoring, and immediate hazards.

Description

Acetylcysteine is not a generic respiratory medicine. As an inhaled mucolytic, its free sulfhydryl group reduces disulfide bonds that cross-link mucin proteins, making secretions less viscous but often more voluminous; the patient must be able to cough or be suctioned. As an acetaminophen antidote, it supplies cysteine for hepatic glutathione synthesis and can directly support detoxification of the reactive metabolite NAPQI. Early treatment is most protective, but clinically indicated treatment should not be withheld merely because the classic early window has passed.

Mechanism

Mucus gel strength depends partly on disulfide bridges between cysteine residues in mucin. Acetylcysteine donates reducing equivalents through its thiol group, cleaving those bridges and lowering viscosity. In acetaminophen overdose, normal glucuronidation and sulfation saturate, CYP metabolism produces NAPQI, and glutathione depletion allows NAPQI to bind hepatocyte proteins. Acetylcysteine is deacetylated to cysteine, replenishing the rate-limiting substrate for glutathione; it also enhances sulfation and can reduce NAPQI directly. Later benefit includes antioxidant and microcirculatory support. This chemistry explains both the sulfur odor and the two very different bedside uses.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: inhaled acetylcysteine can trigger bronchospasm and can suddenly liquefy a large secretion burden, creating obstruction if cough or suction is inadequate. IV acetylcysteine can cause anaphylactoid hypersensitivity with flushing, urticaria, wheeze, hypotension, or angioedema and must be diluted in a weight-appropriate fluid volume. The inhalation/oral solution labeled NOT FOR INJECTION is not interchangeable with the sterile IV product.

Adverse Effects

  • Inhaled: bronchospasm, chest tightness, rhinorrhea, stomatitis, nausea, unpleasant sulfur odor, and increased liquid secretions

  • IV/oral antidote: nausea, vomiting, flushing, urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, and fluid or electrolyte problems

Contraindications

  • Product hypersensitivity; active bronchospasm requires readiness for bronchodilator treatment and reassessment.

  • Do not inject an inhalation/oral product or stop antidote therapy solely because the nomogram time is late when poisoning evidence persists.

Nursing Essentials

  • For mucolysis, assess cough strength, airway clearance, breath sounds, oxygenation, and suction readiness before and after treatment.

  • For overdose, obtain time and pattern of ingestion, acetaminophen concentration, AST/ALT, INR, creatinine, glucose, acid-base status, and toxicology guidance without delaying indicated loading.

Interactions

  • Activated charcoal can reduce oral absorption but may still be used according to overdose timing and protocol; coordinate route and timing.

  • Do not mix nebulized acetylcysteine with other drugs unless compatibility is known; nitroglycerin effects may be enhanced systemically.

Key Labs

  • Acetaminophen concentration plotted only for a valid single acute ingestion time, AST/ALT, INR, bilirubin, creatinine, glucose, pH/lactate

  • Respiratory secretion burden, work of breathing, oxygenation, bronchospasm, intake/output, and fluid volume during IV therapy

Nclex Traps

  • Liquefying mucus does not remove it; airway clearance must follow.

  • The Rumack-Matthew nomogram does not apply to repeated supratherapeutic ingestion or an unknown time, but acetylcysteine may still be required.
