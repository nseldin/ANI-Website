Definition

Motor impersistence is a bedside motor sign. It is a clue to chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction. A positive finding means the requested posture cannot be maintained despite adequate initial power.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Motor impersistence means the requested posture cannot be maintained despite adequate initial power.

Why It Matters

The observed Motor impersistence finding can signal chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction. Disordered motor-program persistence causes involuntary lapses rather than fatigable weakness. Motor impersistence cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction

Assessment Method

Ask the patient to sustain tongue protrusion, eye closure, or a steady posture.

Positive Finding

The requested posture cannot be maintained despite adequate initial power.

Mechanism

Disordered motor-program persistence causes involuntary lapses rather than fatigable weakness.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Motor impersistence finding raises concern for chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction because disordered motor-program persistence causes involuntary lapses rather than fatigable weakness. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Motor impersistence does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Motor impersistence to focus evaluation of chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: myasthenia gravis, poor attention, weakness. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask the patient to sustain tongue protrusion, eye closure, or a steady posture. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Motor impersistence as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Inattention, fatigue, aphasia, and comprehension problems mimic the finding. Therefore, Motor impersistence cannot confirm or exclude chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • myasthenia gravis

  • poor attention

  • weakness

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as myasthenia gravis, poor attention, weakness.

False Negative Situations

Motor impersistence may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction.

Modern Status

Current descriptive adjunct. When performed as described, Motor impersistence can adjust concern for chorea or frontal-subcortical dysfunction, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Motor impersistence: Inattention, fatigue, aphasia, and comprehension problems mimic the finding.

Safety Precautions

Observe Motor impersistence with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Ask the patient to sustain tongue protrusion, eye closure, or a steady posture. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Confirm the patient understood the task before attributing lapses to chorea.
