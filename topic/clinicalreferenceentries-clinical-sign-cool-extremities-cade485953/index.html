Definition

Cool extremities means one or more hands, feet, arms, or legs feel colder than expected for the environment or colder than the opposite side. It is an observed peripheral-perfusion clue, not a diagnosis.

Plain Language

The hands or feet feel unusually cool because less warm blood may be reaching the skin and tissues.

Why It Matters

Coolness in both sides can accompany low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, or vasopressors. A newly cold, painful, pale, numb, weak, or pulseless limb can indicate acute arterial blockage and is time critical.

Associated Conditions

  • reduced skin and limb blood flow from sympathetic vasoconstriction, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, vasopressor effect, acute arterial occlusion, chronic peripheral arterial disease, or environmental cold

Assessment Method

Compare both hands and feet and proximal with distal skin using the back of the hand or an objective temperature method when needed; document symmetry, color, moisture, capillary refill, pulse strength, sensation, movement, pain, blood pressure, mental status, urine output, ambient exposure, core temperature, medications, and response to treatment.

Positive Finding

One or more extremities feel cooler than expected for the environment or cooler than the opposite side, often with pallor, mottling, delayed refill, weak pulse, or clamminess when perfusion is impaired.

Mechanism

Systemic sympathetic vasoconstriction redistributes blood toward vital organs during low effective circulation, while local arterial narrowing or occlusion reduces warm blood flow downstream in one limb.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Cool extremities finding raises concern for reduced skin and limb blood flow from sympathetic vasoconstriction, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, vasopressor effect, acute arterial occlusion, chronic peripheral arterial disease, or environmental cold because systemic sympathetic vasoconstriction redistributes blood toward vital organs during low effective circulation, while local arterial narrowing or occlusion reduces warm blood flow downstream in one limb. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Cool extremities does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Cool extremities to focus evaluation of reduced skin and limb blood flow from sympathetic vasoconstriction, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, vasopressor effect, acute arterial occlusion, chronic peripheral arterial disease, or environmental cold and the reviewed alternatives: environmental cold, Raynaud phenomenon, hypothermia, acute arterial occlusion, low-output shock. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compare both hands and feet and proximal with distal skin using the back of the hand or an objective temperature method when needed; document symmetry, color, moisture, capillary refill, pulse strength, sensation, movement, pain, blood pressure, mental status, urine output, ambient exposure, core temperature, medications, and response to treatment. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Cool extremities as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Room temperature, blankets, examiner hand temperature, neuropathy, skin thickness, chronic vascular disease, and vasopressors affect perception; warm skin does not exclude distributive shock and cool skin alone does not prove poor organ perfusion. Therefore, Cool extremities cannot confirm or exclude reduced skin and limb blood flow from sympathetic vasoconstriction, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, vasopressor effect, acute arterial occlusion, chronic peripheral arterial disease, or environmental cold by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as environmental cold, Raynaud phenomenon, hypothermia, acute arterial occlusion, low-output shock.

False Negative Situations

Cool extremities may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of reduced skin and limb blood flow from sympathetic vasoconstriction, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, shock, hypothermia, vasopressor effect, acute arterial occlusion, chronic peripheral arterial disease, or environmental cold.

Modern Status

Current nonspecific but clinically useful perfusion sign; bilateral coolness suggests a systemic or environmental pattern, whereas new asymmetry raises concern for focal vascular compromise. Safety Precautions

Observe Cool extremities with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Compare both hands and feet and proximal with distal skin using the back of the hand or an objective temperature method when needed; document symmetry, color, moisture, capillary refill, pulse strength, sensation, movement, pain, blood pressure, mental status, urine output, ambient exposure, core temperature, medications, and response to treatment. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not dismiss a newly cold, painful, pale, numb, weak, or pulseless limb as room temperature because acute limb ischemia is time critical; bilateral cool extremities with hypotension, confusion, oliguria, delayed capillary refill, chest pain, dyspnea, or rapid deterioration requires urgent shock and perfusion assessment.
