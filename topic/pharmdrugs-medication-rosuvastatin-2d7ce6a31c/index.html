Used To Treat

Reduction of LDL-C and cardiovascular risk in current label-defined adults and pediatric patients with primary or familial hypercholesterolemia. Doses of 20-40 mg are high intensity; 5-10 mg are moderate intensity in typical adults.

Mechanism

Rosuvastatin competitively inhibits HMG-CoA reductase in hepatocytes, reducing cholesterol and activating SREBP2-mediated LDL-receptor transcription. Its polar structure favors hepatoselective uptake, but OATP1B1 is needed to move drug efficiently into liver and BCRP helps efflux it. Inhibition of either transporter increases plasma exposure and muscle risk. Only a minority is metabolized, mainly by CYP2C9 and to a lesser degree CYP2C19, so a drug can create a major rosuvastatin interaction without touching CYP3A4. A meaningful fraction is eliminated in feces and urine; severe renal impairment raises exposure enough to require a 5-mg start and 10-mg maximum under current U.S. labeling. Proteinuria or microscopic hematuria can occur more often at 40 mg and is usually tubular and transient, but persistent findings still require clinical interpretation rather than dismissal.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Rosuvastatin can cause dose- and exposure-related myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, IMNM, and rare serious liver injury. The 40-mg dose is reserved for patients who need it after inadequate response to 20 mg because adverse effects rise with exposure.

Contraindications

  • Active liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis and serious hypersensitivity are major class barriers; use in stable chronic liver disease is individualized rather than automatically prohibited.

  • Most pregnant patients discontinue because therapy is preventive and fetal cholesterol biology matters. Current labeling permits rare individualized high-risk decisions; breastfeeding is generally not recommended during treatment.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record baseline lipid panel, exact product and dose, expected intensity, liver and thyroid context, kidney function when relevant, pregnancy plans, muscle history, alcohol, and all interacting medicines or supplements.

  • Teach urgent review for severe weakness, dark urine, oliguria, jaundice, or systemic illness. Mild symptoms still deserve assessment, but one episode does not prove permanent intolerance to every statin.

  • Check severe renal impairment and ancestry-related starting guidance, and do not jump to 40 mg before documenting why 20 mg is insufficient.

  • Urinalysis findings at high dose should be interpreted with kidney function, persistence, and other disease rather than automatically labeling glomerular injury.

Interactions

  • Cyclosporine, gemfibrozil, selected antivirals, regorafenib, fostamatinib, febuxostat, and other transporter inhibitors can require avoidance or strict dose limits. Separate aluminum- and magnesium-containing antacids by giving rosuvastatin at least two hours before them.

  • Warfarin INR can rise after initiation or dose change; monitor until stable. Fenofibrate and other myotoxic drugs add pharmacodynamic muscle risk.

Key Labs

  • Repeat lipids after initiation or dose change to verify the expected percentage LDL reduction. Obtain baseline hepatic assessment; repeat liver tests for symptoms or clinical concern rather than routine asymptomatic monthly screening.

  • Use CK, creatinine, potassium, phosphate, urinalysis, and urine output for significant muscle symptoms or dark urine. Check TSH or other reversible contributors when the presentation suggests them.

Nclex Traps

  • Statins lower LDL mainly by increasing hepatic LDL-receptor clearance after intracellular cholesterol falls; they do not simply dissolve plaque or block intestinal fat.

  • Intensity is defined by expected percentage LDL reduction, not by comparing milligram numbers across drugs.

  • Rosuvastatin has limited CYP3A metabolism but important transporter interactions. 'Not CYP3A' does not mean no interactions.

  • Relatively hydrophilic does not eliminate muscle toxicity or rhabdomyolysis.

Class Pathway

  • Hepatic HMG-CoA reductase inhibition

  • OATP1B1 uptake and BCRP transport

  • High-intensity LDL reduction at 20-40 mg

Description

Rosuvastatin, branded Crestor, is a long-acting, relatively hydrophilic statin and the other statin besides atorvastatin that can provide high-intensity therapy. It inhibits hepatic HMG-CoA reductase, increases LDL receptors, and typically lowers LDL-C at least 50 percent at 20-40 mg. Limited CYP metabolism reduces classic CYP3A interactions, but rosuvastatin is not interaction free: OATP1B1 hepatic uptake and BCRP efflux strongly influence exposure, so cyclosporine, some antivirals, gemfibrozil, and transporter genetics matter. Kidney impairment increases exposure and requires a lower starting dose and maximum in severe impairment. Average plasma exposure is higher in many patients of Asian ancestry, so current labeling recommends a 5-mg start unless the individual context supports another plan. Long activity permits any consistent dosing time.

Adverse Effects

  • Muscle symptoms can range from aches with normal CK to myopathy and rare rhabdomyolysis with hyperkalemia and myoglobin-mediated acute kidney injury. Risk rises with greater exposure, interacting drugs, CKD, hypothyroidism, frailty, and acute illness.

  • Mild aminotransferase and glucose or A1c increases can occur. Serious liver failure is rare, and the small diabetes signal usually does not outweigh prevention benefit when the indication is strong.
