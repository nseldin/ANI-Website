Definition

Dysarthria is a motor-speech problem in which weak, slow, or poorly coordinated speech muscles make words hard to pronounce clearly. In isolated dysarthria, the person still knows and understands the intended language.

Plain Language

The speech may sound slurred, slow, strained, very soft, or unusually nasal because the muscles are not controlling speech normally.

Why It Matters

Sudden dysarthria can be a stroke warning sign. It can also occur with brainstem, cerebellar, cranial-nerve, neuromuscular, toxic, or medication problems, and the same weakness may impair swallowing and increase aspiration risk.

Associated Conditions

  • impaired strength, speed, coordination, tone, or control of speech muscles from stroke or other cerebral, brainstem, cerebellar, basal-ganglia, cranial-nerve, neuromuscular, toxic, or medication-related dysfunction

Assessment Method

Listen to spontaneous speech and repetition for articulation, rate, rhythm, loudness, resonance, and breath support; examine face, palate, tongue, voice, cranial nerves, limb findings, language comprehension and naming, and swallowing safety.

Positive Finding

Speech is slurred, slow, strained, irregular, unusually soft, or abnormally nasal because words are not produced clearly, while language formulation and comprehension can remain intact in isolated dysarthria.

Mechanism

Neurologic or muscular impairment disrupts coordinated respiration, phonation, resonance, articulation, or prosody even though the person knows the words they intend to communicate.

Evidence Limitations

Accent, language, dentition, dry mouth, hearing, fatigue, sedation, facial pain, and baseline speech disorders affect intelligibility; bedside observation does not identify the lesion by itself. Therefore, Dysarthria cannot confirm or exclude impaired strength, speed, coordination, tone, or control of speech muscles from stroke or other cerebral, brainstem, cerebellar, basal-ganglia, cranial-nerve, neuromuscular, toxic, or medication-related dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as aphasia, apraxia of speech, dysphonia, intoxication.

False Negative Situations

Dysarthria may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of impaired strength, speed, coordination, tone, or control of speech muscles from stroke or other cerebral, brainstem, cerebellar, basal-ganglia, cranial-nerve, neuromuscular, toxic, or medication-related dysfunction.

Current motor-speech finding; it must be separated from aphasia, apraxia of speech, dysphonia, and ordinary language difference.
