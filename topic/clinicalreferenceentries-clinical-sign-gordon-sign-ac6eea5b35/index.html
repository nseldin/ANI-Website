Definition

Gordon sign is a bedside reflex sign. It is a clue to corticospinal tract dysfunction. A positive finding means the great toe extends and other toes may fan.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Gordon sign means the great toe extends and other toes may fan.

Why It Matters

A positive Gordon sign response can signal corticospinal tract dysfunction. Calf compression supplies an alternative afferent stimulus that can reveal a disinhibited extensor plantar response. Gordon sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • corticospinal tract dysfunction

Assessment Method

Compress the calf muscles firmly but gently.

Positive Finding

The great toe extends and other toes may fan.

Mechanism

Calf compression supplies an alternative afferent stimulus that can reveal a disinhibited extensor plantar response.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Gordon sign response raises concern for corticospinal tract dysfunction because calf compression supplies an alternative afferent stimulus that can reveal a disinhibited extensor plantar response. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Gordon sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Gordon sign to focus evaluation of corticospinal tract dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: calf tenderness, muscle cramp, voluntary toe movement. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compress the calf muscles firmly but gently. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Gordon sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Sensitivity and specificity are not reliably established; calf pain or vascular concern can make testing inappropriate. Therefore, Gordon sign cannot confirm or exclude corticospinal tract dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • calf tenderness

  • muscle cramp

  • voluntary toe movement

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from calf tenderness, muscle cramp, voluntary toe movement, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Gordon sign may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Historical adjunct with limited modern use. Gordon sign remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude corticospinal tract dysfunction. Apply the reviewed limitation for Gordon sign: Sensitivity and specificity are not reliably established; calf pain or vascular concern can make testing inappropriate.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Gordon sign, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Compress the calf muscles firmly but gently. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not perform forceful calf compression when DVT, trauma, or compartment syndrome is suspected.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact Gordon sign observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: The great toe extends and other toes may fan. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: Compress the calf muscles firmly but gently. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, corticospinal tract dysfunction, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

Urgent Escalation

For Gordon sign, apply this exact authored caution when deciding whom to notify and how quickly: Do not perform forceful calf compression when DVT, trauma, or compartment syndrome is suspected. Base escalation for Gordon sign on the patient's current condition, associated findings, and the cause under evaluation; this card does not assign a universal urgency level.
