Definition

Bacillus cereus is a clinically important bacterium associated with emetic food poisoning, diarrheal food poisoning, post-traumatic endophthalmitis, and device-associated bacteremia. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Bacillus cereus is a Gram-positive bacillus with a facultative anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, spore-forming, motile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Heat-stable emetic toxin or enterotoxins cause distinct foodborne syndromes, while tissue-destructive factors can drive severe ocular or invasive infection. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Bacillus cereus is associated with emetic food poisoning, diarrheal food poisoning, post-traumatic endophthalmitis, and device-associated bacteremia. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
