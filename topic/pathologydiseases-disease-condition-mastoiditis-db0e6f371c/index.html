Definition

Mastoiditis develops when untreated or complicated middle-ear infection spreads into mastoid air cells and can erode bone or extend intracranially. The characteristic pattern is fever, severe ear pain, postauricular erythema and swelling, protruding pinna, otorrhea, and persistent toxicity after otitis treatment. Confirm or stage it with urgent otoscopic, cranial-nerve, neurologic, and postauricular examination; distinguish it from otitis externa, lymphadenitis, cellulitis, parotitis, and temporal-bone tumor by ear findings and imaging. The priority is to begin intravenous antibiotics that cover expected ear pathogens because meningitis can complicate Mastoiditis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • urgent otoscopic, cranial-nerve, neurologic, and postauricular examination

  • contrast CT of temporal bone for coalescence, abscess, and bony erosion

  • MRI and venous imaging when intracranial or sinus complications are suspected

  • Distinguish Mastoiditis from otitis externa, lymphadenitis, cellulitis, parotitis, and temporal-bone tumor by ear findings and imaging.

Labs

  • labs are often not primary unless infection/systemic disease is suspected

Treatments

  • begin intravenous antibiotics that cover expected ear pathogens

  • obtain urgent otolaryngology drainage and culture

  • perform mastoid surgery when abscess, coalescence, neurologic complication, or treatment failure occurs

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for increasing postauricular swelling, severe headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, altered consciousness, seizure, focal deficit, facial weakness, vertigo, respiratory or hemodynamic instability, or persistent fever despite therapy because these suggest abscess, meningitis, thrombosis, or sepsis.

  • Assess fever, ear pain and drainage, mastoid redness, tenderness, fluctuance, pinna displacement, hearing, and prior antibiotic response because infection can erode mastoid bone and form a subperiosteal abscess despite an ordinary ear-infection beginning.

  • Arrange urgent hospital and ear-nose-throat evaluation, obtain cultures and imaging as directed, and give prescribed intravenous antibiotics on time because delay permits spread to the venous sinuses, meninges, brain, facial nerve, and inner ear.

  • Trend temperature, pain, swelling measurements, neurologic status, pupils, facial movement, gait, hearing, hydration, and sepsis markers because intracranial or labyrinthine spread may first appear as headache, weakness, vertigo, or behavior change.

  • Provide analgesia, fluids, safe ear-drainage care without blind instrumentation, and prepare for myringotomy, abscess drainage, or mastoidectomy when response is inadequate because antibiotics may not penetrate a walled-off collection.

Complications

  • meningitis can complicate Mastoiditis.

  • brain abscess can complicate Mastoiditis.

  • sigmoid-sinus thrombosis can complicate Mastoiditis.

  • facial palsy can complicate Mastoiditis.

  • hearing loss can complicate Mastoiditis.

  • sepsis can complicate Mastoiditis.

Red Flags

  • Severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, repeated vomiting, confusion, or seizure

  • New facial weakness, double vision, focal deficit, ataxia, severe vertigo, or reduced hearing

  • Fluctuant postauricular swelling, rapidly displaced pinna, worsening pain, or persistent fever despite antibiotics

  • Hypotension, poor perfusion, hypoxemia, oliguria, or other sepsis findings

Patient Education

  • Redness, tenderness, or swelling behind the ear that pushes the ear outward needs urgent hospital assessment; do not treat it only with leftover ear drops or wait for it to drain.

  • Complete the antibiotic and ENT plan and return for fever, swelling, severe headache, vomiting, stiff neck, imbalance, facial weakness, or hearing change because complications can extend beyond the ear.

Nclex Traps

  • A displaced pinna with postauricular swelling is not routine uncomplicated otitis media

  • Improving ear pain does not exclude an expanding intracranial complication if neurologic signs appear
