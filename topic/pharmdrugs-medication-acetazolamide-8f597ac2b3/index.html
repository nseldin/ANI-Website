Used To Treat

Adjunctive treatment of open-angle glaucoma and selected secondary glaucoma, preoperative pressure reduction in acute angle-closure glaucoma, edema in selected heart-failure or medication-related states, selected epilepsy, and prevention or improvement of acute mountain sickness under current labeling.

Mechanism

Normally, secreted hydrogen combines with filtered bicarbonate in the proximal lumen. Brush-border carbonic anhydrase converts carbonic acid to carbon dioxide and water; carbon dioxide enters the cell, cytosolic carbonic anhydrase recreates bicarbonate, and NHE3 recycles hydrogen while bicarbonate returns to blood. Acetazolamide inhibits both sides of this cycle, so bicarbonate and linked sodium remain in the lumen. Downstream sodium delivery increases potassium secretion, while bicarbonate loss causes hyperchloremic acidosis. Alkaline urine reduces conversion of ammonia to trapped ammonium, impairing net acid and nitrogen excretion in cirrhosis. In ciliary epithelium and choroid plexus, reduced bicarbonate transport lowers fluid secretion. At altitude, the kidney-induced acidosis sustains carotid-body ventilatory drive, reducing periodic breathing and increasing oxygen tension; it prevents or reduces acute mountain sickness but does not make continued ascent safe when severe neurologic or pulmonary symptoms appear.

Contraindications

  • Marked kidney or hepatic disease, cirrhosis, sodium or potassium depletion, hyperchloremic acidosis, adrenal insufficiency, and sulfonamide hypersensitivity are current label contraindications.

  • Do not use prophylaxis to mask dangerous altitude illness. Ataxia, confusion, dyspnea at rest, severe weakness, or worsening oxygenation requires descent and emergency care.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before use, identify the exact target and check bicarbonate, chloride, sodium, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, liver disease, stone history, respiratory reserve, and sulfonamide reaction history.

  • Teach that tingling is common but severe weakness, confusion, rash, fever, reduced urine, flank pain, or breathing deterioration is not. At altitude, start under the prescribed schedule and never substitute medication for acclimatization or descent.

Interactions

  • High-dose aspirin with acetazolamide can cause severe acidosis, anorexia, tachypnea, lethargy, coma, and death. Topiramate or another carbonic-anhydrase inhibitor adds acidosis and stone risk.

  • Sodium bicarbonate increases stone risk; urine alkalinization alters excretion of weak acids and bases and can change amphetamine, quinidine, methenamine, and other drug effects.

Key Labs

  • Bicarbonate, chloride, sodium, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, CBC and liver tests when prolonged or symptomatic, urine pH, and the indication-specific endpoint such as intraocular pressure, altitude symptoms, or alkalosis correction.

  • A falling bicarbonate is expected, but the acceptable depth depends on symptoms, respiratory reserve, kidney function, and treatment target; expected pharmacology can still become dangerous.

Nclex Traps

  • Acetazolamide makes urine alkaline and blood acidotic. The compartments move in opposite directions.

  • It helps altitude illness by increasing ventilation through metabolic acidosis, not by directly increasing inspired oxygen.

Description

Acetazolamide, commonly known as Diamox, is a carbonic-anhydrase inhibitor whose defining action is bicarbonate loss, not powerful sodium removal. In the proximal tubule it interrupts bicarbonate reclamation, producing alkaline urine and a hyperchloremic, non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis in blood. That acidosis is therapeutic at altitude because it stimulates ventilation and offsets respiratory alkalosis, improving oxygenation and sleep-period breathing. In the eye it reduces aqueous-humor production and lowers intraocular pressure; in the choroid plexus it reduces cerebrospinal-fluid production. It can help selected chloride-depletion metabolic alkalosis or augment acute decongestion, but the effect wanes as body bicarbonate falls. It can worsen hepatic encephalopathy because alkaline urine traps less ammonium, and it can promote calcium-phosphate stones because urinary pH rises.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Acetazolamide is a sulfonamide derivative and can cause rare fatal severe cutaneous, hepatic, hematologic, or anaphylactic reactions. It is contraindicated in marked kidney or liver disease, cirrhosis, depressed sodium or potassium, hyperchloremic acidosis, adrenal failure, and relevant hypersensitivity. In cirrhosis, reduced urinary ammonium trapping can precipitate encephalopathy.

Adverse Effects

  • Paresthesia, fatigue, polyuria, altered taste, nausea, myopia, hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hyperchloremic non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, alkaline urine, and calcium-phosphate stones follow the mechanism.

  • Severe rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis, cytopenias, hepatic necrosis, crystalluria, kidney injury, and encephalopathy are rare but serious.
