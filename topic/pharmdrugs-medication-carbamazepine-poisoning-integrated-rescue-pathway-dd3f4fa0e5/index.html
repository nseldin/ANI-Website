Used To Treat

Acute or acute-on-chronic carbamazepine toxicity causing nystagmus, ataxia, antimuscarinic delirium, coma, respiratory depression, seizure, hypotension, QRS widening, dysrhythmia, ileus, or delayed recurrent toxicity.

Description

Carbamazepine is a voltage-gated sodium-channel blocker whose overdose blocks neuronal and cardiac conduction while antimuscarinic gut slowing delays absorption, causing recurrent seizures, coma, QRS widening, and dysrhythmia. Poor solubility, antimuscarinic slowing of gut motility, controlled-release formulations, tablet clumping, and enterohepatic recycling can delay the concentration peak for many hours. Early nystagmus, dizziness, ataxia, dysarthria, vomiting, and agitation may progress to delirium, coma, respiratory depression, seizure, hypotension, wide QRS, and ventricular dysrhythmia. Carbamazepine blocks fast sodium channels in the brain and heart; that shared mechanism explains both seizures and conduction failure. A normal first ECG or declining first level does not clear a large ingestion because ongoing absorption can reverse the trend. Rescue includes airway and seizure care, serial ECGs and concentrations, sodium bicarbonate for clinically significant sodium-channel blockade, selected multiple-dose activated charcoal to enhance elimination when the airway and bowel are safe, and hemodialysis for severe persistent toxicity under EXTRIP criteria.

Mechanism

Therapeutic use-dependent blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels limits repetitive neuronal firing. In overdose, blockade becomes nonselective: cortical and brainstem conduction fail, while myocardial phase-zero depolarization slows, widening QRS and reducing contractility. Antimuscarinic effects produce mydriasis, dry mucosa, urinary retention, delirium, and ileus, which further delays absorption. The active carbamazepine-10,11-epoxide metabolite can add toxicity. Hypertonic sodium bicarbonate increases extracellular sodium and serum pH, helping overcome cardiac sodium-channel blockade, but requires ECG, pH, sodium, and potassium monitoring. Repeated activated charcoal maintains a gut concentration gradient and interrupts enterohepatic recirculation, enhancing elimination rather than merely preventing initial absorption. Hemodialysis is effective in severe poisoning despite usual protein binding because free fraction and burden rise at toxic concentrations.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for coma, seizure, breathing difficulty, fainting, wide-complex rhythm, or shock. Stabilize airway and ventilation, check glucose, obtain continuous ECG and serial 12-lead tracings, and call Poison Help for any significant controlled-release or unknown ingestion.

  • Treat seizures with benzodiazepines and sodium-channel-related QRS widening or ventricular dysrhythmia with protocol-directed hypertonic sodium bicarbonate. Monitor pH, sodium, potassium, QRS, blood pressure, and ventilation so treatment endpoints remain physiologic.

  • Use single or multiple-dose activated charcoal only under toxicology guidance with a protected airway and functioning bowel. Contact nephrology early for refractory seizure or dysrhythmia, prolonged ventilated coma, rising or persistent concentration despite charcoal and support, or other EXTRIP severe criteria.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify immediate versus extended-release formulation, total tablets, time, chronic dose, co-ingestants, prior seizure disorder, and drugs that inhibit or induce CYP3A4. Search for pill fragments or bezoar clues without delaying stabilization.

  • Trend neurologic state, nystagmus, gait, pupils, bowel sounds, abdominal distention, bladder retention, ECG intervals, blood pressure, temperature, respiratory effort, urine output, and serial concentrations. Maintain fall, seizure, aspiration, and pressure-injury precautions.

  • Before each charcoal dose, reassess airway protection, emesis, bowel sounds, distention, obstruction risk, and charcoal passage because repeated charcoal can be aspirated or accumulate when consciousness and gut motility decline. Stop or escalate when aspiration or ileus risk exceeds the enhanced-elimination benefit.

Key Labs

  • Serial carbamazepine concentrations until a clear peak and sustained decline accompany clinical improvement; electrolytes, bicarbonate, glucose, creatinine, blood gas, lactate, liver studies, CBC, pregnancy testing, and serial ECG. Consider free level or epoxide metabolite only with specialist interpretation.

  • A tricyclic-antidepressant immunoassay can cross-react with carbamazepine, while routine urine screens do not quantify severity. Follow the measured conduction, neurologic, and concentration trajectory because a qualitative screen can misidentify the drug and cannot show whether toxicity is worsening.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe toxicity causes aspiration, coma, apnea, seizure, antimuscarinic ileus, hypotension, QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia, cardiac arrest, rhabdomyolysis, kidney injury, and death.

  • Bicarbonate can cause alkalemia, hypernatremia, hypokalemia, fluid load, and extravasation; repeated charcoal can cause aspiration, obstruction, and hypernatremia; dialysis can cause hypotension and access complications.

Contraindications

  • Do not give repeated charcoal through an unprotected airway, uncontrolled vomiting, ileus, obstruction, or perforation risk. Do not assume gastric decontamination is finished because a first level is low.

  • Do not use a single serum concentration as a discharge rule, and do not substitute a generic antiarrhythmic for sodium loading without toxicology input because some agents worsen sodium-channel blockade.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Refractory seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, prolonged ventilated coma, shock, progressive QRS widening, rising serial concentrations, ileus with retained burden, or persistent toxicity despite charcoal and support requires ICU, toxicology, and EXTRIP-guided hemodialysis planning.

Interactions

  • CYP3A4 inhibitors such as selected macrolides, azole antifungals, and grapefruit can increase exposure, while enzyme inducers alter chronic kinetics. Valproate can change epoxide metabolism and free fractions.

  • Other sodium-channel blockers, antimuscarinics, sedatives, and QT-active drugs compound conduction, delirium, ileus, or respiratory risk; mixed overdose can remove the safety of repeated charcoal.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Neurologic and cardiac toxicity can be severe after relatively few tablets; use measured weight and pediatric bicarbonate, airway, and charcoal protocols.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Stabilize maternal airway, seizure, rhythm, and perfusion immediately, then coordinate fetal monitoring and teratology follow-up.

  • Population

    Older adults or conduction disease

    Note

    Baseline gait impairment, polypharmacy, cardiac disease, and reduced reserve increase falls, aspiration, dysrhythmia, and delayed-recognition risk.

Evidence Limitations

  • Multiple-dose charcoal and extracorporeal recommendations rely heavily on pharmacokinetics, observational data, and case series. EXTRIP supports hemodialysis in defined severe poisoning, but clinical trajectory remains essential.

Nclex Traps

  • Controlled-release absorption and ileus can make carbamazepine levels rise after an initially reassuring result.

  • Multiple-dose charcoal enhances elimination but becomes dangerous with an unprotected airway or ileus.

  • Carbamazepine sodium-channel blockade can widen QRS and require hypertonic sodium bicarbonate.

Regulatory Status

No specific antidote exists. Hypertonic bicarbonate, repeated charcoal, and hemodialysis are protocol- or specialist-directed; EXTRIP supports extracorporeal treatment in severe poisoning.

Boxed Warning

CARBAMAZEPINE-TOXICITY WARNING: call 911 for coma, seizure, breathing difficulty, fainting, wide-complex rhythm, or shock. Levels can rise late because absorption is delayed; obtain serial ECGs and concentrations, protect the airway before charcoal, and escalate severe persistent toxicity for EXTRIP-guided hemodialysis.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
