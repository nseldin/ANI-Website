Summary

An Osborn wave, usually called a J wave as well, is a positive notch, hump, or slurred deflection around the junction between the end of the QRS complex and the beginning of the ST segment. It is strongly associated with hypothermia when the history and measured core temperature agree, but it is not specific enough to diagnose hypothermia by itself. J-point patterns also occur with early repolarization, Brugada-pattern physiology, hypercalcemia, ischemia or infarction, neurologic injury, and other settings. The finding should trigger rapid evaluation of temperature, rhythm stability, intervals, symptoms, exposures, electrolytes, ischemia, and the complete 12-lead morphology. The ECG supports a physiologic story; it never replaces a reliable core-temperature measurement or clinical context.

Why It Matters

An Osborn (J) wave pattern suggests hypothermia when a measured low core temperature and exposure history agree, but it is not specific; early repolarization, Brugada-pattern physiology, hypercalcemia, and ischemia can also produce J-point patterns. Because hypothermia can cause conduction slowing and ventricular dysrhythmias, assess stability, measure core temperature with an appropriate low-reading method, monitor the rhythm, handle a severely cold patient gently, and escalate urgent abnormalities while cause-directed evaluation continues.

Quick Answer

Identify the deflection at the QRS-ST junction, verify it is present in contiguous leads rather than artifact, and examine the entire tracing. In hypothermia it may accompany sinus bradycardia, atrial fibrillation, prolonged PR/QRS/QT intervals, and ventricular irritability as conduction and repolarization slow. Its likely cellular explanation is unequal accentuation of the early action-potential notch in ventricular epicardium compared with endocardium, creating a voltage gradient visible at the body surface. A prominent J wave in a cold, altered, or exposed patient should accelerate core-temperature measurement, gentle handling, rhythm monitoring, evaluation for trauma or another cause of collapse, and appropriate rewarming. A J wave in a normothermic patient requires a different differential rather than a diagnosis of occult hypothermia by pattern recognition alone.

Sections

  • Label

    Official and alternative names

    Text

    The preferred descriptive term is J wave because the deflection occurs at the J point, the junction of QRS termination and ST-segment onset. Osborn wave is the familiar eponym used especially for hypothermia-associated J waves. Other historical descriptions include camel-hump sign and late delta wave, but these names do not establish cause. 'Early repolarization' is related terminology, not an automatic synonym: early-repolarization patterns include J-point elevation, notching, or slurring with defined lead and ST-segment features and a broad risk spectrum. Likewise, a Brugada pattern may involve prominent right-precordial J-point/ST changes but requires its own morphologic and clinical assessment. Naming the waveform and identifying the suspected context separately prevents an ECG descriptor from becoming an unsupported diagnosis.

  • Label

    Assessment method and waveform location

    Text

    Use a calibrated 12-lead ECG when possible, confirm patient identity and lead placement, reduce motion or muscle artifact, and compare with prior tracings. Find the end of ventricular depolarization and the start of the ST segment. A J wave appears as a notch, slur, dome, or hump at that junction, often in inferior or lateral leads and frequently prominent in precordial leads during hypothermia. Examine polarity, amplitude, distribution, ST slope, QRS width, rate, rhythm, PR interval, QT/QTc, and serial evolution. Do not measure an isolated monitor spike as a J wave without verifying the 12-lead complex. Baseline wander, tremor from shivering, lead reversal, bundle-branch block, paced complexes, and QRS fragmentation can obscure or imitate the junction.

  • Label

    Positive finding and associated conditions

    Text

    The positive observation is a reproducible terminal QRS or J-point notch, slur, or hump that is not explained by artifact. Hypothermia is an important associated condition, particularly when core temperature is substantially reduced and other cold-related ECG changes are present. The differential also includes benign or higher-risk early-repolarization phenotypes, Brugada syndrome or phenocopies, hypercalcemia, acute ischemia or myocardial infarction, resuscitation-related states, and intracranial or systemic illness reported with J-point changes. The same shape does not mean these disorders share one prognosis. Distribution, ST-segment contour, symptoms, family history, temperature, electrolytes, troponin and ischemic presentation, medications, and serial response determine which association is most plausible. The finding should open a differential, not close one.

  • Label

    Mechanism: why hypothermia can produce a J wave

    Text

    Cooling changes ion-channel kinetics and makes electrical behavior less uniform across the ventricular wall. A leading model emphasizes the transient outward potassium current during phase 1 of the action potential. The early notch becomes more prominent in epicardial cells than in endocardial cells, creating a voltage gradient across the wall at the end of depolarization and beginning of repolarization. Surface electrodes register that gradient as the J deflection. Cold also slows pacemaker activity and conduction, which helps explain bradycardia and prolongation of PR, QRS, or QT intervals. The mechanism is more nuanced than 'cold adds an extra wave': it is altered regional depolarization-repolarization balance, and similar gradients can arise from inherited channel behavior or acquired metabolic and ischemic conditions.

  • Label

    Interpretation in suspected hypothermia

    Text

    Interpret the ECG alongside a reliable low-reading core thermometer and exposure history. Oral, axillary, or routine peripheral temperatures may substantially misrepresent deep temperature in a cold patient. Assess wet clothing, environmental exposure, immersion, intoxication, endocrine or metabolic contributors, sepsis, trauma, and medications. A J wave plus bradycardia and interval prolongation in a genuinely hypothermic patient supports the severity story, but absence of a visible J wave does not exclude hypothermia. Rewarming may reduce the deflection and normalize intervals, yet serial improvement is supportive rather than retrospective proof. Severe hypothermia can make pulse and respirations difficult to detect; current resuscitation guidance emphasizes full resuscitative efforts when no obvious lethal injury is present and rewarming appropriate to severity.

  • Label

    Diagnostic value, reliability, and limitations

    Text

    The Osborn wave is a useful clue and teaching pattern, not a stand-alone diagnostic test. It is neither present in every hypothermic patient nor confined to hypothermia. The relationship between wave size and temperature is variable enough that amplitude should not be converted into a bedside thermometer. A normal tracing cannot rule out a dangerous core temperature; a striking wave cannot prove that cold caused altered mental status. Computer interpretation may miss, overcall, or mislabel the feature. Bundle-branch block, ventricular pacing, ischemic ST change, and artifact complicate recognition. Reliability improves when trained visual interpretation, correct lead placement, prior and serial ECGs, measured temperature, labs, and clinical context agree. Current care therefore uses the sign as one piece of evidence rather than a binary diagnostic criterion.

  • Label

    False positives, false negatives, and mimics

    Text

    A false-positive hypothermia inference occurs when a genuine J-point pattern comes from early repolarization, Brugada-pattern physiology, hypercalcemia, ischemia, neurologic disease, or another cause, or when tremor and baseline artifact create a pseudo-notch. A false-negative inference occurs when hypothermia is present but no obvious wave appears, the deflection is hidden within a broad QRS, the tracing quality is poor, or the reviewer focuses only on one lead. J waves can also be mistaken for ST elevation, a second R wave, or a delta wave. Avoid reflexively labeling the tracing benign because the patient is young, or labeling it infarction without evaluating symptoms and reciprocal or evolving changes. The corrective strategy is full-tracing analysis, temperature and electrolyte measurement, clinical risk assessment, and serial comparison.

  • Label

    Safety and emergency significance

    Text

    The waveform itself is not treated; the cause and the patient's stability are. Hypothermic myocardium is electrically irritable, so move the patient gently, prevent further heat loss, monitor continuously, establish a rewarming plan suited to severity, and avoid unnecessary rough manipulation. Escalate immediately for ventricular dysrhythmia, cardiac arrest, severe bradycardia with poor perfusion, hypotension, altered consciousness, respiratory depression, or a very low core temperature. In normothermia, syncope, family history of sudden death, nocturnal agonal breathing, chest pain, dynamic ST change, marked hypercalcemia, or ventricular arrhythmia requires urgent evaluation for nonhypothermic J-wave contexts. Never allow debate over the waveform's name to delay airway, breathing, circulation, core-temperature measurement, or emergency consultation.

  • Label

    Nursing relevance and actions

    Text

    Obtain a high-quality 12-lead tracing without delaying resuscitation, note whether shivering or motion limited it, and begin continuous rhythm monitoring when clinically indicated. Measure core temperature using equipment capable of the suspected range and the route specified by protocol. Remove wet clothing, insulate, apply ordered rewarming, monitor glucose and electrolytes, and assess for trauma, intoxication, frost injury, infection, and exposure duration. Handle a severely cold patient gently and coordinate transport to a center capable of advanced rewarming when directed. Repeat ECGs and vital signs during rewarming because rhythm, intervals, and J-wave appearance may evolve. In a normothermic patient, gather symptoms, medications, family history, calcium and other ordered labs rather than teaching that the waveform automatically means cold exposure.

  • Label

    Documentation and communication

    Text

    Document rate, rhythm, lead distribution and description of the J-point deflection, PR/QRS/QT or QTc information as interpreted, ectopy or dysrhythmia, tracing quality, time, measured core temperature and route, exposure history, mental and perfusion status, interventions, serial changes, and who reviewed the ECG. Use wording such as 'terminal QRS/J-point notch compatible with a J wave; correlate clinically' unless a qualified interpreter has established the diagnosis. Record that the finding is not specific and name the active differential when relevant. During handoff, emphasize temperature trend and hemodynamic or rhythm risk rather than merely saying 'Osborn positive.' Preserve pre- and post-rewarming tracings because evolution can help later interpretation.

  • Label

    Escalation triggers

    Text

    Escalate urgently for a core temperature in the life-threatening range, cardiac arrest, ventricular fibrillation or tachycardia, severe conduction slowing, progressive QRS widening, marked hypotension, depressed respirations, worsening mental status, or inability to rewarm safely in the current setting. In a patient without hypothermia, escalate new J-point change with ischemic symptoms, syncope, resuscitated arrest, documented ventricular arrhythmia, strong family history of unexplained sudden death, a concerning right-precordial pattern, or severe metabolic abnormality. A stable asymptomatic pattern may permit nonemergent specialist comparison, but that decision depends on the whole presentation. The sign's limited specificity increases the need for contextual escalation; it does not justify ignoring it.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and clinical reasoning traps

    Text

    An Osborn wave is not pathognomonic for hypothermia, and an ECG is not a thermometer. Absence of the wave does not exclude cold injury. A larger hump does not supply an exact core temperature or prognosis. Every J-point elevation is not an acute myocardial infarction, but ischemia cannot be dismissed by calling it an Osborn wave. Early repolarization is not universally harmless, and a Brugada pattern is not diagnosed from the word 'J wave' alone. Shivering artifact can imitate several rhythms and distort the baseline. Treating the printed computer interpretation as definitive is unsafe. The durable reasoning sequence is: confirm the waveform, assess stability, measure temperature, read the whole ECG, identify competing causes, and follow serial change.
