Definition

Hemolytic transfusion reaction develops when recipient antibody destroys transfused red cells, releasing hemoglobin and inflammatory mediators that can trigger shock, coagulation, and kidney injury. The characteristic pattern is fever, chills, back or chest pain, dyspnea, hypotension, hemoglobinuria, bleeding, or unexpected anemia during or after transfusion. Confirm or stage it with stop the transfusion and perform clerical and bedside identity checks; distinguish it from febrile nonhemolytic reaction, bacterial contamination, TRALI, TACO, allergy, and underlying hemolysis by timing, blood-bank testing, culture, lungs, and volume status. The priority is to stop the implicated unit and keep intravenous access with compatible fluid because acute kidney injury can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Fatigue, dyspnea, pallor, bruising, petechiae, mucosal bleeding, fever, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, weight loss, neurologic change, or transfusion symptoms guide hematology/oncology priority.

  • Escalate neutropenic fever, active bleeding, severe anemia symptoms, tumor lysis signs, hypercalcemia crisis, or transfusion reaction.

Diagnostics

  • stop the transfusion and perform clerical and bedside identity checks

  • send blood bag and patient samples for direct antiglobulin testing, repeat type, hemolysis markers, and culture when indicated

  • trend urine, creatinine, potassium, coagulation, and hemodynamics

  • Distinguish Hemolytic transfusion reaction from febrile nonhemolytic reaction, bacterial contamination, TRALI, TACO, allergy, and underlying hemolysis by timing, blood-bank testing, culture, lungs, and volume status.

Labs

  • hemoglobin/hematocrit may fall with anemia or bleeding

  • platelets may fall with bleeding risk

  • ANC below 500 means severe neutropenia risk

  • calcium, potassium, phosphate, and uric acid matter in tumor lysis

Treatments

  • stop the implicated unit and keep intravenous access with compatible fluid

  • support circulation and urine flow while treating shock or disseminated coagulation

  • notify the blood bank immediately and prevent further incompatible exposure

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for hypotension, chest or back pain, hemoglobinuria, severe fever or rigors, oliguria, bleeding, or dyspnea because resuscitation and critical-care support may be required.

  • Stop the transfusion immediately and keep intravenous access with compatible saline tubing because continued incompatible blood exposure can intensify intravascular hemolysis.

  • Recheck patient and component identifiers and notify the transfusion service and treating clinician because clerical mismatch is a preventable cause requiring immediate investigation.

  • Send the required blood and urine specimens and return the component and tubing according to policy because laboratory confirmation directs future transfusion safety.

  • Monitor blood pressure, temperature, pain, urine color and output, potassium, hemoglobin, coagulation, creatinine, and lactate to detect shock, hyperkalemia, DIC, and kidney injury.

Complications

  • acute kidney injury can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction.

  • hyperkalemia can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction.

  • disseminated intravascular coagulation can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction.

  • shock can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction.

  • death can complicate Hemolytic transfusion reaction.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension with chest, flank, or back pain

  • Hemoglobinuria or rapidly decreasing urine output

  • Severe fever, rigors, or cardiovascular collapse

  • Hyperkalemia, bleeding, or disseminated coagulation findings

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to report pain, chills, heat, nausea, breathlessness, or a sudden sense of danger at once during transfusion.

  • Explain that the reaction must be documented with the transfusion service so later components can be selected and monitored safely.

Nclex Traps

  • Never restart a unit after a suspected acute hemolytic reaction

  • Patient and unit identification errors must be investigated even if symptoms seem mild
