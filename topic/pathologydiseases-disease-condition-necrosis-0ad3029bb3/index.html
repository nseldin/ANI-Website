Plain Meaning

Uncontrolled cell or tissue death caused by injury, usually with membrane rupture and inflammation.

Word Parts

  • Root

    necr- / necro-

    Meaning

    dead or death

  • Root

    -osis

    Meaning

    condition, process, or abnormal state

Word Origin

Necrosis uses the necro- root for death plus -osis for a pathologic state.

Pathology

Necrosis occurs when severe injury overwhelms cellular energy production and membrane integrity. Cells swell, membranes rupture, enzymes leak, and intracellular material triggers inflammation. Patterns include coagulative necrosis in many infarcts, liquefactive necrosis in abscesses and brain infarcts, caseous necrosis in tuberculosis-like granulomas, fat necrosis, and fibrinoid necrosis in vessel-wall immune injury.

Etiology

Common causes include ischemia, severe hypoxia, toxins, infection, burns, trauma, pancreatitis-related enzyme injury, radiation, immune-mediated vascular injury, and pressure injury.

Signs Symptoms

  • Local pain, swelling, discoloration, loss of function, drainage, odor, or black eschar depending on site

  • Systemic fever, leukocytosis, tachycardia, or sepsis cues if infection or large tissue injury is present

  • Organ-specific injury markers such as troponin, CK, AST/ALT, creatinine, lactate, or neurologic deficits

Diagnostics

  • Physical assessment of color, temperature, pulses, sensation, drainage, odor, and tissue viability

  • Imaging may show infarction, abscess, gas, devitalized tissue, or organ injury

  • Labs reflect the organ injured and whether infection or systemic inflammation is present

  • Culture supports infection decisions but necrosis itself is a tissue-death pattern

Treatments

  • Restore perfusion or oxygenation when ischemia/hypoxia is the driver

  • Debridement, drainage, wound care, revascularization, antibiotics, or surgery may be needed depending on site and infection

  • Support fluids, pain control, glycemic control, nutrition, pressure relief, and sepsis management when indicated

Nursing Priorities

  • Recognize necrosis as dead tissue that cannot simply be 'revived' by routine dressing changes

  • Assess perfusion, infection, pain out of proportion, spreading tissue changes, and systemic instability

  • Escalate rapidly for suspected necrotizing infection, acute limb ischemia, bowel ischemia, or infected pressure injury

Complications

  • Sepsis

  • Abscess

  • Gangrene

  • Amputation

  • Organ failure

  • Poor wound healing

  • Systemic inflammatory response

Patient Education

  • Report black tissue, rapidly spreading redness, foul drainage, fever, severe pain, numbness, or cold/pale extremity.

  • Protect pressure areas and follow wound care exactly.

  • Control diabetes, smoking exposure, and vascular risk factors when relevant.

Nclex Traps

  • Necrosis is not the same as apoptosis. Necrosis is messy, injury-driven, and inflammatory.

  • Pain out of proportion plus tissue changes can signal a limb- or life-threatening process.

  • Antibiotics alone may fail when dead tissue or abscess needs source control.
