Plain Language

Cannabis use disorder is a treatable illness in which cannabis use becomes hard to control and continues even though it is causing harm or getting in the way of life. Legal access, medical authorization, or occasional intoxication does not by itself mean that a person has the disorder.

Why It Matters

Cannabis use disorder can impair driving, learning, work, relationships, pregnancy care, and mental health, and high-potency products can contribute to panic, psychosis, accidental injury, or severe vomiting. Clear diagnosis helps separate the ongoing disorder from short-term intoxication, withdrawal, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, and another psychiatric or medical illness.

Definition

Cannabis use disorder is a problematic pattern of cannabis use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR diagnosis requires at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months, with severity based on the criterion count. Intoxication, withdrawal, a positive cannabinoid test, medical authorization, or use during one risky event does not by itself establish the disorder.

Pathology

There is no single scan, blood test, or urine threshold that diagnoses cannabis use disorder. Repeated exposure to THC-containing products can produce tolerance, withdrawal, craving, and impaired control; acute and chronic harms vary with potency, formulation, route, dose, developmental stage, pregnancy, psychiatric vulnerability, co-exposures, and contaminants.

Pathophysiology

  • THC activates cannabinoid receptors involved in reward, memory, attention, coordination, appetite, and stress responses. Repeated exposure can produce neuroadaptation, so stopping after heavy prolonged use may cause a recognizable withdrawal syndrome.

  • Intoxication can cause euphoria or anxiety, altered time perception, impaired attention and short-term memory, slowed coordination, increased appetite, dry mouth, red eyes, and tachycardia; panic, paranoia, or psychosis may occur, especially with high doses or vulnerable patients.

  • Withdrawal after heavy prolonged use commonly includes irritability or anger, anxiety, sleep disturbance or vivid dreams, decreased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, and sometimes abdominal discomfort, tremor, sweating, chills, or headache. It is uncomfortable but is not usually the seizure-delirium syndrome seen with alcohol or sedative withdrawal.

Etiology

Risk reflects product potency and availability, early and frequent exposure, neurobiologic vulnerability, coping with pain or sleep problems, psychiatric symptoms, trauma, peer and family context, social disadvantage, and beliefs about safety. No one factor proves causation, and patients may have both a legitimate therapeutic goal and a cannabis use disorder requiring separate assessment.

Risk Factors

  • Early adolescent onset, frequent or daily use, high-THC concentrates, escalating dose, and prior cannabis use disorder

  • Psychosis vulnerability, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety, trauma, ADHD, suicide risk, or use to self-treat sleep and pain without follow-up

  • Pregnancy or lactation, driving or safety-sensitive work, cardiovascular disease, and concurrent alcohol, sedative, stimulant, or other drug use

  • Easy access, unstable housing, limited behavioral treatment, and use of unregulated products with uncertain potency or contaminants

Signs Symptoms

  • Using more or longer than intended, unsuccessful efforts to reduce, craving, substantial time obtaining or recovering, role failure, risky use, and continued use despite medical, psychiatric, relationship, or work harm

  • Intoxication with altered perception, impaired memory and judgment, slowed coordination, anxiety or panic, dry mouth, red eyes, increased appetite, tachycardia, paranoia, or psychosis

  • Withdrawal with irritability, anxiety, sleep change, reduced appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, craving, and selected physical symptoms after stopping heavy prolonged use

  • Recurrent severe nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, repeated hot bathing, and symptom recurrence with continued use may suggest cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome

Diagnostics

  • Ask about flower, concentrates, vapes, edibles, synthetic or unregulated products, THC and cannabidiol content if known, amount, frequency, route, last use, delayed edible dosing, purpose, driving, co-use, pregnancy, prior withdrawal, vomiting, psychosis, and all use-disorder features in the same 12 months.

  • Confirm at least 2 qualifying DSM-5-TR features with impairment or distress; separately determine current intoxication, withdrawal, cannabis-induced anxiety or psychosis, and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome.

  • Establish the timing of mood or psychotic symptoms relative to cannabis exposure and sober periods. Persistent symptoms, a prior independent episode, delirium, or atypical neurologic findings require evaluation for another or co-occurring disorder.

  • Interpret urine cannabinoid testing by assay and detection window. A positive result may reflect prior exposure long after impairment has resolved, and a negative result may not exclude a synthetic or untested cannabinoid.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses cannabis use disorder or measures current impairment by itself.

  • For severe vomiting or dehydration, assess electrolytes, glucose, renal function, acid-base status, urinalysis, pregnancy, ECG risk, and other abdominal causes according to the presentation.

  • For severe intoxication, psychosis, chest pain, altered mental status, or suspected co-exposure, select glucose, ECG, toxicology, organ studies, imaging, and observation from the clinical differential rather than assuming cannabis is the only cause.

Assessment

First assess airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, trauma, chest pain, severe vomiting, dehydration, dangerous agitation, psychosis, suicidality, and co-ingestants. Then distinguish current intoxication, withdrawal, hyperemesis, and a persistent use disorder; document product, dose, timing, route, potency if known, function, pregnancy, developmental stage, and the patient’s goals.

Differential

Differentiate cannabis intoxication from alcohol, sedative, stimulant, hallucinogen, synthetic cannabinoid, anticholinergic, metabolic, infectious, neurologic, and psychiatric causes. Differentiate withdrawal from anxiety, depression, insomnia, nicotine withdrawal, and other substance withdrawal; distinguish cannabinoid hyperemesis from pregnancy-related vomiting, infection, obstruction, pancreatitis, metabolic disease, and other urgent abdominal causes.

Treatments

  • Offer cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational enhancement therapy, or their combination; build a specific plan for triggers, sleep, craving, pain, peers, product access, and return to use. Include family or recovery supports when the patient wants and safety permits.

  • No medication is established or FDA approved specifically for cannabis use disorder. Treat short-term withdrawal symptoms and co-occurring illness individually without presenting a symptom medicine as a cure for the disorder.

  • For uncomplicated withdrawal, provide a supportive environment, sleep and hydration guidance, monitoring, and follow-up. Escalate severe depression, suicidality, psychosis, inability to hydrate, or important medical comorbidity.

  • For suspected cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, provide condition-based emergency evaluation and fluid, electrolyte, antiemetic, and ECG-aware care; sustained cessation is central to preventing recurrence.

Contraindications

  • Do not recommend cannabis as treatment for cannabis withdrawal or cannabis use disorder; continued exposure can maintain the cycle and may perpetuate hyperemesis.

  • Do not drive, operate machinery, supervise high-risk activity, or combine cannabis with alcohol or sedatives while impaired. Delayed edible effects make rapid redosing unsafe.

  • Do not use a positive urine cannabinoid result as proof of present impairment, severity, parenting capacity, or the diagnosis.

  • Do not present cannabis as universally safe because it is legal, plant-derived, labeled cannabidiol, or medically authorized; product content, evidence, and patient risk still require review.

Safety Considerations

  • Use a calm, low-stimulation environment for panic, paranoia, or perceptual disturbance while repeatedly assessing psychosis, suicide risk, trauma, co-exposures, vital signs, and ability to remain safe.

  • Treat persistent vomiting, syncope, chest pain, severe confusion, seizure, focal deficit, or psychosis as a medical or psychiatric emergency rather than routine intoxication.

  • Store edibles and concentrates in locked, child-resistant packaging; accidental pediatric ingestion can cause prolonged serious sedation and requires poison or emergency guidance.

  • Use nonpunitive pregnancy and adolescent screening and explain confidentiality and reporting limits before testing whenever possible.

Nursing Priorities

  • Record exact product, route, timing, approximate dose, THC concentration if reliable, repeated edible dosing, co-use, vital signs, mental status, coordination, vomiting, hydration, pregnancy, and suicide risk.

  • Reduce stimulation, prevent falls and driving, monitor cardiovascular and neurologic status, and reassess because oral products may have delayed and prolonged effects.

  • With recurrent vomiting, trend intake and output, weight change, electrolytes, renal function, ECG-related risk, response to treatment, and whether symptoms improve during abstinence.

  • At discharge, use teach-back on withdrawal expectations, urgent symptoms, impaired-driving prevention, product storage, behavioral treatment, and a follow-up plan.

Red Flags

  • Severe or persistent psychosis, command hallucinations, violent behavior, suicidal intent, delirium, seizure, focal neurologic change, or inability to remain safe

  • Chest pain, syncope, severe tachycardia, severe headache, suspected contaminant or synthetic cannabinoid, or mixed intoxication

  • Repeated vomiting with oliguria, syncope, major electrolyte disturbance, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe abdominal findings, or pregnancy

  • Accidental child ingestion, unsafe driving, trauma, or rapidly worsening sedation

Complications

  • Motor-vehicle and occupational injury, impaired attention and learning, role failure, relationship strain, and legal or financial consequences

  • Panic, cannabis-induced psychosis, worsening of a vulnerable psychotic or mood illness, depression, and suicide risk

  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome with dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, acute kidney injury, burns from very hot bathing, and repeated emergency visits

  • Pregnancy and newborn concerns, accidental pediatric ingestion, and exposure to contaminants or unexpectedly potent products

Prognosis

Cannabis use disorder is treatable, and reduction or remission is possible with behavioral care and practical support. Withdrawal usually improves with time, but craving, sleep change, and return-to-use risk may last longer. Persistent psychosis, recurrent vomiting, or functional decline requires reassessment rather than reassurance based only on reported abstinence.

Prevention

  • Delay adolescent exposure, use nonjudgmental early screening, discuss potency and route, and correct the belief that legal or natural means risk-free.

  • Avoid impaired driving, rapid edible redosing, mixing with alcohol or sedatives, and unsecured storage around children or pets.

  • Treat pain, sleep, anxiety, trauma, and mood symptoms with evidence-based care and follow patients using cannabis for a medical goal.

Patient Education

  • Cannabis use disorder is diagnosed from loss of control and harm over time, not from one test or the fact that cannabis is legal or prescribed.

  • Withdrawal can cause irritability, anxiety, poor sleep, appetite change, and low mood; get urgent help for suicidal thoughts, psychosis, or inability to keep fluids down.

  • Edibles can take longer to affect you and can last longer than expected. Do not redose quickly or drive while impaired.

  • Repeated vomiting can be caused by cannabis even if cannabis once seemed to relieve nausea; stopping exposure is important when hyperemesis is suspected.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and lactation: advise stopping cannabis and offer supportive behavioral care; use respectful, nonpunitive screening and shared decision-making rather than assuming a product is safe for nausea or sleep.

  • Adolescents need confidential, developmentally appropriate assessment of cognition, school function, driving, peers, family safety, trauma, psychosis, and suicide risk, with family involvement when safe.

  • Patients with psychosis or bipolar vulnerability need careful exposure-symptom timelines and prompt reassessment of paranoia, mania, hallucinations, and function.

  • Older adults and patients with cardiovascular disease, falls, cognitive impairment, or polypharmacy need added attention to delayed effects, interactions, balance, and dosing errors.

Nclex Traps

  • A positive urine cannabinoid test does not prove current impairment or cannabis use disorder.

  • Cannabis withdrawal is real but is not usually the life-threatening seizure-delirium withdrawal of alcohol or sedatives.

  • No medication is FDA approved for cannabis use disorder; behavioral treatment is central.

  • Hot bathing may temporarily relieve cannabinoid hyperemesis symptoms but does not treat the cause and can cause burns or dehydration.
