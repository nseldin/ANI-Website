Definition

Anemia of CKD is reduced red-cell mass caused mainly by insufficient kidney erythropoietin signaling, iron-restricted erythropoiesis, inflammation, shortened red-cell survival, and blood loss. It is often normocytic, but that pattern does not prove the cause or exclude iron, vitamin, marrow, hemolytic, or bleeding disorders.

Etiology

Declining renal interstitial function reduces the erythropoietin response to hypoxia. CKD inflammation raises hepcidin and traps iron away from marrow, while dialysis circuits, phlebotomy, GI loss, impaired absorption, infection, hyperparathyroidism, and nutritional deficiency add to the deficit.

Pathology

Renal erythropoietin-producing interstitial cells lose normal hypoxia-responsive signaling as fibrosis advances. Bone marrow receives too little survival stimulus and may also lack accessible iron despite normal or high stored-iron markers.

Pathophysiology

Erythropoietin normally prevents apoptosis of erythroid precursors; insufficient signal lowers reticulocyte production. Reduced renal clearance and inflammation increase hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin and limits intestinal iron absorption and macrophage iron release. Uremic milieu shortens red-cell lifespan, so ongoing replacement demand rises. Anemia increases cardiac output and myocardial workload, contributing to fatigue, dyspnea, left-ventricular strain, transfusion exposure, and reduced function.

Risk Factors

  • Lower GFR and longer CKD duration

  • Iron deficiency, inflammation, infection, dialysis blood loss, frequent phlebotomy, or occult GI bleeding

  • Malnutrition, B12 or folate deficiency, hemolysis, marrow disease, or severe hyperparathyroidism

  • Recent hospitalization, surgery, anticoagulation, or access bleeding

Signs Symptoms

  • Fatigue, weakness, reduced exercise tolerance, exertional dyspnea, dizziness, pallor, or tachycardia

  • Angina, heart-failure worsening, syncope, or cognitive decline when severe

  • Pica, restless legs, glossitis, neuropathy, bleeding, or melena suggesting contributing causes

  • Symptoms may be masked by gradual adaptation

Diagnostics

  • Trend CBC, indices, reticulocytes, ferritin, transferrin saturation, and prior hemoglobin

  • Evaluate B12, folate, hemolysis, bleeding, inflammation, infection, thyroid, marrow, and medication causes as indicated

  • Assess dialysis adequacy, access/circuit blood loss, nutrition, and menstrual or GI loss

  • Individualize treatment decisions using symptoms, transplant candidacy, cardiovascular/thrombotic risk, and transfusion avoidance goals

Labs

  • Hemoglobin is low, commonly with normocytic indices

  • Ferritin can be elevated by inflammation and does not guarantee iron availability

  • Low transferrin saturation supports limited circulating iron delivery

  • Reticulocyte response may be inappropriately low for the degree of anemia

Treatments

  • Treat iron deficiency and ongoing blood loss

  • Use erythropoiesis-stimulating agents or hypoxia-inducible-factor prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors in appropriately selected patients under current guidance

  • Treat infection, inflammation, nutritional deficiency, dialysis inadequacy, or hyperparathyroidism contributing to hyporesponsiveness

  • Use transfusion when individualized urgency or symptoms outweigh sensitization, volume, and other risks

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess fatigue, dyspnea, chest symptoms, dizziness, bleeding, diet, dialysis losses, infection, and medication history because reduced erythropoietin commonly contributes but iron loss and other causes remain important.

  • Trend hemoglobin, reticulocytes, ferritin, transferrin saturation, blood count indices, kidney function, inflammation, B12, folate, and occult blood when indicated because correctable deficiencies and bleeding alter treatment.

  • Administer prescribed iron and erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy using dose, route, and blood-pressure safeguards because adequate substrate and stimulation improve red-cell production while excessive correction increases vascular risk.

  • Monitor blood pressure, access patency, thrombosis, hemoglobin trajectory, iron response, dyspnea, chest pain, and treatment adverse effects because rapid rise or overshoot can harm cardiovascular and dialysis access safety.

  • Escalate for ischemic chest pain, severe dyspnea, syncope, active bleeding, hypertensive crisis, acute neurologic deficit, or access thrombosis because profound anemia or treatment complications require urgent evaluation.

Complications

  • Reduced function and quality of life

  • Left-ventricular strain, heart-failure symptoms, ischemia, and hospitalization

  • Transfusion reactions, volume overload, iron exposure, and HLA sensitization affecting transplant

  • Hypertension or thrombosis from overly aggressive erythropoiesis-directed treatment

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose CKD anemia without evaluating reversible causes

  • Do not use ferritin alone to declare iron sufficiency

  • Avoid targeting a normal hemoglobin automatically because cardiovascular and thrombotic harm can rise

Red Flags

  • Chest pain, resting dyspnea, syncope, hypoxemia, or signs of cardiac ischemia

  • Active gastrointestinal or other bleeding with instability or rapid hemoglobin decline

  • Severe hypertension, sudden neurologic deficit, seizure, or acute heart failure

  • Painful absent access thrill, limb swelling, or suspected vascular thrombosis

Patient Education

  • Keep iron and anemia laboratory appointments because symptoms alone cannot show whether iron, erythropoietin treatment, bleeding evaluation, or another approach is needed.

  • Report chest pain, fainting, worsening breathlessness, bleeding, severe headache, or loss of dialysis-access thrill because anemia and its treatment affect circulation.

Nclex Traps

  • Erythropoietin does not replace iron

  • High ferritin can coexist with iron-restricted erythropoiesis

  • Normocytic does not mean no iron deficiency

  • The goal is safer function and transfusion avoidance, not automatic normalization
