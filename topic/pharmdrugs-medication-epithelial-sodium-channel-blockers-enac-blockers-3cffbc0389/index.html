Used To Treat

Usually combined with a kaliuretic diuretic to prevent or correct hypokalemia in selected patients. Amiloride is also used off label for lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and for ENaC-driven Liddle syndrome; exact indications and evidence differ from triamterene.

Description

Amiloride and triamterene are direct ENaC blockers, not aldosterone antagonists. They are secreted into the tubular lumen and inhibit sodium entry through epithelial sodium channels in principal cells. Because only a small fraction of filtered sodium reaches this site, they are weak stand-alone diuretics. Their value is preserving potassium when a loop or thiazide increases distal sodium delivery. Amiloride has an especially important mechanistic role in lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus because lithium enters principal cells through ENaC; closing the channel reduces intracellular lithium exposure. Triamterene is less predictably absorbed and can form urinary crystals or stones. Neither drug carries the anti-fibrotic and disease-outcome evidence of MRAs, even though all are casually grouped as potassium-sparing diuretics.

Mechanism

ENaC normally allows sodium to move from the tubular lumen into principal cells. Sodium is then pumped into blood by basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase, leaving the lumen electrically negative. That negative voltage favors potassium exit through ROMK and BK channels and supports hydrogen secretion by adjacent intercalated cells. Direct ENaC inhibition therefore reduces sodium reabsorption, potassium secretion, and hydrogen secretion immediately and independently of aldosterone concentration or receptor transcription. Hyperkalemia and a type-4-RTA-like normal-anion-gap acidosis can follow, especially when GFR is low or RAAS is also blocked. In lithium-associated nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, less ENaC-mediated lithium entry reduces inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase pathways and aquaporin-2 expression, helping restore collecting-duct water responsiveness over time.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Both drugs can cause fatal hyperkalemia. The danger is greatest in renal impairment, diabetes, older age, acute illness, or combination with potassium supplements, salt substitutes, RAAS blockers, trimethoprim, or another potassium-sparing drug.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyperkalemia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, dizziness, and normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis are class-pattern effects.

  • Triamterene can cause crystalluria, interstitial nephritis, kidney stones, megaloblastic anemia in folate deficiency, and rare hepatic or hematologic injury.

Contraindications

  • Hyperkalemia, anuria, acute or severe chronic renal impairment, and concurrent potassium-sparing therapy or routine potassium supplementation are central contraindication patterns.

  • Do not use a potassium-sparing label to justify therapy when urine flow is absent; without excretion capacity, blocking potassium secretion can rapidly become dangerous.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check potassium, bicarbonate, and kidney function before treatment, early after initiation or dose change, and during intercurrent illness. Investigate weakness, paresthesia, bradycardia, or rhythm change as possible hyperkalemia.

  • For lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, confirm the diagnosis and specialist plan; amiloride does not reverse lithium toxicity and should not delay lithium-level assessment or toxicity management.

Interactions

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, potassium products, trimethoprim, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, NSAIDs, and heparin raise hyperkalemia risk.

  • Lithium is a therapeutic target in one off-label amiloride use but remains a narrow-therapeutic-index drug; continue lithium concentration, kidney, thyroid, and fluid monitoring.

Key Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, sodium, pressure, volume, and ECG when hyperkalemia is clinically significant.

  • For nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, include fluid balance, urine volume, sodium, serum and urine osmolality, lithium concentration, and symptom trajectory.

Nclex Traps

  • Amiloride protects against lithium entry through ENaC; spironolactone does not directly close that channel.

  • ENaC blockers conserve potassium by reducing electrical secretion, not by supplying potassium. Hyperkalemia remains possible even without a supplement.
