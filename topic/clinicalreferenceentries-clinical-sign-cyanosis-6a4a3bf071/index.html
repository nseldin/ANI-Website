Definition

Cyanosis is an observed color sign. It is a clue to increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance. A positive finding means a persistent blue, blue-gray, or dusky discoloration is visible, especially on the tongue or oral mucosa for central cyanosis.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Cyanosis means a persistent blue, blue-gray, or dusky discoloration is visible, especially on the tongue or oral mucosa for central cyanosis.

Why It Matters

The observed Cyanosis finding can signal increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance. Deoxygenated or abnormal hemoglobin absorbs and reflects light differently; central arterial desaturation can discolor mucosa while slow peripheral flow can increase local oxygen extraction and discolor cool extremities. Cyanosis cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance

Assessment Method

Inspect the tongue and oral mucosa as well as lips, nail beds, and skin in neutral light; assess oxygen saturation, perfusion, respiratory effort, and whether the color is central or limited to cool extremities.

Positive Finding

A persistent blue, blue-gray, or dusky discoloration is visible, especially on the tongue or oral mucosa for central cyanosis.

Mechanism

Deoxygenated or abnormal hemoglobin absorbs and reflects light differently; central arterial desaturation can discolor mucosa while slow peripheral flow can increase local oxygen extraction and discolor cool extremities.

Clinical Interpretation

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Cyanosis to focus evaluation of increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance and the reviewed alternatives: peripheral vasoconstriction, bruising, medication or dye discoloration. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect the tongue and oral mucosa as well as lips, nail beds, and skin in neutral light; assess oxygen saturation, perfusion, respiratory effort, and whether the color is central or limited to cool extremities. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Cyanosis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Skin pigmentation, lighting, hemoglobin concentration, temperature, perfusion, and dyshemoglobins alter appearance; severe hypoxemia can occur without obvious cyanosis. Therefore, Cyanosis cannot confirm or exclude increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • peripheral vasoconstriction

  • bruising

  • medication or dye discoloration

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as peripheral vasoconstriction, bruising, medication or dye discoloration.

False Negative Situations

Cyanosis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased deoxygenated hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia, or markedly slowed peripheral blood flow that gives skin or mucous membranes a blue-gray appearance.

Modern Status

Current urgent contextual sign, but visual cyanosis is neither sensitive nor specific enough to confirm or exclude hypoxemia. Interpret Cyanosis in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Skin pigmentation, lighting, hemoglobin concentration, temperature, perfusion, and dyshemoglobins alter appearance; severe hypoxemia can occur without obvious cyanosis.

Safety Precautions

Observe Cyanosis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect the tongue and oral mucosa as well as lips, nail beds, and skin in neutral light; assess oxygen saturation, perfusion, respiratory effort, and whether the color is central or limited to cool extremities. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Treat new cyanosis with respiratory distress, altered consciousness, chest pain, shock, or low oxygen saturation as an emergency; do not delay oxygenation and perfusion assessment while deciding whether the color is central or peripheral.
