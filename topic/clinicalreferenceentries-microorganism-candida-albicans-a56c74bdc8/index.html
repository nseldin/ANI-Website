Definition

Candida albicans is a yeast that commonly lives as part of the human gastrointestinal, oral, vaginal, and skin microbiota. It becomes pathogenic when local ecology, immune defense, or anatomic barriers are disrupted, causing disease from superficial thrush or vulvovaginitis to candidemia and deep-organ infection. Colonization therefore does not equal candidiasis, and a positive result from a nonsterile site must be interpreted with symptoms and host risk.

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    C. albicans is one Candida species. The word Candida names a genus containing several clinically important species with different resistance patterns, so it is a search term rather than an exact species alias. Candidiasis names disease caused by Candida, while candidemia specifically means Candida in blood. Species identification matters because therapy and expected susceptibility differ.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    C. albicans is a eukaryotic fungus that can grow as budding yeast, pseudohyphae, or true hyphae depending on conditions. Switching morphology supports tissue invasion and biofilm formation. Its cell wall contains glucans and other polysaccharides absent from human cells, creating diagnostic and drug targets. It is nonmotile and can grow with or without oxygen, helping it occupy mucosal surfaces and device-associated environments.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    The major reservoir is the patient's own gastrointestinal tract, skin, and mucosa. Broad-spectrum antibiotics reduce bacterial competition, while diabetes, pregnancy, immune suppression, moisture, and barrier injury favor overgrowth. Invasive disease often begins when endogenous organisms cross damaged mucosa or enter through a catheter; less commonly, contaminated hands or devices transmit organisms in healthcare. Because colonization can be lifelong, one fixed incubation interval is not meaningful.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Adhesion permits attachment to epithelium and devices, morphologic switching promotes invasion, and biofilms protect cells from immune clearance and antifungals. Superficial inflammation produces itching, soreness, plaques, or discharge. When a central line, abdominal leak, neutropenia, or critical illness provides access to blood, organisms can disseminate to the eye, heart, kidney, bone, brain, or other sites.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Mucosal candidiasis includes oral thrush, esophagitis, and vulvovaginitis; cutaneous disease favors moist folds. Invasive candidiasis often causes persistent fever or chills despite antibacterial therapy, but its findings are nonspecific. Candidemia is an emergency because dissemination and organ injury can occur. Hemodynamic instability, neutropenic fever, visual symptoms, or persistent fever with an intravascular catheter needs urgent evaluation and source-control assessment.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Microscopy or culture from a symptomatic mucosal site can support superficial disease, but colonization must not be overcalled. Blood culture is central for candidemia, though sensitivity is imperfect; species identification and susceptibility guide treatment. Beta-D-glucan can support invasive-fungal evaluation but is not Candida specific and can be falsely positive. Repeat cultures and evaluation for metastatic foci may be required in invasive disease.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Standard Precautions, hand hygiene, aseptic catheter care, timely device removal, antibiotic stewardship, glucose management, and protection of skin and mucosal barriers reduce risk. Antifungal choice depends on site, severity, species, susceptibility, prior azole exposure, immune status, pregnancy, organ function, and source control. Treating colonization without disease can add toxicity and select resistance.

    Reviewed against NCBI Taxonomy and current CDC invasive and superficial candidiasis guidance. The fluconazole edge is restricted to susceptible, clinically appropriate syndromes and does not imply that all Candida or all invasive disease should start with fluconazole.
