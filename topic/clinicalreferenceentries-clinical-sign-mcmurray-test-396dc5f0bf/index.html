Definition

McMurray test is a bedside meniscal maneuver. It is a clue to meniscal tear. A positive finding means a palpable or audible click with concordant joint-line pain occurs.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive McMurray test means a palpable or audible click with concordant joint-line pain occurs.

Why It Matters

A positive McMurray test response can signal meniscal tear. A torn meniscal fragment is trapped and displaced between femur and tibia during rotation. McMurray test cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • meniscal tear

Assessment Method

Flex the knee, rotate the tibia, and extend while applying joint-line stress in a controlled manner.

Positive Finding

A palpable or audible click with concordant joint-line pain occurs.

Mechanism

A torn meniscal fragment is trapped and displaced between femur and tibia during rotation.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive McMurray test response raises concern for meniscal tear because a torn meniscal fragment is trapped and displaced between femur and tibia during rotation. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; McMurray test does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use McMurray test to focus evaluation of meniscal tear and the reviewed alternatives: patellofemoral crepitus, loose body, collateral sprain. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Flex the knee, rotate the tibia, and extend while applying joint-line stress in a controlled manner. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating McMurray test as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Clicks without pain are common and acute guarding limits range. Therefore, McMurray test cannot confirm or exclude meniscal tear by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • patellofemoral crepitus

  • loose body

  • collateral sprain

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from patellofemoral crepitus, loose body, collateral sprain, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

McMurray test may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current adjunct; no single maneuver rules meniscal tear in or out. When performed as described, McMurray test can adjust concern for meniscal tear, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for McMurray test: Clicks without pain are common and acute guarding limits range.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing McMurray test, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Flex the knee, rotate the tibia, and extend while applying joint-line stress in a controlled manner. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not force a locked, swollen, or acutely injured knee.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact McMurray test observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: A palpable or audible click with concordant joint-line pain occurs. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: Flex the knee, rotate the tibia, and extend while applying joint-line stress in a controlled manner. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, meniscal tear, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

Urgent Escalation

For McMurray test, apply this exact authored caution when deciding whom to notify and how quickly: Do not force a locked, swollen, or acutely injured knee. Base escalation for McMurray test on the patient's current condition, associated findings, and the cause under evaluation; this card does not assign a universal urgency level.
