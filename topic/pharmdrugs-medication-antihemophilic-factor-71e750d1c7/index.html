Used To Treat

On-demand control and prevention of bleeding, perioperative hemostasis, and routine prophylaxis in hemophilia A. Product age ranges, half-lives, dosing intervals, and prior-treatment requirements differ. Factor VIII alone is not the treatment for hemophilia B and standard recombinant factor VIII products are not interchangeable with VWF replacement for von Willebrand disease.

Description

Antihemophilic factor is concentrated factor VIII given intravenously to replace the deficient coagulation cofactor in hemophilia A. Factor VIII does not cut a substrate by itself; when thrombin activates it to VIIIa, it becomes the organizing cofactor that lets factor IXa activate factor X efficiently on an activated platelet surface. Restoring this complex supports thrombin generation and a stable fibrin clot. The most important failure signal is bleeding that continues or factor activity that does not rise as expected, because a neutralizing factor VIII inhibitor may be consuming the infused protein.

Mechanism

Circulating factor VIII normally travels bound to von Willebrand factor, which protects it from premature clearance. At an injured vessel, thrombin releases and activates factor VIII. Factor VIIIa binds factor IXa, factor X, calcium, and negatively charged platelet phospholipid to form the intrinsic tenase complex. Tenase accelerates factor X conversion to Xa, which then joins factor Va to generate the thrombin burst needed to cross-link fibrin. Infused recombinant or plasma-derived factor VIII temporarily restores this missing cofactor. It does not correct a factor IX deficiency, and it cannot work normally when a high-titer inhibitor binds factor VIII. Dose in IU is therefore individualized to body weight, desired activity rise, bleed site, product recovery, half-life, and measured response.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Serious hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis can occur. Neutralizing factor VIII inhibitors can develop and make an expected dose ineffective; suspect an inhibitor when bleeding persists or factor recovery is lower than predicted. Treat head, neck, airway, abdominal, iliopsoas, or major trauma bleeding as an emergency and follow a hemophilia treatment-center plan.

Adverse Effects

  • Factor VIII inhibitor formation with loss of response

  • Hypersensitivity, urticaria, wheeze, hypotension, or anaphylaxis

  • Thrombotic events are uncommon in congenital deficiency but can occur with excessive replacement, central access, surgery, or additional procoagulant risk

Contraindications

  • Serious hypersensitivity to the product or product-specific excipients

  • Not a substitute for factor IX in hemophilia B

  • Persistent bleeding despite dosing requires inhibitor testing and diagnostic reassessment, not automatic unbounded redosing

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify product, vial potency in IU, patient weight, target factor rise, prescribed dose, reconstitution, route, infusion rate, and lot documentation. Do not calculate from vial nominal strength alone when the actual labeled potency differs.

  • Assess the bleed clinically before and after treatment. Joint pain, warmth, limited motion, neurologic change, flank/groin pain, dysphagia, falling hemoglobin, or hemodynamic instability can reveal concealed hemorrhage.

Interactions

  • Emicizumab changes assay interpretation and breakthrough-bleed planning but does not chemically inactivate factor VIII.

  • Antiplatelet drugs, NSAIDs, anticoagulants, and invasive procedures add bleeding risk even when factor activity improves.

Key Labs

  • Factor VIII activity with a product-appropriate assay, inhibitor titer when response is poor, CBC/hemoglobin, and aPTT interpreted in treatment context

  • Clinical hemostasis, joint or muscle circumference/function, neurovascular status, and perioperative peak/trough targets

Nclex Traps

  • Factor VIII is the deficient protein in hemophilia A; factor IX is deficient in hemophilia B.

  • Factor VIII is a cofactor in intrinsic tenase, not the enzyme factor IXa and not the adhesive protein von Willebrand factor.
