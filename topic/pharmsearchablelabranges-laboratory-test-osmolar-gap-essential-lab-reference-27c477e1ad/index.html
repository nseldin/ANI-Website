Range

The osmolar (osmolal) gap is derived, not directly measured: the reporting laboratory subtracts calculated osmolarity from measured serum osmolality. One common U.S. calculation is 2 x sodium (mmol/L) + glucose (mg/dL) / 18 + BUN (mg/dL) / 2.8, with the laboratory's ethanol term added when applicable; SI-unit formulas, ethanol divisors, analyzers, and reference intervals differ. Results are commonly displayed in mOsm/kg even though the calculation is an estimate, so use same-time samples and the laboratory's exact formula, units, ethanol handling, and printed interval rather than a universal cutoff.

Why

An increased osmolar-gap result signals dissolved particles that the selected calculation did not account for and raises concern for an unmeasured alcohol or other solute when the exposure history and acid-base pattern fit. Important examples include methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol, ethanol, propylene glycol, mannitol, and ketoacidosis. Promptly assess the possible substance, dose and time, coingestants, mental status, vision, breathing, perfusion, hydration, and urine output; cause-directed evaluation can include repeat measured osmolality with same-time sodium, glucose, BUN and ethanol, plus electrolytes and anion gap, bicarbonate, blood gas, lactate, ketones, kidney function, and specific toxic-alcohol concentrations as ordered. Contact Poison Control or toxicology immediately for a credible methanol or ethylene-glycol exposure, and escalate emergently for new vision deficit, severe or persistent acidosis, coma, seizure, shock, or acute kidney injury. A normal or small gap does not exclude late toxic-alcohol poisoning because the parent alcohol may already have been metabolized; one gap cannot identify the solute, diagnose poisoning, determine severity, or by itself authorize an antidote or dialysis. EXTRIP osmol-gap treatment thresholds apply only when there is evidence of the named exposure and depend on the antidote and clinical context.

High Causes

  • Methanol exposure

  • Ethylene glycol exposure

  • Isopropanol or ethanol exposure

  • Propylene glycol exposure

  • Mannitol or another unmeasured osmole

  • Ketoacidosis or another clinical process that adds unmeasured particles

Low Causes

  • No substantial unmeasured osmole with the selected formula

  • Late methanol or ethylene-glycol presentation after much of the parent alcohol has been metabolized

  • Formula, unit, timing, or ethanol-adjustment differences that make a result falsely reassuring

Critical Concerns

  • Suspected methanol or ethylene-glycol exposure requires immediate Poison Control or toxicology escalation without waiting for a confirmatory concentration.

  • New vision deficit, severe or persistent acidosis, coma, seizure, shock, or acute kidney injury is an emergency.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess the exact substance, dose and time, coingestants, mental status, vision, breathing, perfusion, hydration, and urine output.

  • Verify that measured osmolality and calculation inputs were collected at the same time and confirm the laboratory's formula, units, reference interval, and ethanol adjustment.

  • Anticipate serial acid-base and electrolyte testing and specific toxin concentrations as ordered; treatment and extracorporeal-removal decisions belong to the clinician-led toxicology pathway.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal osmolar gap does not exclude late methanol or ethylene-glycol poisoning.

  • An elevated gap is a clue to an unmeasured osmole, not the identity or severity of a toxin.

  • Do not apply an EXTRIP treatment threshold outside its named exposure, antidote, and clinical context.
