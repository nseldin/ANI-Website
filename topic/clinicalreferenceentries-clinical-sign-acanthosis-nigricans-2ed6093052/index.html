Definition

Acanthosis nigricans is an observed skin finding in which body folds develop velvety, thickened, darker patches or plaques. It is a clue to an underlying cause, not itself a diagnosis of diabetes or cancer.

Plain Language

The skin becomes thicker, darker, and velvet-like, commonly on the back of the neck or in the armpits. It is not dirt and cannot be scrubbed away.

Why It Matters

Acanthosis nigricans commonly accompanies insulin resistance and should prompt respectful metabolic and medication assessment. Sudden extensive change, intense itching, mouth or palm involvement, or unexplained weight loss needs prompt evaluation because rare malignancy-associated cases can present that way.

Assessment Method

Inspect and gently palpate the posterior and lateral neck, axillae, groin, knuckles, elbows, and other folds in neutral light; compare texture, color, symmetry, distribution, onset, progression, itch, mucosal or palmar involvement, weight trend, medications, family history, and metabolic symptoms.

Positive Finding

Poorly defined, velvety, thickened hyperpigmented patches or plaques are present, usually symmetrically in flexural skin such as the posterior neck or axillae.

Mechanism

In hyperinsulinemia, insulin and insulin-like growth-factor signaling can stimulate epidermal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts; medication, genetic, endocrine, and tumor-derived growth-factor pathways can produce a similar morphology.
