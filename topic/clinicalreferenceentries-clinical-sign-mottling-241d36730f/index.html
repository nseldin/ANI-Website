Definition

Mottling is an irregular patchy or marbled skin-color pattern caused by uneven blood flow through small skin vessels. It is an observed perfusion sign, not a diagnosis.

Plain Language

Mottling makes the skin look patchy or marbled because blood is not flowing evenly through the small vessels.

Why It Matters

Temporary mottling can occur with cold, but new or worsening mottling during acute illness can signal poor tissue perfusion or unstable shock. It must be assessed with temperature, capillary refill, pulses, vital signs, mental status, urine output, oxygenation, and the patient's trend; its absence does not rule out shock.

Associated Conditions

  • heterogeneous skin blood flow from peripheral vasoconstriction or microcirculatory dysfunction during shock, sepsis, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, hypothermia, or cold exposure, with some transient physiologic and chronic vascular mimics

Assessment Method

Expose only the necessary skin and inspect the knees, thighs, limbs, trunk, and mucosa in neutral light while comparing with baseline and prior examinations; document location and extent, palpate temperature, assess capillary refill and pulses, and trend blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygenation, mental status, urine output, and other ordered perfusion markers.

Positive Finding

Irregular patchy, marbled, netlike, pale, dusky, or violaceous discoloration appears compared with baseline, often around the knees or limbs; when caused by hypoperfusion the skin may also be cool with delayed capillary refill.

Mechanism

Uneven constriction and flow through small skin vessels create alternating better-perfused and underperfused areas; in shock, compensatory redistribution and microcirculatory dysfunction can reduce and fragment peripheral skin blood flow.

Clinical Interpretation

Evidence Limitations

Visibility and agreement vary with skin tone, lighting, temperature, chronic vascular patterns, and examiner; knee-based mottling scores were studied mainly in selected sepsis and intensive-care populations and are not equally validated across skin colors or every cause of shock, while absent mottling does not exclude hypoperfusion. False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as Livedo reticularis, Cyanosis, Pallor, cold-induced color change.

False Negative Situations

Mottling may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of heterogeneous skin blood flow from peripheral vasoconstriction or microcirculatory dysfunction during shock, sepsis, low cardiac output, hypovolemia, hypothermia, or cold exposure, with some transient physiologic and chronic vascular mimics.
