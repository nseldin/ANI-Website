Used To Treat

Cryptococcal meningitis and disseminated cryptococcosis, especially in advanced HIV or other major immunosuppression, with regimen and duration adapted to host and guideline.

Description

Cryptococcal meningitis is infection of the meninges and cerebrospinal fluid by encapsulated Cryptococcus, often presenting subacutely with headache, fever, nausea, cranial-nerve findings, confusion, or visual change. The capsule and high organism burden obstruct CSF resorption, so raised intracranial pressure is a direct threat independent of fungal killing. Treatment is phased. Induction uses amphotericin plus flucytosine to reduce viable organisms rapidly; combination works faster and limits flucytosine resistance. Consolidation uses high-dose fluconazole after clinical response and sterile or improving CSF criteria to eradicate residual infection. Maintenance uses lower-dose fluconazole until adequate treatment and immune recovery reduce relapse risk. Echinocandins are inactive and should not be substituted.

Mechanism

Amphotericin disrupts the cryptococcal membrane, while intracellularly converted flucytosine disrupts RNA and DNA synthesis; attacking independent targets increases early fungicidal activity. Flucytosine monotherapy rapidly selects mutations in cytosine transport or metabolism, explaining why it is paired. Fluconazole penetrates CSF well and suppresses ergosterol synthesis, making it practical for longer consolidation and maintenance. Opening pressure must be measured during lumbar puncture because symptoms and imaging do not reliably quantify it. Repeated therapeutic lumbar puncture mechanically removes CSF when pressure is high; acetazolamide, mannitol, or routine corticosteroids do not correct the obstructed-resorption mechanism and can be harmful outside specific indications.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Raised intracranial pressure can cause blindness, herniation, neurologic injury, and death even while antifungal therapy is microbiologically active. Severe headache, vomiting, visual change, cranial-nerve deficit, or declining consciousness requires urgent pressure assessment.

Adverse Effects

  • Amphotericin causes renal/electrolyte and infusion toxicity; flucytosine accumulation causes marrow and hepatic toxicity; fluconazole causes hepatic, QT, and interaction risk.

  • Starting antiretroviral therapy too early in CNS cryptococcosis can trigger dangerous intracranial immune reconstitution inflammation; timing follows HIV specialist guidance.

Contraindications

  • Do not use flucytosine alone because resistance can emerge rapidly.

  • Do not call a persistent headache treatment failure until intracranial pressure, adherence, drug exposure, culture status, and immune inflammatory syndromes are evaluated.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record opening and closing pressure, CSF culture status, neurologic/visual findings, exact phase and day of therapy, renal function, potassium, magnesium, CBC, liver tests, and all doses received.

  • Ensure flucytosine renal adjustment and level monitoring when available. Prepare for repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures when pressure remains elevated.

Interactions

  • Nephrotoxins magnify amphotericin injury; renal decline then raises flucytosine exposure, connecting two toxicities in the same induction regimen.

  • Fluconazole interacts with warfarin, sulfonylureas, phenytoin, tacrolimus, and QT-active drugs; antiretroviral timing is a disease-management interaction rather than a simple CYP issue.

Key Labs

  • CSF opening pressure, cell count, glucose/protein, cryptococcal antigen and culture, CBC, creatinine/eGFR, potassium, magnesium, liver tests, flucytosine concentration when available, HIV viral load/CD4 in relevant patients.

  • Cryptococcal antigen titer is useful diagnostically but should not be used alone to decide relapse or stop therapy; culture and clinical context matter.

Nclex Traps

  • The three phases have different jobs: induction kills quickly, consolidation clears residual disease, and maintenance prevents relapse.

  • Managing intracranial pressure is not optional supportive care; it directly prevents neurologic injury.

Source Note

Current NIH adult/adolescent HIV opportunistic-infection guidance and current amphotericin, flucytosine, and fluconazole labels.
