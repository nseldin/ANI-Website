Used To Treat

Selected secondary prevention and acute treatment of arterial thrombosis, pain, fever, and inflammatory conditions. The indication determines the dose: low-dose platelet inhibition is pharmacologically different from full-dose analgesic or anti-inflammatory therapy.

Mechanism

Aspirin acetylates a serine residue in COX enzymes and prevents arachidonic-acid conversion to prostaglandin H2. Platelets cannot synthesize replacement COX-1, so thromboxane A2 production remains suppressed until new platelets are made. Endothelial and other nucleated cells can replace enzyme, helping low portal exposure preferentially affect platelets. Reduced gastric prostaglandins lower mucus, bicarbonate, and mucosal blood flow; reduced renal prostaglandins constrict the afferent circulation when perfusion is already dependent on them. In overdose, salicylate stimulates medullary ventilation and uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, producing tachypnea, heat, respiratory alkalosis, organic-acid accumulation, and metabolic acidosis.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Aspirin should not inherit the non-aspirin-NSAID cardiovascular-thrombosis boxed warning. Its major dangers are GI or intracranial bleeding, hypersensitivity and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, renal injury, dose-dependent salicylate toxicity, fetal renal/ductal effects later in pregnancy, and Reye syndrome in children or teenagers with viral illness. New tinnitus, rapid breathing, vomiting, sweating, fever, confusion, or acid-base disturbance requires urgent toxicity assessment.

Contraindications

  • Active major bleeding, serious aspirin/NSAID hypersensitivity, or aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease unless a specialist-directed plan exists.

  • Avoid for viral-illness fever in children or teenagers unless specifically prescribed because of Reye syndrome risk; later pregnancy exposure requires clinician direction because fetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramnios, and ductal effects can occur.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm the indication, formulation, and dose. In suspected acute coronary syndrome, emergency protocol timing matters; in chronic prevention, adherence and bleeding surveillance matter.

  • Assess GI bleed history, anticoagulants and other antiplatelets, renal function, asthma/polyps, pregnancy context, alcohol use, and upcoming procedures before routine administration.

Interactions

  • Anticoagulants, other antiplatelets, corticosteroids, SSRIs/SNRIs, alcohol, and other NSAIDs can increase bleeding or mucosal injury.

  • Ibuprofen may occupy platelet COX-1 before aspirin and interfere with irreversible low-dose antiplatelet action; exact timing should be reviewed with a pharmacist or prescriber.

Key Labs

  • CBC, hemoglobin/hematocrit, renal function, stool or source-specific bleeding evaluation, and indication-specific cardiovascular assessment.

  • For toxicity: serial salicylate levels, blood gas, electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, temperature, ECG, urine pH during alkalinization, and frequent neurologic checks.

Nclex Traps

  • Low-dose aspirin affects platelets for their lifespan even though plasma aspirin clears quickly; the persistence belongs to the irreversibly inhibited target.

  • Tinnitus is an early toxicity clue, and a near-normal pH can conceal simultaneous respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis.

Description

Aspirin is an irreversible cyclooxygenase inhibitor whose most distinctive use is antiplatelet therapy. By permanently disabling platelet COX-1, it lowers thromboxane A2 and reduces platelet aggregation for the remaining life of the exposed platelet. Larger doses inhibit prostaglandins throughout more tissues, adding analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory effects but also increasing gastric, renal, and bleeding toxicity. It is not merely an overdose antidote or rescue drug. Enteric coating delays dissolution and may reduce immediate stomach irritation, but it does not prevent systemic bleeding, renal effects, or serious GI ulceration.

Adverse Effects

  • Dyspepsia, nausea, bruising, prolonged bleeding, occult GI blood loss, tinnitus, and bronchospasm in susceptible patients.

  • GI ulceration or perforation, intracranial or other major hemorrhage, anaphylaxis, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, acute kidney injury, severe salicylate poisoning, pulmonary edema, cerebral edema, and death.
