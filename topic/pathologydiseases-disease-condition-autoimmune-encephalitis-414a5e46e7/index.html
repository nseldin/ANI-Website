Definition

Autoimmune encephalitis is a group of inflammatory brain disorders in which immune responses disrupt neuronal or glial function. It usually evolves over days to weeks with memory loss, altered behavior or psychiatric symptoms, seizures, movement disorder, sleep disruption, or autonomic instability. It is a neurologic emergency that can resemble infection, intoxication, primary psychiatric illness, or seizure-related encephalopathy.

Pathology

Antibodies against cell-surface or synaptic targets often alter receptor density or signaling without destroying neurons, which helps explain potential reversibility. Syndromes directed against intracellular antigens are more often paraneoplastic and T-cell mediated, with greater irreversible neuronal injury. MRI or CSF can be normal despite substantial functional brain disturbance.

Pathophysiology

  • Target-specific immune effects produce characteristic network failure. NMDAR internalization disrupts excitatory signaling and can cause psychiatric symptoms, dyskinesia, catatonia, seizures, and hypoventilation. LGI1-related synaptic dysfunction can produce faciobrachial dystonic seizures, memory loss, and hyponatremia.

  • Other antibodies affect GABA, AMPA, CASPR2, or glial proteins. Tumors may express a neural antigen that primes the immune response, while postinfectious immune activation can occur without a tumor.

Etiology

Autoimmune encephalitis may arise from antibodies to neuronal surface, synaptic, intracellular, or glial antigens; from a paraneoplastic response to an antibody-associated tumor; or after infection, including herpes simplex encephalitis. A carefully defined seronegative syndrome is possible when clinical and inflammatory criteria are met after reasonable exclusion of alternatives.

Risk Factors

  • An antibody-associated tumor; young women with NMDAR encephalitis warrant teratoma evaluation, while selected intracellular antibodies in older adults strongly suggest malignancy

  • Prior encephalitis, particularly herpes simplex encephalitis, or an autoimmune background

Signs Symptoms

  • Rapidly progressive short-term memory loss, confusion, disorientation, or reduced consciousness

  • New psychosis, agitation, mood change, catatonia, sleep disruption, or bizarre behavior accompanied by neurologic evolution

  • New focal or generalized seizures, faciobrachial dystonic seizures, or status epilepticus

  • Orofacial or limb dyskinesia, rigidity, ataxia, tremor, language loss, or abnormal movements

  • Autonomic lability, cardiac-rhythm change, fever without infection, urinary dysfunction, and central hypoventilation

Diagnostics

  • Treat the syndrome as encephalitis first: obtain MRI, EEG, lumbar puncture, infectious studies, metabolic and toxic evaluation, and malignancy assessment while stabilizing airway and seizures.

  • Send paired serum and cerebrospinal-fluid neuronal antibody panels through an experienced laboratory. CSF is essential for NMDAR and several other antibodies; a serum-only low-specificity result must fit the phenotype before being called causal.

  • A patient may meet provisional criteria for possible autoimmune encephalitis when subacute memory, mental-status, or psychiatric change is accompanied by new focal findings, unexplained seizures, CSF inflammation, or supportive MRI and alternatives are reasonably excluded; this is not the same as a definitive antibody-specific diagnosis.

  • Screen for a tumor according to antibody, age, sex, and phenotype and repeat surveillance when the syndrome remains strongly tumor-associated despite an initially negative study.

  • Normal MRI, EEG, or routine CSF does not independently exclude autoimmune encephalitis, and antibody negativity does not exclude a carefully defined seronegative syndrome.

Labs

  • CSF may show lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, oligoclonal bands, or increased IgG index, but can be normal.

  • EEG often shows diffuse or focal slowing and may detect nonconvulsive seizures; extreme delta brush can support NMDAR encephalitis but is neither required nor unique.

  • MRI may show medial temporal or other inflammatory signal, yet many antibody-mediated cases have normal initial imaging.

  • Hyponatremia is common with LGI1 disease; creatine kinase, liver tests, toxicology, infectious PCR, thyroid and nutritional studies help evaluate mimics and complications.

Assessment

Assess the speed and sequence of cognitive, psychiatric, seizure, movement, sleep, language, autonomic, and respiratory symptoms and obtain collateral history because the patient may be unable to report progression. Complete neurologic, infectious, toxic, metabolic, and medication assessments in parallel. Trend airway protection, respiratory depth, seizure activity, temperature, heart rhythm, blood pressure, sodium, and level of consciousness while MRI, EEG, CSF, antibody, and tumor investigations proceed.

Differential

Differentiate autoimmune encephalitis from HSV and other infectious encephalitis, toxic-metabolic encephalopathy, medication effect or withdrawal, endocrine disease, status epilepticus and postictal states, primary psychotic or catatonic disorders, CNS lymphoma, leptomeningeal disease, vasculitis, demyelination, prion disease, neurodegeneration, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and serotonin syndrome.

Treatments

  • Begin empiric antiviral and other antimicrobial therapy when infectious encephalitis remains plausible; immunotherapy must not create an untreated HSV window.

  • First-line immune treatment generally uses high-dose corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasma exchange, or a combination chosen for severity, infection risk, hemodynamics, and access.

  • Remove or treat an associated tumor promptly because ongoing antigen exposure can sustain the immune response.

  • Escalate refractory disease to specialist-directed second-line therapy such as rituximab or cyclophosphamide, with infection screening and fertility or organ-risk counseling.

  • Provide intensive supportive care for status epilepticus, hypoventilation, dysautonomia, aspiration, immobility, nutrition, sleep, agitation, and rehabilitation. Recovery therapy includes cognitive, speech, occupational, physical, and psychiatric support.

Contraindications

  • Do not call a serum antibody result diagnostic without phenotype and, when relevant, CSF confirmation.

  • Do not delay empiric acyclovir solely because autoimmune disease is suspected when HSV has not been reasonably excluded.

  • Avoid reflexive high-dose antipsychotic escalation in a patient with evolving rigidity, dysautonomia, catatonia, or NMDAR encephalitis because sensitivity and malignant syndromes can complicate care.

  • Immunosuppression requires active-infection evaluation and should not be initiated from an isolated nonspecific antibody result.

Safety Considerations

  • Lumbar puncture timing must account for mass effect, severe coagulopathy, and cardiopulmonary instability, but necessary infectious evaluation should not be abandoned without an alternative plan.

  • Autonomic instability can change abruptly; continuous cardiac, blood-pressure, temperature, and respiratory monitoring is often necessary in severe disease.

  • Sedatives can obscure neurologic trend and worsen hypoventilation, while uncontrolled agitation can injure the patient; use a coordinated lowest-risk plan.

  • Seizures may be subtle or nonconvulsive, so unexplained decline warrants EEG rather than repeated sedation alone.

Nursing Priorities

  • Protect airway, screen swallowing, use aspiration precautions, and watch respiratory depth because central hypoventilation can occur without primary lung disease.

  • Use seizure precautions and continuous or repeated EEG when ordered; document eye, facial, limb, and autonomic episodes with timing rather than assuming all movements are behavioral.

  • Trend orientation, memory, language, behavior, sleep, temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygenation, urine output, sodium, and medication exposure.

  • Provide a low-stimulation, injury-resistant environment with one consistent communication approach and family collateral history.

  • Prevent pressure injury, venous thrombosis, contracture, malnutrition, and deconditioning during prolonged immobility, then support staged neurorehabilitation.

Red Flags

  • Status epilepticus, rapidly declining consciousness, inability to protect the airway, or central hypoventilation

  • Severe blood-pressure or heart-rate instability, hyperthermia, arrhythmia, or shock

  • New fever, focal deficit, meningismus, or MRI/CSF evidence requiring urgent infectious treatment

  • Rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, or rising creatine kinase after antipsychotic exposure

Complications

  • Refractory seizures, respiratory failure, aspiration, dysautonomia, arrhythmia, and prolonged intensive-care stay

  • Venous thrombosis, pressure injury, infection, malnutrition, and medication toxicity

  • Persistent memory, executive, mood, sleep, speech, and functional deficits

  • Relapse or recurrence of an associated tumor

Prognosis

Many patients improve substantially, especially when immunotherapy and tumor treatment begin early, but recovery often unfolds over months and cognition or fatigue can lag behind motor improvement. Intracellular-antigen paraneoplastic disease generally has less reversibility than cell-surface antibody disease. Relapse risk and surveillance depend on the antibody and tumor context.

Prevention

Most autoimmune encephalitis cannot be prevented. Early recognition of mixed psychiatric-neurologic symptoms, prompt treatment of infectious encephalitis, and antibody-specific tumor surveillance can reduce avoidable injury. After recovery, follow the relapse plan, immunotherapy monitoring, vaccination guidance, and cancer surveillance defined by the treating team.

Patient Education

  • Autoimmune encephalitis is brain inflammation that can first look psychiatric; symptoms are not deliberate behavior.

  • A negative antibody panel does not always end the evaluation, and a positive blood test alone does not prove the diagnosis.

  • Recovery may be uneven, with sleep, memory, mood, and stamina improving later than basic movement.

  • Seek urgent help for new seizures, confusion, abnormal movements, fainting, breathing changes, fever, or a return of prior symptoms.

Special Populations

  • Children may present with behavioral regression, seizures, language loss, or movement disorder and require pediatric antibody interpretation and developmental rehabilitation.

  • Pregnancy changes tumor imaging and immunotherapy choices but does not remove the need to stabilize severe encephalitis.

  • Older adults have higher malignancy and medication-toxicity risk and need careful separation from delirium and neurodegenerative disease.

Nclex Traps

  • New psychosis plus seizure, dyskinesia, catatonia, autonomic change, or memory loss is not assumed to be a primary psychiatric disorder.

  • Both serum and CSF testing matter; normal MRI does not rule out autoimmune encephalitis.

  • Exclude and empirically cover dangerous infection while immune evaluation proceeds.

  • The highest priorities are airway, seizure, autonomic, aspiration, and injury control before long-term immunotherapy teaching.
