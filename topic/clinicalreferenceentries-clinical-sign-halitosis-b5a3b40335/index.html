Definition

Halitosis means frequent or persistent unpleasant breath odor. It is most often related to the mouth, tongue coating, teeth, gums, or reduced saliva, but it can occasionally come from nasal, respiratory, medication, or systemic causes.

Plain Language

Halitosis means persistent bad breath.

Why It Matters

Persistent halitosis commonly signals a treatable oral or dental problem and can strongly affect comfort and social well-being. Odor alone cannot diagnose infection, diabetic ketoacidosis, kidney failure, or liver failure, so concerning associated symptoms must guide urgency.

Associated Conditions

  • oral bacterial biofilm, periodontal or dental disease, tongue coating, dry mouth, food, tobacco, dentures, nasal or tonsillar disease, respiratory disease, medication, or less commonly systemic metabolism

Assessment Method

Ask permission and assess without shaming; document duration, timing, diet, tobacco and alcohol, oral hygiene, dentures, medications, dry mouth, dental pain or bleeding, nasal and sinus symptoms, cough, reflux, fever, weight change, and metabolic symptoms, then inspect the tongue, teeth, gums, mucosa, tonsils, and saliva rather than relying on odor alone.

Positive Finding

A frequent or persistent unpleasant odor is detected in exhaled breath beyond a brief food-related or morning episode.

Mechanism

In most cases oral anaerobic bacteria break down retained proteins and cells into volatile sulfur compounds, while reduced saliva increases retention and selected nasal, pulmonary, medication, or systemic processes add other odor-producing molecules.

Evidence Limitations

Food, tobacco, temporary morning breath, masking products, impaired smell, pseudo-halitosis, and halitophobia complicate assessment; a fruity, ammonia-like, or musty odor cannot diagnose a metabolic disorder. Therefore, Halitosis cannot confirm or exclude oral bacterial biofilm, periodontal or dental disease, tongue coating, dry mouth, food, tobacco, dentures, nasal or tonsillar disease, respiratory disease, medication, or less commonly systemic metabolism by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as transient food odor, dry mouth, periodontal disease, tonsillolith, pseudo-halitosis.

False Negative Situations

Halitosis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of oral bacterial biofilm, periodontal or dental disease, tongue coating, dry mouth, food, tobacco, dentures, nasal or tonsillar disease, respiratory disease, medication, or less commonly systemic metabolism.

Modern Status

Current common but nonspecific sign; it is usually oral in origin and should be evaluated respectfully because self-perception and examiner perception are imperfect.
