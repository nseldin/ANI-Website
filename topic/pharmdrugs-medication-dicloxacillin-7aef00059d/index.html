Used To Treat

Mild to moderate susceptible staphylococcal infections, commonly skin and soft-tissue disease, and selected oral step-down situations after the invasive source and stability requirements are satisfied.

Mechanism

The bulky side chain solves two problems: it protects the drug from gastric acid enough for oral use and obstructs many staphylococcal beta-lactamases. Food decreases absorption, so dosing on an empty stomach, generally one hour before or two hours after meals according to product instruction, preserves exposure. The drug still requires adequate fT>MIC and source control. Protein binding and renal plus nonrenal elimination influence concentration; hepatic injury, neutropenia, and interstitial nephritis become more relevant during extended courses.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Serious beta-lactam hypersensitivity can occur. Oral treatment failure can allow rapid MSSA progression, so spreading infection, systemic signs, persistent bacteremia, or deep focal pain requires escalation rather than repeated outpatient dosing.

Contraindications

  • Serious penicillin hypersensitivity requires avoidance or specialist assessment.

  • Do not use for MRSA or as unsupported oral monotherapy for unstable S. aureus bacteremia/endocarditis because target resistance and compartment exposure are separate problems.

Nursing Essentials

  • Give on an empty stomach with a full glass of water according to the product instruction because food lowers absorption; explain the reason so the timing is not treated as optional trivia.

  • Mark the edge of cellulitis when useful and reassess pain, spread, fever, drainage, perfusion, and abscess because incision and drainage may be more important than changing oral antibiotics.

Interactions

  • Probenecid prolongs penicillin exposure by reducing tubular secretion.

  • Warfarin response can change during infection and antibiotic therapy, so INR monitoring is appropriate in susceptible patients.

Key Labs

  • Culture when purulence, recurrence, treatment failure, or invasive disease warrants it; CBC, renal, and liver tests for prolonged therapy or symptoms.

  • No serum dicloxacillin level is routine; apparent failure prompts adherence/food timing, organism, abscess, perfusion, and severity review.

Nclex Traps

  • Dicloxacillin is an oral MSSA drug, not an MRSA drug.

  • Empty-stomach timing is used to protect absorption, not because food causes a dangerous chemical reaction.

    Note

    Pediatric indication, meningitis role, formulation, dose, interval, renal maturation, and maximum dose differ by agent and age. Use the exact label or pediatric infectious-disease protocol because beta-lactams are not safely scaled from an adult regimen by intuition.

    Older adult caution

    Note

    Older adults can have sharply reduced renal clearance despite an unremarkable serum creatinine. Estimate kidney function, reassess it during acute illness, and connect new confusion, myoclonus, or seizure to possible beta-lactam accumulation rather than assuming delirium is unrelated.

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Many beta-lactams have substantial pregnancy and lactation experience, but infection severity, allergy history, maternal organ function, placental exposure, neonatal bilirubin handling, and the exact agent still matter. Treat serious infection promptly with an evidence-supported regimen rather than applying one class-wide pregnancy rule.

  • Oral isoxazolyl penicillin

  • Staphylococcal penicillinase stability

  • PBP inhibition in susceptible MSSA

Description

Dicloxacillin is an oral, penicillinase-resistant antistaphylococcal beta-lactam antibiotic that binds native PBPs in susceptible MSSA and blocks peptidoglycan cross-linking, causing bactericidal cell-wall failure. Its bulky dichloro-isoxazolyl side chain makes the molecule acid stable for oral use and protects its beta-lactam ring from staphylococcal penicillinase. It does not treat MRSA because PBP2a has low beta-lactam affinity. Oral bioavailability and food interference make it unsuitable as an automatic substitute for IV nafcillin, oxacillin, or cefazolin in unstable bacteremia, endocarditis, CNS infection, or an uncontrolled deep focus.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, epigastric discomfort, diarrhea, glossitis, candidiasis, rash, urticaria, and CDI.

  • Rare anaphylaxis, severe cutaneous reaction, hepatitis/cholestasis, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, or interstitial nephritis.
