Definition

Stewart-Holmes rebound sign is a bedside coordination sign. It is a clue to cerebellar dysfunction. A positive finding means the forearm overshoots and may strike the patient without the examiner's protection.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Stewart-Holmes rebound sign means the forearm overshoots and may strike the patient without the examiner's protection.

Why It Matters

The observed Stewart-Holmes rebound sign finding can signal cerebellar dysfunction. Cerebellar failure delays antagonist-muscle braking after sudden load release. Stewart-Holmes rebound sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • cerebellar dysfunction

Assessment Method

Ask the patient to flex the elbow against resistance while guarding the face, then release the resistance unexpectedly.

Positive Finding

The forearm overshoots and may strike the patient without the examiner's protection.

Mechanism

Cerebellar failure delays antagonist-muscle braking after sudden load release.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Stewart-Holmes rebound sign finding raises concern for cerebellar dysfunction because cerebellar failure delays antagonist-muscle braking after sudden load release. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Stewart-Holmes rebound sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Stewart-Holmes rebound sign to focus evaluation of cerebellar dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: motor weakness, sensory ataxia, functional disorder. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask the patient to flex the elbow against resistance while guarding the face, then release the resistance unexpectedly. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Stewart-Holmes rebound sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Strength, comprehension, and examiner release technique affect the result. Therefore, Stewart-Holmes rebound sign cannot confirm or exclude cerebellar dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • motor weakness

  • sensory ataxia

  • functional disorder

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as motor weakness, sensory ataxia, functional disorder.

False Negative Situations

Stewart-Holmes rebound sign may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of cerebellar dysfunction.

Modern Status

Historical but useful coordination adjunct; requires guarding. Stewart-Holmes rebound sign remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude cerebellar dysfunction. Apply the reviewed limitation for Stewart-Holmes rebound sign: Strength, comprehension, and examiner release technique affect the result.

Safety Precautions

Observe Stewart-Holmes rebound sign with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Ask the patient to flex the elbow against resistance while guarding the face, then release the resistance unexpectedly. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Always shield the patient's face and use minimal force.
