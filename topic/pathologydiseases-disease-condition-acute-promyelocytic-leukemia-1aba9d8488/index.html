Definition

Acute promyelocytic leukemia is a distinct, highly treatable subtype of acute myeloid leukemia driven most often by a PML::RARA fusion. It is a hematologic emergency because abnormal promyelocytes can trigger simultaneous coagulation activation and fibrinolysis, causing fatal bleeding before definitive therapy has time to work.

Pathology

Bone marrow and blood accumulate abnormal promyelocytes, often with heavy granulation and bundles of Auer rods. The characteristic fusion usually results from a translocation involving chromosomes 15 and 17. Molecular confirmation of PML::RARA defines classic APL and enables sensitive monitoring for residual disease.

Pathophysiology

  • PML::RARA blocks normal retinoic-acid signaling, trapping myeloid precursors at the promyelocyte stage. The leukemic cells express procoagulant proteins and promote excessive fibrinolysis, so thrombocytopenia, clotting-factor consumption, and fibrinogen loss can coexist with thrombosis.

  • All-trans retinoic acid releases the differentiation block, while arsenic trioxide targets the fusion protein and promotes leukemic-cell differentiation and death. Rapid differentiation can itself produce cytokine-mediated capillary leak called differentiation syndrome.

Etiology

APL is caused by an acquired somatic genetic rearrangement in a hematopoietic precursor; it is not inherited from the patient or contagious. Most cases arise de novo. A minority occur after prior cytotoxic therapy, but an individual patient usually has no preventable initiating exposure.

Risk Factors

  • Prior treatment with selected topoisomerase-II-inhibiting chemotherapy

  • Prior cytotoxic therapy for another malignancy, although therapy-related APL is uncommon

  • Most cases occur without an identifiable or preventable exposure risk

Signs Symptoms

  • Easy bruising, petechiae, mucosal bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding, hematuria, gastrointestinal bleeding, or intracranial hemorrhage

  • Fatigue, pallor, dyspnea, fever, and infection from marrow failure

  • Bone pain, leukocytosis or leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia

  • During differentiation therapy: new fever, rapid weight gain, edema, hypotension, dyspnea, pulmonary infiltrates, or acute kidney injury

Diagnostics

  • When APL is clinically and morphologically suspected, obtain urgent hematology consultation and begin all-trans retinoic acid immediately while rapid molecular confirmation is pursued; waiting for final genetics can permit catastrophic hemorrhage.

  • Confirm PML::RARA by rapid reverse-transcription PCR, FISH, or another validated molecular method. Bone marrow morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and a full myeloid evaluation define the disease and exclude mimics.

  • If morphology suggests APL but PML::RARA testing is negative, urgently investigate a rare alternative RARA fusion because the fusion partner can change sensitivity to all-trans retinoic acid and arsenic.

  • Risk stratification and subsequent molecular monitoring use presenting counts and serial molecular testing appropriate to the confirmed fusion and treatment protocol.

Labs

  • CBC often shows anemia and thrombocytopenia with a variable white-cell count; the smear may show abnormal promyelocytes and Auer rods.

  • Coagulation studies commonly show falling fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer or fibrin-degradation products, and prolonged clotting times, but severe bleeding can occur before every value is abnormal.

  • Monitor electrolytes, creatinine, liver tests, uric acid, and ECG variables because treatment can cause tumor lysis, organ injury, and arsenic-related QT prolongation.

  • PML::RARA molecular testing confirms the diagnosis and later assesses remission or relapse.

Assessment

Treat the initial assessment as a hemorrhage emergency. Assess neurologic status, headache, vision, mucosal and menstrual bleeding, urine and stool blood, bruising, dyspnea, infection, and procedure sites while reviewing the smear and serial coagulation studies. After differentiation therapy starts, add frequent surveillance for fever, weight gain, edema, oxygen requirement, hypotension, kidney injury, and pulmonary findings.

Differential

Differentiate APL from other acute myeloid leukemia subtypes, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, sepsis-associated DIC, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and other microangiopathies, immune thrombocytopenia, liver-related coagulopathy, and medication-related bleeding. Rapid PML::RARA testing resolves the specific leukemia identity, but emergency retinoic-acid therapy should not wait when suspicion is high.

Treatments

  • Start all-trans retinoic acid as soon as APL is suspected because reversing the differentiation block quickly can reduce the coagulopathy, and provide protocol-directed blood-product support while confirmation is pending.

  • For confirmed classic PML::RARA APL, definitive induction and consolidation use all-trans retinoic acid with arsenic trioxide for many lower-risk patients, while higher-risk disease generally requires additional cytoreductive therapy selected by an APL-experienced hematology team. A rare alternative RARA fusion may respond differently, so the confirmed molecular partner directs definitive retinoid and arsenic use after the emergency empiric phase.

  • Supportive care aggressively corrects thrombocytopenia and low fibrinogen using protocol targets, treats infection, prevents tumor lysis, and minimizes invasive procedures until the coagulopathy is controlled.

  • Suspected differentiation syndrome requires immediate corticosteroid treatment and close cardiopulmonary monitoring; whether differentiation therapy is temporarily held depends on severity and specialist direction.

  • Relapse therapy is selected by prior exposure and molecular status and may include arsenic- or retinoid-based salvage and stem-cell transplantation in eligible patients.

Contraindications

  • Do not delay all-trans retinoic acid for bone-marrow scheduling or final molecular results when clinical suspicion is high.

  • Do not use heparin or antifibrinolytic therapy routinely to correct APL coagulopathy; these are exceptional specialist decisions because bleeding and thrombosis coexist.

  • Avoid lumbar puncture, central venous access, intramuscular injection, and other invasive procedures until hemostasis is adequate unless the lifesaving benefit clearly outweighs the risk.

  • All-trans retinoic acid and arsenic trioxide have major pregnancy and fetal-safety implications; urgent specialist planning is required rather than automatic abandonment of maternal treatment.

Safety Considerations

  • Coagulation support often requires testing and reassessment several times per day early in treatment; one improved value does not end hemorrhage risk.

  • Arsenic trioxide requires ECG and electrolyte management because QT prolongation can lead to malignant arrhythmia.

  • Differentiate infection, fluid overload, pulmonary hemorrhage, and differentiation syndrome promptly because presentations overlap but therapies diverge.

  • Avoid leukapheresis as routine cytoreduction in APL because invasive handling can worsen coagulopathic risk; follow the disease-specific protocol.

Nursing Priorities

  • Use bleeding precautions, minimize venipunctures, avoid rectal procedures and intramuscular injections, and apply prolonged pressure after necessary access because APL can consume platelets and fibrinogen before leukemia treatment controls the process.

  • Trend neurologic status, headache, vision, mucosal bleeding, stool and urine blood, coagulation results, fibrinogen, platelets, and transfusion response; report change immediately.

  • During differentiation therapy, measure weight, intake and output, oxygen need, lung sounds, edema, blood pressure, renal function, and temperature for early capillary leak.

  • Monitor QT interval and maintain protocol-directed potassium and magnesium during arsenic therapy because QT prolongation and electrolyte loss can precipitate dangerous ventricular arrhythmias; review every QT-prolonging medicine.

  • Maintain neutropenic and infection precautions as indicated while recognizing that fever may also signal differentiation syndrome.

Red Flags

  • New severe headache, focal deficit, vomiting, confusion, or reduced consciousness suggesting intracranial hemorrhage

  • Severe headache with visual symptoms, pulsatile tinnitus, vomiting, or papilledema during all-trans retinoic acid therapy, particularly in a child, suggesting intracranial hypertension

  • Uncontrolled mucosal, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, urinary, postpartum, or procedure-site bleeding

  • Fever with dyspnea, rapid weight gain, edema, hypotension, rising creatinine, or pulmonary infiltrates during differentiation therapy

  • Syncope, palpitations, marked QT prolongation, or severe electrolyte disturbance during arsenic treatment

Complications

  • Fatal intracranial or pulmonary hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and thrombosis

  • Differentiation syndrome with respiratory, renal, and circulatory failure

  • All-trans retinoic acid-associated intracranial hypertension or pseudotumor cerebri, especially in children

  • Tumor lysis syndrome, infection, hepatic injury, and arsenic-associated arrhythmia

  • Molecular persistence or relapse and late treatment toxicity

Prognosis

APL is now among the most curable acute leukemias when disease-specific therapy begins promptly. The greatest immediate threat is early death from hemorrhage, differentiation syndrome, or infection, so rapid recognition and meticulous supportive care are as important as the antileukemic regimen. Molecular follow-up detects recurrence before overt marrow failure in many patients.

Prevention

APL itself cannot usually be prevented. Treatment-related mortality is reduced by immediate retinoic-acid initiation when suspected, rapid transfer to an experienced center, protocol-based transfusion support, and early differentiation-syndrome treatment. Survivors need molecular surveillance and therapy-specific cardiac, reproductive, hepatic, and second-cancer follow-up.

Patient Education

  • Explain that APL is unusually curable but dangerous during the first days because clotting proteins and platelets are being consumed.

  • Report any headache, vision change, blood in urine or stool, heavy vaginal bleeding, breathlessness, fever, sudden weight gain, swelling, or fainting immediately.

  • Do not take aspirin, NSAIDs, supplements, or anticoagulants unless the hematology team has reviewed them.

  • Keep every molecular-monitoring appointment even after blood counts normalize.

Special Populations

  • Pediatric APL uses age-appropriate protocols and requires attention to anthracycline exposure, growth, fertility, and long-term effects.

  • Pregnancy requires immediate multidisciplinary hematology and maternal-fetal planning because trimester, bleeding risk, and teratogenic treatment effects change the regimen.

  • Older adults and patients with cardiac, renal, or hepatic disease need individualized intensity without weakening emergency coagulopathy support.

Nclex Traps

  • APL is the acute leukemia in which suspected diagnosis triggers immediate all-trans retinoic acid before final confirmation.

  • The priority is hemorrhage prevention and differentiation-syndrome surveillance, not routine leukapheresis or heparin.

  • A rising white-cell count after differentiation begins can reflect treatment response but increases differentiation-syndrome risk; it is not interpreted in isolation.

  • New dyspnea and edema during ATRA or arsenic therapy require urgent assessment, not routine fluid administration.
