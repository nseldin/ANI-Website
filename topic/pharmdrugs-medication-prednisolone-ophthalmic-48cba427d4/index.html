Class

Topical ophthalmic glucocorticoid anti-inflammatory suspension

Used To Treat

Steroid-responsive inflammation of the conjunctiva, cornea and anterior eye segment after infection and contraindications have been assessed; it is not treatment for an undiagnosed red eye.

Description

Prednisolone acetate ophthalmic is a potent local glucocorticoid that suppresses ocular cytokines, leukocyte migration, vascular permeability and tissue edema. It can rapidly improve inflammation, but the same immune and wound-healing suppression can worsen herpes simplex, fungal, mycobacterial or purulent infection, thin the cornea/sclera, delay healing, raise intraocular pressure and cause posterior subcapsular cataract with prolonged use.

Mechanism

Prednisolone binds cytosolic glucocorticoid receptors, changes gene transcription and induces anti-inflammatory proteins while suppressing NF-kappa-B/AP-1 cytokine pathways, phospholipase-A2 products and leukocyte trafficking. Reduced inflammatory signaling lowers redness and edema but also limits host defense, fibroblast activity and epithelial repair. Steroid-responsive trabecular changes reduce aqueous outflow and raise IOP in susceptible patients.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Contraindicated in untreated purulent ocular infection, epithelial herpes simplex dendritic keratitis, vaccinia/varicella, mycobacterial infection and fungal ocular disease. Prolonged use can cause glaucoma/optic-nerve damage and posterior subcapsular cataract. Corneal or scleral thinning can progress to perforation. Renewal beyond short courses requires slit-lamp and IOP review.

Adverse Effects

  • Burning/blur, elevated IOP, steroid-induced glaucoma, posterior subcapsular cataract, delayed healing, secondary infection, herpes reactivation, corneal/scleral thinning or perforation and rebound inflammation after abrupt discontinuation.

Contraindications

  • Untreated purulent, most viral including epithelial HSV, mycobacterial or fungal ocular infection; hypersensitivity. Clarify glaucoma, corneal thinning, recent surgery and whether infection has been excluded.

Nursing Essentials

  • Shake suspensions thoroughly. Verify infection evaluation, duration and taper; never encourage unsupervised reuse for a new red eye. Monitor IOP and slit-lamp findings when treatment is prolonged or repeated.

  • Verify the exact eye, formulation, concentration, frequency, contact-lens instructions, and whether other drops must be separated by at least 5 minutes. Never let the bottle tip touch the eye, lashes, fingers, or another surface.

  • After the drop, gently close the eyelid and press the nasolacrimal duct for 1 to 2 minutes when appropriate. This reduces drainage into the nose and systemic exposure; wiping excess from the skin also limits local irritation.

Interactions

  • Other ocular steroids add IOP/cataract risk. Topical NSAIDs plus steroids can increase corneal-healing complications in susceptible eyes. Immunosuppressive therapy can add infection risk.

Key Labs

  • Visual acuity, slit-lamp/fluorescein examination and infection assessment

  • Intraocular pressure during prolonged/repeated treatment

  • Lens and corneal/scleral integrity

Nclex Traps

  • A steroid can make a red eye look better while an infection becomes more destructive.

  • Shake prednisolone acetate suspension before use.

  • Do not stop a prolonged ocular steroid abruptly without the prescribed taper.
