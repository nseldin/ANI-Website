Class

Chimeric mouse-human IgG1 monoclonal antibody against extracellular EGFR

Used To Treat

Selected head-and-neck squamous-cell cancers and RAS-wild-type metastatic colorectal cancer, including BRAF V600E disease with encorafenib under the labeled regimen. Colorectal use requires the exact molecular and treatment context because downstream RAS activation bypasses EGFR blockade.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNINGS: serious and sometimes fatal infusion reactions, and cardiopulmonary arrest. Severe reactions often occur with the first infusion. Premedicate with an H1 antagonist, administer where resuscitation is available, monitor during treatment and for at least 1 hour afterward, and permanently discontinue a serious reaction. Cardiopulmonary arrest or sudden death has occurred with radiation or platinum/fluorouracil regimens; correct electrolyte abnormalities and monitor patients with coronary disease, heart failure, or dysrhythmia risk closely.

Mechanism

Cetuximab competes with EGF and TGF-alpha for extracellular EGFR binding, preventing receptor activation, dimerization-dependent phosphorylation, and downstream RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK and PI3K-AKT survival signaling. Receptor internalization and reduced VEGF and matrix-metalloproteinase signaling add antitumor effects, while the IgG1 Fc region can engage immune effectors for ADCC. A constitutively active RAS protein remains on even when EGFR is blocked, explaining lack of benefit in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer. Alpha-gal-specific IgE can cross-link when cetuximab enters the bloodstream, rapidly releasing mast-cell mediators and explaining why the first infusion can trigger severe anaphylaxis despite antihistamine premedication.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before the first infusion, ask specifically about alpha-gal syndrome, delayed reactions after mammalian meat, tick bites, gelatin or mammalian-product allergy, and prior biologic reactions; vague 'food allergy' documentation can miss the mechanism.

  • Give ordered H1 premedication, verify emergency medications and airway support, obtain baseline vitals, and observe at least 1 hour after infusion. Approximately 90 percent of severe infusion reactions in labeling occurred with the first infusion, so prior tolerance lowers but does not erase later risk.

  • Check magnesium, potassium, and calcium weekly during therapy and for at least 8 weeks after the last dose. The extended window exists because cetuximab persists and EGFR-TRPM6 blockade can continue renal magnesium wasting after treatment stops.

Contraindications

  • Serious prior cetuximab hypersensitivity requires permanent discontinuation; carefully assess alpha-gal syndrome, red-meat allergy, tick-bite history, and mammalian-product reactions before first exposure.

  • Do not use for RAS-mutant metastatic colorectal cancer; the downstream signal remains active and treatment can add toxicity without the intended pathway benefit.

  • Severe uncorrected magnesium, potassium, or calcium depletion and unexplained active pulmonary symptoms require evaluation before further dosing.

Interactions

  • Encorafenib plus cetuximab is a defined BRAF V600E colorectal regimen; the combination is not evidence that either drug treats every BRAF- or EGFR-related cancer.

  • Radiation and platinum/fluorouracil head-and-neck regimens can increase mucosal, skin, electrolyte, and cardiopulmonary burden.

  • Other magnesium-wasting, nephrotoxic, QT-prolonging, or pulmonary-toxic drugs can magnify downstream consequences even without a direct pharmacokinetic interaction.

Key Labs

  • RAS status for metastatic colorectal cancer; BRAF V600E when considering encorafenib combination

  • Magnesium, potassium, calcium, creatinine, and hydration status weekly and for at least 8 weeks after therapy

  • CBC and liver tests according to regimen

  • ECG for severe electrolyte loss or dysrhythmia symptoms

  • Pulse oximetry and chest imaging for suspected ILD/pneumonitis

Nclex Traps

  • Cetuximab rash is an on-target EGFR effect, but severe pain, blistering, purulence, fever, or functional impairment needs active treatment rather than reassurance alone.

  • Cetuximab is chimeric IgG1; panitumumab is fully human IgG2. That structural difference helps explain cetuximab's alpha-gal and first-infusion emphasis.

  • A RAS mutation is downstream of EGFR, so blocking the receptor cannot turn off a constitutively active RAS signal.

Population Risks

  • People with alpha-gal syndrome, red-meat allergy, or relevant tick exposure have increased cetuximab anaphylaxis risk.

  • Patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, dysrhythmia, or severe electrolyte disturbance have less reserve for cardiopulmonary events.

  • Pregnancy exposure can harm a fetus; follow current contraception and post-treatment guidance.

Description

Cetuximab is an IV chimeric IgG1 antibody that binds the extracellular domain of EGFR, blocks growth-factor signaling, promotes receptor downregulation, and can recruit immune cells for antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. It is clinically distinctive because preexisting IgE to the alpha-gal carbohydrate on cetuximab can cause abrupt first-infusion anaphylaxis, especially in people with alpha-gal syndrome, red-meat allergy, or certain tick exposures. Its acneiform rash and magnesium wasting are not random side effects: they result from blocking normal EGFR function in hair-follicle epithelium and distal-tubule magnesium transport.

Adverse Effects

  • Acneiform rash, xerosis, fissures, paronychia, pruritus, photosensitivity, and secondary skin infection

  • Hypomagnesemia, sometimes accompanied by hypokalemia or hypocalcemia

  • Infusion reaction or alpha-gal-mediated anaphylaxis

  • Mucositis, diarrhea, fatigue, headache, and ocular inflammation

  • Cardiopulmonary arrest, interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis, and embryo-fetal harm
