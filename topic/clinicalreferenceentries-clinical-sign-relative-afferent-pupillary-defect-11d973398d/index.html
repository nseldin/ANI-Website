Definition

Relative afferent pupillary defect is a bedside pupillary sign. It is a clue to asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction. A positive finding means both pupils paradoxically dilate or constrict less when light moves to the affected eye.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Relative afferent pupillary defect means both pupils paradoxically dilate or constrict less when light moves to the affected eye.

Why It Matters

The observed Relative afferent pupillary defect finding can signal asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction. Reduced afferent input from one retina or optic nerve drives less bilateral Edinger-Westphal activation. Relative afferent pupillary defect cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction

Assessment Method

Perform the swinging-flashlight test with equal-duration illumination of each eye.

Positive Finding

Both pupils paradoxically dilate or constrict less when light moves to the affected eye.

Mechanism

Reduced afferent input from one retina or optic nerve drives less bilateral Edinger-Westphal activation.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Relative afferent pupillary defect finding raises concern for asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction because reduced afferent input from one retina or optic nerve drives less bilateral Edinger-Westphal activation. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Relative afferent pupillary defect does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Relative afferent pupillary defect to focus evaluation of asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: anisocoria, poor technique, asymmetric retinal disease. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Perform the swinging-flashlight test with equal-duration illumination of each eye. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Relative afferent pupillary defect as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Bilateral symmetric disease may produce no RAPD and media opacity alone usually does not. Therefore, Relative afferent pupillary defect cannot confirm or exclude asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • anisocoria

  • poor technique

  • asymmetric retinal disease

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as anisocoria, poor technique, asymmetric retinal disease.

False Negative Situations

Relative afferent pupillary defect may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction.

The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Keep light intensity and timing equal and do not call baseline anisocoria an RAPD.
