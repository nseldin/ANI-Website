Definition

Peritonsillar abscess develops when suppurative infection forms between the tonsillar capsule and pharyngeal muscle, displacing local structures and threatening the airway. The characteristic pattern is severe unilateral throat pain, muffled hot-potato voice, trismus, drooling, uvular deviation, and tender cervical nodes. Confirm or stage it with airway and secretion assessment followed by focused oropharyngeal examination; distinguish it from severe tonsillitis, retropharyngeal abscess, epiglottitis, dental infection, and malignancy by trismus, asymmetry, imaging, and drainage. The priority is to secure a threatened airway before procedures because airway obstruction can complicate Peritonsillar abscess. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • airway and secretion assessment followed by focused oropharyngeal examination

  • needle aspiration or drainage confirming pus

  • ultrasound or contrast CT when anatomy is unclear or deep-neck extension is suspected

  • Distinguish Peritonsillar abscess from severe tonsillitis, retropharyngeal abscess, epiglottitis, dental infection, and malignancy by trismus, asymmetry, imaging, and drainage.

Labs

  • labs are often not primary unless infection/systemic disease is suspected

Treatments

  • secure a threatened airway before procedures

  • drain the abscess and give antimicrobial therapy

  • provide analgesia, hydration, and otolaryngology follow-up

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for stridor, inability to handle secretions, rapidly increasing neck swelling, toxic appearance, chest pain, crepitus, hypotension, or worsening trismus because airway obstruction, deep-neck spread, or mediastinitis may be developing.

  • Assess airway patency, drooling, muffled voice, trismus, unilateral swelling, uvular deviation, neck movement, hydration, and respiratory effort because deep swelling can obstruct the airway or spread into neck spaces.

  • Keep the patient upright and nothing by mouth while maintaining suction and difficult-airway equipment nearby because secretions and procedural drainage can compromise an already narrowed airway.

  • Establish vascular access and administer prescribed antibiotics, fluids, analgesia, and corticosteroid when indicated because infection, poor intake, and edema reinforce one another.

  • Prepare otolaryngology drainage and obtain cultures when directed because a mature pus collection usually requires source control rather than antibiotics alone.

Complications

  • airway obstruction can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

  • aspiration can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

  • deep-neck infection can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

  • mediastinitis can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

  • sepsis can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

  • internal-jugular thrombophlebitis can complicate Peritonsillar abscess.

Red Flags

  • Stridor, drooling, or inability to handle secretions

  • Rapidly increasing neck swelling or respiratory distress

  • Toxic appearance, hypotension, or altered mental status

  • Chest pain, crepitus, or severe neck stiffness suggesting deep spread

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to complete antibiotics, hydrate as tolerated after clearance, and return immediately for breathing difficulty, drooling, increasing swelling, or inability to drink.

  • Explain that drainage removes trapped infection and that recurrent abscess or repeated tonsillitis may require specialist follow-up.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not force oral examination in a patient who cannot handle secretions

  • Antibiotics alone may not control a mature drainable abscess
