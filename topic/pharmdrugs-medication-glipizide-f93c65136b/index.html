Used To Treat

Adjunct to nutrition and activity for adults with type 2 diabetes who retain functioning pancreatic beta cells. Immediate-release and extended-release products have different timing instructions. It is not effective for type 1 diabetes and does not treat DKA.

Mechanism

Glipizide binds the SUR1 regulatory subunit of the ATP-sensitive potassium channel on pancreatic beta cells. KATP-channel closure depolarizes the cell membrane, opens voltage-gated calcium channels, raises intracellular calcium, and triggers insulin-granule exocytosis. The pathway partially bypasses normal glucose sensing, so insulin release can continue when caloric intake is low; that explains fasting, exercise, alcohol-related, renal/hepatic, and dosing-error hypoglycemia. Functioning beta cells are required. The immediate-release product produces a prominent meal-related insulin response and is generally taken before meals, while extended-release matrix delivery spreads exposure across the day and must be swallowed intact.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: All sulfonylureas can cause severe hypoglycemia, including seizure, coma, and death. Risk rises with missed meals, malnutrition, prolonged exercise, alcohol, renal or hepatic dysfunction, older age, and combination glucose-lowering therapy. Historical class labeling also discusses increased cardiovascular mortality observed with an older sulfonylurea; use individualized contemporary cardiovascular-risk therapy rather than assuming every agent has equivalent outcome evidence.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity to glipizide or product components

  • Type 1 diabetes or DKA

  • Active hypoglycemia; use conservative dosing or alternatives when meals, renal/hepatic clearance, or self-management are unreliable

Nursing Essentials

  • Give immediate-release glipizide about 30 minutes before a meal under the prescribed plan; give ER with breakfast/first main meal and do not crush, chew, or split the osmotic/matrix tablet.

  • Teach rapid-glucose rescue and recheck. If a meal is delayed or the patient is NPO, question the dose rather than giving a secretagogue automatically.

Interactions

  • Miconazole, fluconazole, other CYP2C9 inhibitors, alcohol, insulin, and additional glucose-lowering drugs can amplify hypoglycemia.

  • Beta blockers may mask palpitations/tremor; clonidine or reserpine can also blunt warning signs.

  • Colesevelam can reduce glipizide exposure; administer glipizide at least 4 hours before it under labeling.

Key Labs

  • Glucose/CGM pattern and A1c

  • Renal and hepatic function

  • Timing, severity, awareness, and recurrence of hypoglycemia plus meal reliability

Nclex Traps

  • Shorter than glyburide does not mean short enough to ignore recurrent hypoglycemia.

  • Sulfonylureas release insulin; they do not replace insulin in type 1 diabetes.

  • An intact ER shell can appear in stool and does not automatically mean the medication failed to release.

Description

Glipizide is a second-generation sulfonylurea that makes pancreatic beta cells release insulin. It is shorter acting than glyburide and has inactive metabolites, so it is often the sulfonylurea chosen when renal impairment or older age makes prolonged hypoglycemia a concern, although it can still cause severe lows. Its benefit depends on surviving beta cells; it cannot manufacture insulin when beta-cell function is absent. The practical identity is therefore meal-linked glucose lowering with hypoglycemia and weight-gain risk, not a general insulin-sensitizing drug.

Adverse Effects

  • Hypoglycemia, sometimes prolonged or recurrent

  • Weight gain, nausea, constipation/diarrhea, dizziness, headache, and photosensitivity

  • Rare cholestatic/hepatocellular injury, cytopenias, hyponatremia, severe skin reaction, or hemolysis in G6PD deficiency
