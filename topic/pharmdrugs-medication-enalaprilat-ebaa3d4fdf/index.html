Class

Intravenous active ACE inhibitor and enalapril metabolite

Used To Treat

Hypertension when oral therapy is not practical according to injection labeling. It is not a preferred minute-to-minute hypertensive-emergency infusion because onset, peak effect, and duration are slower and less titratable than dedicated IV vasodilator infusions.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. Parenteral administration does not reduce fetal risk. Enalaprilat directly inhibits fetal and maternal ACE and should be stopped when pregnancy is detected.

Mechanism

Enalaprilat directly occupies ACE without requiring metabolic activation. It decreases angiotensin-II production, reduces AT1-mediated arteriolar and efferent-arteriole constriction, lowers aldosterone-dependent sodium retention and potassium excretion, and slows bradykinin degradation. Systemic arteriolar dilation lowers afterload and blood pressure; efferent dilation lowers glomerular capillary pressure. In a stable patient that may reduce maladaptive load, but in a patient whose filtration depends on angiotensin-II support it can cause oliguria and progressive azotemia. Enalaprilat is substantially renally excreted, so CKD or AKI prolongs exposure. Its delayed peak and prolonged action explain why repeated dosing before the full effect is known can overshoot pressure and why it is not equivalent to a rapidly titrated nicardipine or clevidipine infusion.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before the IV dose, verify pressure trend, perfusion, volume, renal function, potassium, pregnancy, recent oral ACE inhibitor or ARNI use, and why oral therapy is impossible.

  • Monitor pressure long enough to capture delayed peak effect; trend urine output and kidney function because renal accumulation can extend the response.

  • Check pressure and orthostasis, volume, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, pregnancy status, cough, facial or tongue swelling, and every NSAID, diuretic, potassium product, lithium drug, or duplicate RAAS blocker.

  • Teach that persistent dry cough should be evaluated, while lip, tongue, throat, voice, or swallowing change is an emergency because bradykinin swelling can obstruct the airway.

Contraindications

  • Previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, hereditary or idiopathic angioedema, pregnancy, and relevant hypersensitivity.

  • Avoid casual use in unstable perfusion, acute evolving kidney injury, severe volume depletion, or when a titratable emergency agent is required.

  • Pregnancy, previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, and hereditary or idiopathic angioedema where specified by labeling.

  • Do not combine with sacubitril/valsartan or administer within 36 hours; do not combine aliskiren with an ACE inhibitor in diabetes.

Interactions

  • Diuretics and low intake magnify first-dose hypotension; NSAIDs can reduce effect and worsen renal perfusion.

  • Do not overlap with sacubitril/valsartan, potassium-raising therapy, lithium, or duplicate RAAS blockers without explicit management.

  • Potassium supplements or salt substitutes, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, diuretics, lithium, and duplicate RAAS blockers can produce hyperkalemia, AKI, hypotension, or lithium toxicity.

  • ACE inhibitor plus sacubitril/valsartan is contraindicated because overlapping peptide-degradation blockade increases angioedema risk.

Key Labs

  • Frequent pressure and perfusion checks, creatinine/eGFR, potassium, urine output, volume status, and sodium when heart failure or diuresis is present.

  • There is no routine serum enalaprilat level; duration is inferred from renal function and clinical response.

  • Baseline and follow-up potassium and creatinine/eGFR, blood pressure/orthostasis, volume, and urine albumin when kidney protection is the goal.

  • Investigate a major or progressive renal change for dehydration, NSAIDs, diuretics, sepsis, obstruction, hypotension, or renal-artery disease.

Nclex Traps

  • IV does not mean rapid titration. Enalaprilat can peak late and persist, so it differs from a continuous, quickly adjustable hypertensive-emergency drip.

  • Enalaprilat is active drug, not merely another spelling of oral enalapril.

  • ACE inhibition lowers angiotensin II and raises bradykinin; those are separate consequences of inhibiting the same enzyme.

  • A modest early creatinine rise can reflect lower intraglomerular pressure, but oliguria, severe hyperkalemia, symptoms, or a large progressive decline is not routine adaptation.

Description

Enalaprilat is the active intravenous ACE inhibitor produced when oral enalapril is hydrolyzed. Giving it IV bypasses absorption and prodrug conversion, but it does not create an instant, easily reversible pressure switch. Antihypertensive effect begins over minutes, may peak over several hours, and can persist for many hours, especially when renal clearance is reduced. That profile can be useful when a patient cannot take oral medication, yet it is hazardous in volume depletion, acute MI, unstable heart failure, bilateral renal-artery stenosis, or rapidly changing kidney function. The drug lowers angiotensin II and aldosterone and preserves bradykinin, so its central risks remain hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine rise, cough, and angioedema. Because an IV dose cannot be retrieved, pressure and volume assessment before administration matter more than route convenience.

Adverse Effects

  • Prolonged or excessive hypotension, dizziness, headache, nausea, and injection-related management burden can occur.

  • Oliguria, progressive azotemia, acute renal failure, hyperkalemia, angioedema, and shock are the major severe risks.

  • Class effects include dry cough, dizziness, hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine increase, and less commonly rash or taste change.

  • Airway angioedema, severe AKI, dangerous potassium elevation, neutropenia in susceptible patients, hepatic injury, and fetal toxicity are high-consequence events.
