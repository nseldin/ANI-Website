Used To Treat

Edema associated with heart failure, hepatic disease, and renal disease including nephrotic syndrome under current labeling. It is chosen when a potent small-volume oral or parenteral loop dose is useful, not because it is a fundamentally stronger transporter blocker at equivalent exposure.

Mechanism

Bumetanide is secreted from proximal tubular blood into the lumen and blocks NKCC2 on the apical surface of thick-ascending-limb cells. Sodium-chloride reabsorption and medullary-gradient generation fall. Loss of ROMK-supported lumen positivity increases calcium and magnesium excretion, while greater distal sodium delivery and RAAS activation increase potassium and hydrogen loss, producing hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. Its high potency reflects molecular and pharmacokinetic properties, not a different nephron target from furosemide. The terminal half-life is roughly one to one and a half hours in healthy adults and is prolonged in some hepatic or neonatal settings. If response is poor, assess absorption, perfusion, renal secretion, NSAIDs, sodium intake, and the threshold dose rather than equating a small tablet with weak therapy.

Contraindications

  • Anuria and hypersensitivity are core label contraindications. Severe sodium, potassium, chloride, or volume depletion and progressive oliguria or azotemia require correction or reassessment before more drug.

  • Cirrhosis with ascites requires cautious titration because an abrupt fluid or electrolyte shift can precipitate hepatic encephalopathy.

  • The current label also contraindicates hepatic coma and severe electrolyte depletion until the underlying state is corrected.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record weight, congestion, pressure, orthostasis, intake/output, recent response, creatinine/eGFR, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, and magnesium before aggressive dosing.

  • Reassess within the expected route-specific effect window. Teach daily weight and symptom thresholds, morning timing when feasible, fall prevention, and urgent reporting of hearing change or severe weakness.

  • State the dose in milligrams clearly and compare it with the prior loop exposure. Verify decimal placement and route because 1 mg bumetanide is not equivalent to 1 mg furosemide.

Interactions

  • NSAIDs can blunt natriuresis and worsen kidney perfusion; aminoglycosides, cisplatin, and other ototoxins increase hearing risk; lithium clearance falls with sodium depletion.

  • RAAS blockers and vasodilators add hypotension and filtration effects, while low potassium or magnesium increases digoxin and arrhythmia risk.

Key Labs

  • Daily weight, intake/output, blood pressure and orthostasis, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, and calcium or uric acid when clinically relevant.

  • Judge response by decongestion with preserved perfusion and acceptable chemistry, not urine volume alone.

Nclex Traps

  • Loop drugs increase distal sodium delivery, so potassium and hydrogen are lost together and hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis can result.

  • They increase urinary calcium and magnesium; thiazides decrease urinary calcium.

  • Bumetanide's small milligram dose reflects high potency. It does not mean the drug is gentler or safer than furosemide.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: The U.S. label has a prominent warning that bumetanide is a potent diuretic and excessive administration can cause profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion. Because about 1 mg is commonly equated with 40 mg oral furosemide, decimal, unit, and conversion errors deserve deliberate checking. Ototoxicity risk rises with high IV exposure, impaired renal clearance, or concurrent ototoxins.

Class Pathway

  • Thick ascending limb

  • Luminal NKCC2 inhibition

  • Approximately 40-fold furosemide milligram potency

Description

Bumetanide, historically branded Bumex, is a potent loop diuretic that blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, causing rapid sodium, chloride, and water loss. Roughly 1 mg of bumetanide produces a loop effect comparable to about 40 mg oral furosemide in common clinical conversion tables, so its small milligram number must never be read as a small pharmacologic dose. Oral absorption is generally rapid and more predictable than furosemide; diuresis usually begins within 30 to 60 minutes, reaches peak effect in about one to two hours, and is largely complete within four hours after a single oral dose in many patients. It is used for edema, not as a disease-modifying heart-failure drug. The short duration means sodium can be retained after the effect fades. Its potency, parenteral availability, and compact dose are useful, but an accidental tenfold or fortyfold conversion error can cause abrupt depletion.

Adverse Effects

  • Dose-related volume depletion, orthostasis, hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, and kidney-function change arise from potent salt loss and downstream aldosterone signaling.

  • Tinnitus or hearing loss becomes more likely with high or rapid IV exposure, kidney impairment, low albumin, or another ototoxin; severe rash, interstitial nephritis, cytopenia, and pancreatitis are less common.
