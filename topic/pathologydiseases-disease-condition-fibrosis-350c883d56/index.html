Plain Meaning

Fibrosis is scar-like collagen-rich extracellular matrix deposition after injury, inflammation, or abnormal repair that stiffens organs, distorts architecture, and reduces functional reserve when progressive.

Word Parts

  • Root

    fibr- / fibro-

    Meaning

    fiber or fibrous connective tissue

  • Root

    -osis

    Meaning

    condition, process, or abnormal state

Word Origin

Fibrosis is built from fiber-related roots plus -osis. The word points to a fibrous tissue process, not just temporary swelling.

Pathology

Fibrosis is excessive extracellular matrix deposition, especially collagen, after repeated injury or chronic inflammation. Fibroblasts and myofibroblasts are activated by mediators such as transforming growth factor beta, then lay down scar tissue. Early fibrosis may stabilize an injured area, but progressive fibrosis stiffens organs, distorts architecture, compresses vessels or ducts, and reduces functional reserve.

Etiology

Repeated tissue injury is the common theme. Examples include chronic viral hepatitis or steatohepatitis in the liver, interstitial lung disease, chronic kidney disease, radiation injury, autoimmune inflammation, ischemic injury, toxic exposures, and poorly controlled chronic inflammation.

Signs Symptoms

  • Often silent until enough tissue architecture or compliance is lost

  • Organ-specific findings such as dyspnea in lung fibrosis, portal hypertension in liver fibrosis, or reduced filtration in kidney fibrosis

  • Stiffness, reduced elasticity, impaired perfusion, or impaired drainage depending on the organ

Diagnostics

  • Imaging may show scarring, stiffness, reticulation, nodularity, or organ shrinkage depending on site

  • Elastography estimates liver stiffness and fibrosis burden

  • Biopsy can show collagen deposition, architectural distortion, and fibrosis stage when needed

  • Function tests matter: pulmonary function tests, renal labs, liver synthetic markers, or cardiac imaging depending on organ

Treatments

  • Stop or reduce the injury driver whenever possible

  • Treat inflammation, infection, metabolic disease, toxin exposure, autoimmune activity, or obstruction according to the organ system

  • Some diseases have antifibrotic or disease-modifying therapy, but late scar architecture may be only partially reversible

Nursing Priorities

  • Frame fibrosis as scarring that changes tissue mechanics and organ function

  • Trend functional reserve: oxygenation, creatinine/eGFR, albumin/INR, exercise tolerance, fluid status, or perfusion depending on the organ

  • Escalate signs of decompensation because the scarred organ has less reserve during infection, bleeding, hypoxia, dehydration, or drug toxicity

Complications

  • Organ stiffness

  • Reduced reserve

  • Cirrhosis

  • Pulmonary restriction

  • Chronic kidney disease progression

  • Heart failure remodeling

  • Obstruction or pressure effects

Patient Education

  • Follow treatment for the underlying cause because fibrosis usually reflects repeated injury.

  • Report new shortness of breath, swelling, jaundice, confusion, low urine output, chest pain, or severe fatigue depending on the organ involved.

  • Avoid alcohol, smoking, toxins, or unsafe medication/supplement use when they worsen the affected organ.

Nclex Traps

  • Fibrosis is scarring, not edema.

  • A normal inflammatory marker does not erase established scar tissue.

  • The priority problem may be loss of reserve rather than active pain or fever.
