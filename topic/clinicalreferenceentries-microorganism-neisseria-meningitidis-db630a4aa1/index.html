Definition

Neisseria meningitidis, or meningococcus, is an encapsulated Gram-negative diplococcus that may colonize the human nasopharynx without symptoms but can invade the bloodstream or meninges and cause rapidly life-threatening meningococcal disease. Carriage and invasive disease are different states: many carriers remain well, whereas meningococcemia and meningitis require immediate antibiotics and public-health action.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    N. meningitidis is a Neisseria species divided into capsular serogroups. The organism name is not interchangeable with meningitis, because many organisms and noninfectious processes can inflame the meninges. Serogroup labels guide surveillance and vaccine coverage; they do not replace culture, polymerase-chain-reaction testing, or susceptibility when invasive disease is suspected.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The cells are kidney-bean-shaped Gram-negative diplococci, nonmotile, non-spore-forming, aerobic, and fastidious. Clinical culture grows best in a carbon-dioxide-enriched atmosphere, which is described as capnophilic growth. A polysaccharide capsule helps invasive strains resist complement-mediated killing and phagocytosis, while the Gram-negative outer membrane contains lipooligosaccharide. Complement is especially important for defense, explaining the extreme risk in terminal-complement deficiency or during complement-inhibitor therapy.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Humans are the only reservoir. Colonized people and patients can transmit organisms through respiratory and throat secretions during close or prolonged contact. Nasopharyngeal carriage often causes no illness, but invasion is more likely in susceptible hosts and crowded settings. The incubation of invasive disease is usually short, which is why exposed close contacts need rapid risk assessment rather than waiting for symptoms.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    After mucosal attachment, some strains cross the epithelial barrier and enter blood. Capsule and complement evasion permit rapid multiplication. Lipooligosaccharide triggers intense cytokine release, endothelial dysfunction, capillary leak, shock, and disseminated coagulation; this mechanism explains why a petechial or purpuric rash can accompany abrupt hemodynamic collapse. Crossing the blood-brain barrier produces purulent meningeal inflammation and increased intracranial risk.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    The two classic invasive presentations are meningitis and septicemia, and they can overlap. Fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, altered mental status, petechiae or purpura, rapidly worsening pain, hypotension, or poor perfusion requires emergency treatment. Infants may show lethargy, irritability, poor feeding, or a bulging fontanel rather than textbook meningismus. Do not delay empiric therapy for a lumbar puncture when the patient is unstable.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Blood and cerebrospinal-fluid culture or validated PCR can identify the organism; Gram stain may show intracellular Gram-negative diplococci but cannot safely exclude disease when negative. Specimens should be obtained promptly when this does not delay antibiotics. Susceptibility testing matters for definitive therapy and for public-health prophylaxis decisions in areas with relevant resistance.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Use Standard plus Droplet Precautions for suspected or confirmed meningococcal disease according to current duration guidance. Immediately notify the appropriate infection-control and public-health teams so close contacts can receive timely chemoprophylaxis. Vaccination reduces disease from included serogroups but does not eliminate all risk, especially in people receiving complement inhibitors. Emergency stabilization and antibiotics take priority over routine isolation tasks.
