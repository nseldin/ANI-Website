Risk Factors

  • Kidney failure reduces ammonium and net-acid excretion, so daily nonvolatile acid accumulates.

  • Hypoventilation retains carbon dioxide, while hyperventilation removes it faster than metabolism produces it.

  • Shock, sepsis, ketoacidosis, toxins, diarrhea, vomiting, diuretics, and mineralocorticoid disorders change acid production, bicarbonate balance, chloride, potassium, or effective circulating volume.

Signs Symptoms

  • Mild disorders may be clinically silent because buffering and compensation limit the pH change.

  • Deep rapid breathing can represent respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis, while slow or shallow breathing can cause respiratory acidosis.

  • Confusion, marked weakness, hypotension, dysrhythmia, tetany, seizure, or respiratory fatigue suggests severe physiology rather than an isolated laboratory curiosity.

Diagnostics

  • Begin with the patient's airway, ventilation, oxygenation, circulation, mental status, medications, losses, kidney function, and timeline because the same numbers have different causes and urgency in different settings.

  • Determine acidemia, alkalemia, or a near-normal pH; identify which PaCO2 or HCO3 change explains that direction; test expected compensation; then calculate the anion gap and investigate mixed processes when indicated.

  • Repeat testing when the result conflicts with the bedside picture because collection, transport, rapidly changing treatment, or comparing specimens drawn at different times can create apparent contradictions.

Labs

  • Typical adult teaching anchors are arterial pH 7.35-7.45, PaCO2 35-45 mm Hg, and HCO3 about 22-26 mEq/L, but the reporting laboratory and specimen define the actual interval.

  • Electrolytes, albumin, lactate, ketones, glucose, creatinine, chloride, potassium, and toxin studies identify the mechanism that a pH alone cannot reveal.

  • A venous gas can support many acid-base assessments, but venous oxygen values cannot substitute for PaO2 and venous PCO2 is not automatically interchangeable with PaCO2.

Treatments

  • Treat the process producing acid, base, or ventilatory change because forcing one laboratory number toward normal can leave the driver active.

  • Support airway, ventilation, oxygen delivery, perfusion, and circulation when unstable because buffers cannot compensate for failing organ function.

  • Use bicarbonate, acidifying therapy, dialysis, electrolyte replacement, antidotes, or ventilatory adjustments only for the specific indication and monitored clinical context because each intervention can create a second disorder.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess pH, PaCO2, bicarbonate, sodium, chloride, albumin, lactate, ketones, renal function, and clinical context because bicarbonate buffering links respiratory carbon-dioxide removal with renal acid handling.

  • Trend serial blood gases, electrolytes, anion gap, respiratory pattern, mental status, and hemodynamics because acid-base balance can change quickly while compensation or treatment is evolving.

  • Verify specimen timing and quality, then compare measured compensation with the expected response because an unexpected PaCO2 or bicarbonate suggests a second acid-base process rather than failed arithmetic.

  • Administer cause-directed fluids, insulin, dextrose, electrolytes, ventilation, or antidotal therapy as prescribed because correcting the generator of excess acid or base restores the bicarbonate buffer safely.

  • Escalate for pH below 7.20 or above 7.60, worsening potassium-related ECG changes, shock, seizures, severe confusion, or respiratory exhaustion because extreme acidemia or alkalemia impairs cardiac and neurologic function.

Complications

  • Severe acidemia can impair cardiac output, vascular responsiveness, and electrical stability and can worsen hyperkalemia.

  • Severe alkalemia can reduce cerebral blood flow and ionized calcium, producing neurologic and rhythm complications.

  • Mixed disorders can be missed when one process makes the pH appear deceptively normal.

Contraindications

  • Do not equate acidemia with a single metabolic diagnosis or alkalemia with a single respiratory diagnosis.

  • Do not treat a serum bicarbonate value in isolation without pH, PaCO2, chloride, anion gap, kidney function, and clinical context.

  • Do not suppress compensatory hyperventilation with sedatives unless airway and minute ventilation are deliberately supported.

Red Flags

  • pH below 7.20 or above 7.60 with clinical deterioration

  • New dysrhythmia or ECG change with severe potassium disturbance

  • Progressive confusion, seizure, coma, or loss of protective reflexes

  • Respiratory fatigue, falling ventilation, shock, or rapidly rising lactate

Patient Education

  • Explain that bicarbonate does not work alone: lungs regulate carbon dioxide within minutes, while kidneys replace bicarbonate and excrete acid over hours to days.

  • Teach that an abnormal bicarbonate value does not automatically mean bicarbonate medicine is needed, because treatment depends on the primary disorder, compensation, volume status, and cause.

Nclex Traps

  • Acidemia and alkalemia are measured states; acidosis and alkalosis are processes.

  • Normal pH does not exclude a mixed disorder.

  • Compensation limits pH change but does not cure the primary problem.

Name

Acid-base balance and the bicarbonate buffer

Category

Acid-base physiology

Definition

Acid-base balance is the coordinated control of hydrogen-ion activity that keeps extracellular pH compatible with protein function, membrane excitability, circulation, and metabolism. Acidemia means measured blood pH is below the reference interval, whereas alkalemia means it is above; acidosis and alkalosis describe processes pushing pH downward or upward and can coexist. The bicarbonate buffer links a metabolic base, HCO3, to a volatile acid component represented by dissolved carbon dioxide, so the lungs can change PaCO2 within minutes while the kidneys conserve or generate bicarbonate over hours to days.

Etiology

Normal metabolism continuously produces carbon dioxide and nonvolatile acids from sulfur-containing amino acids, phosphates, ketoacids, lactate, and other pathways. Ventilation removes carbon dioxide; extracellular and intracellular buffers limit immediate pH change; kidneys reclaim filtered bicarbonate and excrete net acid. Acid-base disorders appear when acid production, bicarbonate loss or gain, ventilation, kidney response, or several of these processes change beyond compensatory capacity.

Pathology

Hydrogen ions alter protein charge and shape, so severe pH disturbance changes enzyme activity, vascular tone, myocardial performance, catecholamine responsiveness, ion-channel behavior, and the binding of calcium and potassium. Acidemia can depress contractility and promote hyperkalemic electrical instability, while alkalemia increases albumin binding of calcium and can provoke paresthesias, tetany, seizures, and dysrhythmias. The measured pH can look normal when opposing disorders offset each other, so a normal number does not prove normal physiology.

Pathophysiology

The Henderson-Hasselbalch relationship is pH = 6.1 + log10[HCO3 / (0.03 x PaCO2)]. This ratio explains the direction of each primary disorder: lowering HCO3 or raising PaCO2 lowers pH, whereas raising HCO3 or lowering PaCO2 raises pH. Buffers reduce the first pH swing but do not remove acid from the body. Alveolar ventilation removes carbon dioxide, while renal ammonium and titratable-acid excretion remove nonvolatile acid and add new bicarbonate to blood; failure of either organ therefore changes the numerator-to-denominator ratio. Compensation changes the other side of the ratio but does not erase the initiating disease, and two primary processes can move both variables in directions that hide severity.
