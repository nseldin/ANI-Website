Definition

Empyema develops when infected pleural fluid becomes loculated and organized, preventing lung expansion and limiting antibiotic penetration. The characteristic pattern is persistent fever, pleuritic pain, toxicity, dyspnea, and a unilateral effusion that does not improve with pneumonia treatment. Confirm or stage it with chest ultrasound or CT to define free versus loculated fluid; distinguish it from uncomplicated parapneumonic effusion, malignancy, hemothorax, and chylothorax by pleural chemistry, microbiology, imaging, and clinical context. Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • chest ultrasound or CT to define free versus loculated fluid

  • diagnostic thoracentesis with pH, glucose, LDH, Gram stain, and cultures

  • serial imaging, inflammatory markers, drainage output, and lung expansion

  • Distinguish Empyema from uncomplicated parapneumonic effusion, malignancy, hemothorax, and chylothorax by pleural chemistry, microbiology, imaging, and clinical context.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • give pathogen-appropriate antibiotics

  • drain pus, positive-culture fluid, low-pH complicated fluid, or clinically significant loculations

  • use intrapleural therapy or surgery when drainage remains inadequate

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for increasing oxygen need, hypotension, persistent fever, rapidly falling drainage with ongoing collection, or new subcutaneous air because critical illness or tube obstruction requires urgent review.

  • Administer prescribed antibiotic therapy and obtain pleural fluid cultures when drained because infected pleural material requires both organism treatment and source control.

  • Prepare for image-guided drainage or chest-tube placement because loculated pus often cannot be cleared by systemic antibiotics alone.

  • Assess pleuritic pain, breath sounds, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, fever, and chest expansion because enlarging empyema restricts ventilation.

  • Monitor chest-tube patency, output, air leak, insertion site, imaging, white blood cell count, lactate, and temperature to detect inadequate drainage or sepsis.

Complications

  • sepsis can complicate Empyema.

  • bronchopleural fistula can complicate Empyema.

  • trapped lung can complicate Empyema.

  • fibrothorax can complicate Empyema.

  • procedure-related bleeding or pneumothorax

  • persistent abscess or chest-wall extension

Red Flags

  • Increasing oxygen requirement or respiratory distress

  • Hypotension, rising lactate, or other sepsis findings

  • Persistent collection despite chest-tube drainage

  • Abrupt tube-output change with worsening symptoms

Patient Education

  • Teach chest-tube safety, pulmonary hygiene, mobility, and completion of antibiotic therapy because drainage and lung re-expansion take time.

  • Tell the patient to report recurrent fever, worsening breathlessness, chest pain, or drainage-site redness promptly.

Nclex Traps

  • Antibiotics alone are often insufficient once infected pleural fluid is organized

  • A negative culture after antibiotics does not exclude empyema
