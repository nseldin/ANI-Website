Range

Mayo Clinic Laboratories lists an adult plasma lactate interval of 0.5-2.0 mmol/L. Venous and arterial intervals can differ by specimen, collection conditions, assay, and laboratory, so use the printed range and local sepsis or shock pathway rather than treating one lactate cutoff as universal.

Why

An elevated or rising lactate result signals that production is exceeding metabolism and clearance and raises concern for shock or impaired perfusion, but it is not specific for tissue hypoxia. Important causes include septic, hypovolemic, or cardiogenic shock, hypoxemia, seizure or intense muscle activity, liver dysfunction, thiamine deficiency, and selected drugs or toxins such as metformin in a susceptible patient. Promptly assess vital signs, perfusion, mental status, urine output, skin and capillary refill, oxygenation, work of breathing, infection clues, and medications; cause-directed evaluation can include repeat blood testing for the lactate trend, blood-gas and acid-base or organ-function testing, cultures, and imaging as ordered. Escalate immediately when a rising lactate accompanies shock, sepsis, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, or rapid deterioration while ordered resuscitation and evaluation proceed. One lactate value does not diagnose sepsis or prove inadequate oxygen delivery; interpret its trend after cause-directed care with the full clinical context and other causes of elevation.

High Causes

  • Sepsis or septic shock

  • Hypovolemic or cardiogenic shock

  • Hypoxemia or severe anemia

  • Seizure or intense muscle activity

  • Reduced clearance with liver dysfunction

  • Thiamine deficiency

  • Drug or toxin effect, including metformin in a susceptible patient

Low Causes

  • Low lactate is usually not the safety problem.

Critical Concerns

  • A rising lactate with shock, sepsis, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, or rapid deterioration requires prompt escalation.

  • Normal blood pressure does not exclude impaired perfusion, but lactate elevation is not specific for hypoperfusion.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess hemodynamics, mental status, urine output, skin and capillary refill, oxygenation, respiratory effort, infection clues, liver function, and relevant medications or toxins.

  • Trend lactate after cause-directed treatment according to the ordered pathway and interpret the direction with the patient's perfusion rather than targeting the number in isolation.

  • Anticipate cause-directed blood tests, blood gas or acid-base evaluation, cultures, and imaging as ordered.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not wait for hypotension when a rising lactate accompanies other deterioration, but do not diagnose sepsis from lactate alone.

  • A falling lactate can support improvement; it does not prove that shock or the underlying cause has resolved.
