Definition

Candida auris is a yeast that can colonize the skin and cause invasive infection, especially in patients with substantial healthcare exposure or breached protective barriers. It is a high-consequence healthcare organism because it can spread between patients, persist in care environments, be difficult for some laboratory systems to identify, and show resistance to one or more antifungal classes. Colonization is not the same as infection and should not be interpreted as candidemia.

    Definition

  • Content

    C. auris is an abbreviation for the species Candida auris. Candida is a genus, not an exact alias for this organism, and candidiasis describes disease rather than organism identity. C. auris differs clinically from Candida albicans because healthcare transmission and environmental persistence are especially important, so a generic Candida label is not adequate for species-level infection-control or susceptibility decisions.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    C. auris is a cellular, nonmotile yeast with a fungal cell wall. This bounded evidence packet supports the controlled yeast morphology but does not assign a species-wide oxygen-growth category, and the draft does not infer one from another Candida species. Surface adherence and biofilm-associated persistence can complicate care around invasive devices, but morphology alone cannot confirm species identity or predict antifungal susceptibility.

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Colonized or infected patients are the important healthcare reservoir. The organism can remain on skin and contaminate nearby surfaces or reusable equipment, allowing contact spread through hands, equipment, and the care environment when infection-control steps fail. Patients with long healthcare stays, invasive devices, wounds, mechanical ventilation, or extensive antimicrobial exposure may be at increased risk. Colonization can persist for an indeterminate period, so one exposure-to-infection interval is not clinically meaningful.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    C. auris may remain as asymptomatic skin colonization or enter normally sterile sites when a vascular catheter, wound, surgery, critical illness, or impaired host defense disrupts barriers. Adherence and biofilm formation can support persistence on skin, equipment, and implanted material. Once bloodstream or deep infection develops, clinical deterioration reflects invasive candidiasis and host response rather than a named exotoxin syndrome.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    C. auris can cause bloodstream, wound, ear, and other invasive infections, while many identified patients are colonized without symptoms. Fever, chills, hypotension, altered mental status, increasing oxygen need, reduced urine output, or other sepsis findings in a high-risk patient require prompt escalation; none is specific to C. auris. A positive colonization screen does not diagnose infection, and a positive culture from a sterile site must be communicated promptly for clinical, infection-prevention, and laboratory follow-up.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Blood culture can recover C. auris during candidemia, but species confirmation requires a validated laboratory identification method because some conventional systems may misidentify the organism. The laboratory should be alerted when C. auris is suspected, preserve and route the isolate according to current policy, and perform or obtain antifungal susceptibility testing as indicated. A skin or other screening swab documents colonization status for infection control; it does not by itself establish invasive disease or authorize antifungal treatment.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Use the setting-specific precautions recommended by current CDC, public-health, and facility policy. Hand hygiene, correct gown-and-glove use when indicated, dedicated or thoroughly disinfected shared equipment, effective environmental cleaning, and clear interfacility communication are central nursing actions. Do not treat colonization as infection. Management of clinical infection requires species confirmation, susceptibility context, source assessment, and specialist or public-health coordination; worsening sepsis should never wait for final species confirmation.
