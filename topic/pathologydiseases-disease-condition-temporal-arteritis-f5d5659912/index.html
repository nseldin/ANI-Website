Definition

Temporal arteritis develops when granulomatous inflammation of medium and large arteries, especially cranial branches, narrows the lumen and threatens ocular and cerebral blood flow. The characteristic pattern is new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw or tongue claudication, visual symptoms, polymyalgia symptoms, and elevated inflammatory markers in an adult over fifty. Confirm or stage it with immediate ESR, CRP, CBC, platelet, and ophthalmic assessment; distinguish it from migraine, tension headache, dental disease, nonarteritic optic neuropathy, intracranial disease, and other vasculitis by age, ischemic symptoms, imaging, and tissue. The priority is to start high-dose glucocorticoid immediately when vision is threatened or suspicion is high because irreversible blindness can complicate Temporal arteritis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • immediate ESR, CRP, CBC, platelet, and ophthalmic assessment

  • temporal-artery ultrasound and biopsy according to local pathway

  • large-vessel imaging when aortic or branch involvement is suspected

  • Distinguish Temporal arteritis from migraine, tension headache, dental disease, nonarteritic optic neuropathy, intracranial disease, and other vasculitis by age, ischemic symptoms, imaging, and tissue.

Labs

  • WBC may rise or fall

  • lactate may rise with sepsis

  • CRP/ESR may rise with inflammation

  • organ labs can worsen in severe infection

Treatments

  • start high-dose glucocorticoid immediately when vision is threatened or suspicion is high

  • add steroid-sparing therapy when indicated and protect bone and infection risk

  • monitor the aorta and relapse symptoms

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for transient or permanent vision change, diplopia, jaw or tongue ischemia, stroke symptoms, syncope, limb ischemia, or sudden chest, back, or abdominal pain because ocular artery occlusion, cerebral ischemia, aortic aneurysm, or dissection requires emergency care.

  • Assess new headache, scalp tenderness, temporal-artery change, jaw or tongue claudication, transient or persistent visual symptoms, constitutional features, polymyalgia symptoms, pulses, bruits, blood pressure, and focal neurologic findings because giant cell arteritis can obstruct ocular, cerebral, and large-vessel blood flow.

  • Notify the prescriber immediately and administer ordered high-dose glucocorticoid without waiting for biopsy when GCA is strongly suspected, especially with visual symptoms, because treatment delay can allow sudden irreversible blindness in one or both eyes.

  • Trend ESR, CRP, complete blood count, symptoms, vision, glucose, blood pressure, infection, and bone protection and coordinate temporal-artery ultrasound or biopsy and large-vessel imaging as ordered because no single blood test proves the diagnosis and therapy creates substantial toxicity.

  • Arrange urgent ophthalmology for any visual symptom and monitor the unaffected eye, cranial nerves, and neurologic status because ocular ischemia may fluctuate briefly before permanent loss and can affect the second eye quickly.

Complications

  • irreversible blindness can complicate Temporal arteritis.

  • stroke can complicate Temporal arteritis.

  • aortic aneurysm or dissection

  • glucocorticoid complications can complicate Temporal arteritis.

Red Flags

  • Transient or persistent vision loss, diplopia, blurred vision, or visual-field defect

  • Jaw or tongue claudication, tongue color change, or severe new headache

  • Facial droop, weakness, speech change, seizure, syncope, or other cerebral ischemia

  • Sudden chest, back, or abdominal pain, pulse deficit, or acute limb ischemia

Patient Education

  • Report a new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw fatigue with chewing, or any vision change immediately because treatment is started on suspicion to prevent blindness rather than waiting for every test.

  • Take glucocorticoids exactly as prescribed and keep glucose, blood-pressure, infection, eye, and bone monitoring because abrupt withdrawal risks relapse while long treatment has preventable complications.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not delay treatment for biopsy when visual loss is possible

  • A normal inflammatory marker lowers probability but does not absolutely exclude a compelling ischemic presentation
