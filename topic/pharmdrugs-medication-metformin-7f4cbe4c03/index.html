Mechanism

Metformin reaches hepatocytes through organic cation transporters and alters mitochondrial energy and redox signaling. Proposed, partly overlapping mechanisms include inhibition of respiratory-chain complex I, changes in mitochondrial glycerophosphate handling, increased AMP relative to ATP, AMPK activation, reduced glucagon-cAMP signaling, and gut-mediated effects. The complete molecular mechanism remains unsettled, but the physiologic endpoint is clear: hepatic gluconeogenesis falls, insulin sensitivity improves, and intestinal glucose handling changes. Because metformin does not close beta-cell KATP channels or supply insulin, it does not independently drive glucose below the available fuel state. It is eliminated unchanged by the kidneys, so declining filtration and tubular secretion prolong exposure.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING: Postmarketing metformin-associated lactic acidosis has caused death, hypothermia, hypotension, and resistant bradyarrhythmias. Risk rises with eGFR below safe thresholds, acute kidney injury, age 65 or older, iodinated contrast in specified high-risk settings, surgery/prolonged fasting, acute heart failure or other hypoxic states, excessive alcohol, hepatic impairment, interacting carbonic-anhydrase inhibitors, and mitochondrial disorders such as MELAS or MIDD. Stop metformin and obtain urgent hospital evaluation for unexplained malaise, myalgia, somnolence, abdominal symptoms, respiratory distress, hypothermia, or anion-gap acidosis; prompt hemodialysis is recommended when toxicity is suspected.

Contraindications

  • Severe renal impairment with eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2

  • Acute or chronic metabolic acidosis, including DKA

  • Hold and reassess during severe hypoxia, shock, sepsis, major dehydration, acute kidney injury, or label-defined contrast/procedure situations

Nursing Essentials

  • Check eGFR before initiation and at least annually, more often when renal function may change. Metformin is not intrinsically nephrotoxic; renal decline matters because clearance falls.

  • Trend vitamin B12 periodically and when anemia or neuropathy appears. Do not attribute new numbness automatically to diabetic neuropathy when a reversible B12 deficiency is possible.

Interactions

  • Topiramate, acetazolamide, and other carbonic-anhydrase inhibitors can promote acidosis and increase lactic-acidosis risk.

  • Iodinated contrast may precipitate acute kidney injury in susceptible patients; hold/restart rules depend on eGFR, hepatic/heart-failure risk, and intra-arterial versus venous exposure.

  • Alcohol impairs hepatic lactate utilization and adds lactic-acidosis and hypoglycemia risk during poor intake.

Key Labs

  • eGFR/creatinine before and during treatment

  • A1c and fasting glucose response

  • Vitamin B12 and CBC; bicarbonate, anion gap, lactate, pH, renal/hepatic function when toxicity is suspected

Nclex Traps

  • Metformin itself is not a contrast allergy and is not held because it damages kidneys; the concern is accumulation if AKI follows the procedure.

  • A normal or mildly elevated glucose does not exclude lactic acidosis.

  • GI upset is common and dose-related, but tachypnea, hypothermia, profound weakness, or anion-gap acidosis is not routine intolerance.

Used To Treat

Adjunct to nutrition and activity for type 2 diabetes in adults and, for immediate-release labeling, children 10 years and older. It is also used off-label in selected insulin-resistance conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome. It does not replace insulin in type 1 diabetes and does not treat diabetic ketoacidosis.

Description

Metformin is a biguanide that lowers glucose mainly by suppressing inappropriate liver glucose output between meals and overnight. It also improves insulin-mediated glucose use and changes gut-liver metabolism without directly forcing pancreatic beta cells to release insulin. That is why metformin alone rarely causes hypoglycemia and is usually weight-neutral or modestly weight-lowering. Its defining danger is not kidney damage caused by the drug; it is metformin accumulation during severe renal impairment or hypoxic/metabolic stress, which can contribute to life-threatening lactic acidosis.

Adverse Effects

  • Diarrhea, nausea, abdominal discomfort, flatulence, and metallic taste, often reduced by slow titration and administration with meals

  • Vitamin B12 malabsorption with possible macrocytosis, neuropathy, cognitive change, or glossitis during long-term therapy

  • Rare metformin-associated lactic acidosis when accumulation and impaired lactate handling converge
