Used To Treat

Recent external chemical contamination or selected ingestions in which safe removal of unabsorbed material can reduce ongoing exposure. Decontamination is a time-sensitive exposure-control procedure, not a universal antidote and not a substitute for resuscitation or toxin-specific therapy.

Description

Decontamination removes unabsorbed poison from clothing, skin, hair, eyes, or the gastrointestinal tract, reducing ongoing exposure while protecting patients and responders from secondary contamination. For external contamination, responder PPE, scene safety, clothing removal, blotting or brushing visible material, and gentle water plus mild soap usually remove most surface burden; CDC notes that clothing removal alone can remove much contamination. For ingestions, no one procedure fits every poison. Activated charcoal adsorbs selected molecules but not caustics, alcohols, lithium, iron, and many other ions, and aspiration can be worse than the ingestion. Whole-bowel irrigation is reserved for selected sustained-release or enteric-coated drugs, body packers, or substances poorly bound by charcoal when bowel and airway conditions are safe. Syrup of ipecac, routine induced vomiting, routine gastric lavage, and chemical neutralization are not modern home treatments.

Mechanism

Clothing removal and washing reduce the external dose available for skin absorption and prevent secondary contamination of staff and family. Blotting or brushing avoids spreading liquid or dry material across more skin; copious irrigation creates dilution and physical removal, while mild soap lifts adherent oily chemicals. Activated charcoal provides a large porous adsorptive surface that traps compatible molecules in the gut. Polyethylene-glycol electrolyte whole-bowel irrigation moves intact intraluminal material to stool. None of these methods removes toxin already distributed into tissue, and each can create harm when performed through an unprotected airway, injured esophagus, obstructed bowel, or chemically incompatible route.

Administration Timing

  • For collapse, breathing difficulty, seizure, severe burns, or major exposure, call 911; otherwise call Poison Help immediately for product-specific instructions. Keep rescuers out of the hazard, use PPE, move to fresh air when safe, and remove contaminated clothing without pulling it over the face when cutting is safer.

  • For most skin and hair contamination, blot rather than rub, then wash gently with lukewarm water and mild soap; irrigate symptomatic eyes with plain water while removing easy contact lenses. Brush off dry powders first when water could spread them, and follow incident-specific guidance for water-reactive materials.

  • For ingestion, do not induce vomiting or give food, drink, charcoal, ipecac, neutralizer, or lavage unless Poison Help or a clinician directs it. In hospital, select charcoal, whole-bowel irrigation, gastric aspiration, endoscopy, or no GI decontamination by substance, formulation, time, amount, airway, bowel function, and evidence.

Nursing Essentials

  • Protect staff and prevent the emergency department from becoming contaminated: establish hot, warm, and clean zones when relevant; bag clothing and waste as directed; and notify hazardous-material teams for volatile, pesticide, or warfare-agent exposure.

  • Record product name, concentration, safety-data label, route, time, body surface, wounds, eye symptoms, decontamination start and finish, and every GI procedure. Continue airway, breathing, circulation, temperature, and toxidrome care during decontamination.

  • Before activated charcoal or whole-bowel irrigation, document mental status and airway protection, aspiration risk, vomiting, GI bleeding, ileus, obstruction or perforation risk, and whether the substance is actually adsorbed or radiopaque. Stop when procedural risk exceeds remaining absorbable burden.

Key Labs

  • No laboratory result should delay removal from ongoing external exposure. Use toxin-directed ECG, glucose, blood gas, electrolytes, renal and hepatic studies, co-oximetry, cholinesterase, metal or drug concentrations, and imaging as the product requires.

  • Abdominal imaging can support selected iron-tablet, body-packer, or sustained-release decisions but a negative image does not prove an empty GI tract. Decontamination success is not measured by charcoal-colored stool alone.

Adverse Effects

  • External decontamination can cause hypothermia, skin injury from scrubbing, chemical spread, privacy trauma, and responder contamination. Eye delay can worsen corneal injury.

  • Activated charcoal can cause vomiting, aspiration pneumonitis, obstruction, and electrolyte problems when combined with cathartics. Whole-bowel irrigation can cause vomiting, aspiration, distention, pain, ileus, obstruction, and poor tolerance. Lavage can cause aspiration, perforation, dysrhythmia, and hazardous staff exposure.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce emesis or use syrup of ipecac. Do not neutralize acids with bases or bases with acids because exothermic reactions can worsen burns. Do not give charcoal for caustic injury when it obscures endoscopy without binding benefit, or for a hydrocarbon aspiration risk without expert rationale.

  • An unprotected airway, uncontrolled vomiting, bowel obstruction, perforation, ileus, severe GI hemorrhage, or instability can contraindicate oral decontamination. Water-reactive chemicals require substance-specific guidance rather than automatic irrigation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Airway swelling, bronchospasm, hypoxia, severe eye pain or vision loss, progressive skin burn, shock, seizure, secondary contamination, packet rupture, bowel obstruction, or aspiration requires emergency specialty escalation. Symptoms after apparently complete washing indicate absorbed toxin or tissue injury and require cause-specific treatment.

Interactions

  • Activated charcoal can adsorb oral antidotes and routine medicines, so timing and route must be coordinated. Cathartics add fluid and electrolyte losses and are not routine companions to charcoal.

  • Some pesticides, hydrofluoric acid, phenol, elemental metals, and warfare agents need substance-specific agents or techniques after gross decontamination; generic soap-and-water language is not the whole protocol.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Larger surface-area-to-mass ratio, thinner skin, fear, aspiration risk, and rapid hypothermia require warm water, privacy, family support when safe, and close temperature and airway monitoring.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Prompt maternal decontamination reduces ongoing dose; do not delay airway and circulation support or indicated antidotes while seeking obstetric input.

  • Population

    Responders and healthcare staff

    Note

    PPE, ventilation, runoff control, and contaminated-waste handling prevent secondary casualties and preserve the care team.

Evidence Limitations

  • GI-decontamination outcome evidence is limited and strongly affected by timing and selection. The 2026 charcoal statement is current, while the AACT/EAPCCT whole-bowel-irrigation position paper is older and does not support routine use. CDC public chemical guidance is general and must yield to substance-specific hazmat or toxicology instructions.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not make a poisoned patient vomit or use syrup of ipecac because induced emesis can cause aspiration and has not shown meaningful poisoning-outcome benefit.

  • Activated charcoal does not bind every toxin and can be more dangerous than helpful when aspiration risk is high.

  • Decontamination removes external or intraluminal toxin; it does not reverse toxin already absorbed.

Regulatory Status

This is a procedure-selection pathway, not an FDA-approved antidote. Activated-charcoal products and PEG-electrolyte solutions have product labeling, but overdose decontamination uses are poison-center and protocol directed; ipecac and routine emesis are not recommended rescue strategies.

Boxed Warning

DECONTAMINATION WARNING: protect rescuers, call 911 for severe symptoms, and call Poison Help before giving anything by mouth. Remove contaminated clothing and wash external exposure promptly, but never induce vomiting, use ipecac, neutralize a caustic, or give charcoal through an unprotected airway; the wrong procedure can cause aspiration, perforation, or a second casualty.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
