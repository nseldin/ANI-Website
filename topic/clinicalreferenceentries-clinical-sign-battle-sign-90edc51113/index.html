Definition

Battle sign is mastoid ecchymosis (bruising over the bony area just behind the ear) that develops after head trauma. It is a clinician-observed sign, not a symptom reported by the patient.

Plain Language

Battle sign is bruising behind one or both ears that appears after a head injury.

Why It Matters

It is important because it can signal a severe head injury, especially a fracture at the base of the skull. The bruise may not appear for 1 to 3 days, so a normal early examination does not rule out a fracture. It requires urgent trauma evaluation and head imaging, although the sign does not prove the fracture by itself.

Associated Conditions

  • basilar skull fracture, especially a lateral or middle skull-base or temporal-bone fracture

Assessment Method

After head trauma, inspect the skin over both mastoid processes (the bony areas just behind the ears) and repeat the assessment during observation because bruising can be delayed.

Positive Finding

New bruising appears over the bony area behind one or both ears, often 1 to 3 days after injury.

Mechanism

A skull-base or temporal-bone fracture allows blood to escape and pool in subcutaneous tissue over the mastoid, so visible discoloration may take time to develop.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Battle sign finding raises concern for basilar skull fracture, especially a lateral or middle skull-base or temporal-bone fracture because a skull-base or temporal-bone fracture allows blood to escape and pool in subcutaneous tissue over the mastoid, so visible discoloration may take time to develop. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Battle sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Battle sign to focus evaluation of basilar skull fracture, especially a lateral or middle skull-base or temporal-bone fracture and the reviewed alternatives: direct mastoid trauma, ear surgery, dependent bruising, coagulopathy. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: After head trauma, inspect the skin over both mastoid processes (the bony areas just behind the ears) and repeat the assessment during observation because bruising can be delayed. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Battle sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

It is frequently absent on the initial examination, absence does not exclude fracture, and local trauma, surgery, or a bleeding tendency can mimic it; imaging defines the injury. Therefore, Battle sign cannot confirm or exclude basilar skull fracture, especially a lateral or middle skull-base or temporal-bone fracture by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as direct mastoid trauma, ear surgery, dependent bruising, coagulopathy.

False Negative Situations

Battle sign may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of basilar skull fracture, especially a lateral or middle skull-base or temporal-bone fracture.

Observe Battle sign with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: After head trauma, inspect the skin over both mastoid processes (the bony areas just behind the ears) and repeat the assessment during observation because bruising can be delayed.
