Contraindications

  • Serious prior allopurinol hypersensitivity; a positive HLA-B*58:01 result generally makes use inadvisable unless exceptional benefit outweighs risk.

  • Clarify ancestry/genetic risk, CKD, liver disease, rash history, thiazides, ampicillin/amoxicillin, azathioprine/mercaptopurine, cancer therapy, pregnancy, and ability to complete titration and monitoring.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain serum urate, CBC, renal and liver tests before treatment; start low, titrate to target rather than a fixed renal ceiling, and provide indicated anti-inflammatory flare prophylaxis.

  • Teach that an early flare does not mean failure or allergy: continue urate-lowering therapy and treat the flare, but stop immediately for rash or systemic hypersensitivity signs.

Interactions

  • Allopurinol blocks metabolism of azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine, so unadjusted coadministration can cause life-threatening marrow suppression; specialist dose reduction and CBC monitoring are essential.

  • Thiazides and renal impairment can increase hypersensitivity risk; ampicillin/amoxicillin increase rash frequency, warfarin effect may change, and didanosine exposure rises.

Key Labs

  • Serum urate with a usual gout target below 6 mg/dL, CBC, creatinine/eGFR, AST/ALT, alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin; HLA-B*58:01 when indicated

  • Rash/mucosal and systemic hypersensitivity assessment, flare frequency, tophus burden, stone symptoms, adherence, and interacting medicines

Nclex Traps

  • Allopurinol prevents future crystal disease by lowering urate; it is not the rapid pain-relief drug for today's flare.

  • Do not stop established allopurinol during a flare. Stopping makes urate fluctuate and undermines crystal dissolution; treat the inflammation separately.

Used To Treat

Chronic urate lowering in patients with gout, prevention or management of hyperuricemia associated with cancer therapy, and selected recurrent calcium oxalate stones in hyperuricosuric patients. It is not indicated simply because an asymptomatic uric-acid value is mildly high, and it is not the immediate analgesic treatment for a flare.

Mechanism

Purine catabolism converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and then xanthine to uric acid through xanthine oxidoreductase, usually called xanthine oxidase clinically. Allopurinol resembles hypoxanthine and is converted by the enzyme to oxypurinol; both inhibit xanthine oxidase, with oxypurinol providing prolonged inhibition because renal elimination is slow. Hypoxanthine and xanthine are more soluble than uric acid and can be salvaged back into nucleotides, so net urate production falls. Sustained serum urate below its saturation threshold creates a concentration gradient out of tissue deposits. Kidney function determines oxypurinol exposure, but renal disease is a reason for low starting dose and careful titration, not automatic denial of treat-to-target therapy. The HLA-B*58:01 allele strongly raises the risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome, SJS/TEN, and DRESS in genetically at-risk populations.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Allopurinol can cause severe and sometimes fatal hypersensitivity, including DRESS, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, vasculitis, hepatitis, and renal injury. Stop immediately at the first rash, blister, mucosal lesion, facial swelling, fever, eosinophilia, or systemic symptom; do not casually rechallenge. Consider HLA-B*58:01 testing before initiation in genetically high-risk populations. Start lower with kidney impairment and titrate by serum urate. Monitor liver, kidney, and blood counts. Xanthine-oxidase inhibition dangerously increases azathioprine and mercaptopurine exposure unless those doses are drastically reduced under specialist management.

Description

Allopurinol is the preferred first-line long-term urate-lowering drug for most patients with gout, including many with chronic kidney disease. It inhibits xanthine oxidase, the enzyme that converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Its active metabolite oxypurinol persists much longer and supplies most of the sustained effect. Lower serum urate allows existing monosodium urate crystals to dissolve gradually, shrinking the tissue reservoir that causes flares, tophi, and urate injury. The paradox is that beginning therapy can temporarily provoke a flare: as serum urate falls, the surface of old deposits remodels and sheds inflammatory microcrystals. That is why allopurinol is started low, titrated to a serum-urate target, paired with temporary flare prophylaxis when appropriate, and continued during an acute flare rather than stopped.

Adverse Effects

  • Early gout flares, nausea, diarrhea, drowsiness, and mild rash or liver-enzyme elevation

  • Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome, DRESS, SJS/TEN, anaphylaxis, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, myelosuppression, vasculitis, and severe azathioprine/mercaptopurine toxicity
