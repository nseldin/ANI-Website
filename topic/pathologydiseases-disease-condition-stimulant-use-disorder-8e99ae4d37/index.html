Plain Language

Stimulant use disorder is a treatable illness in which cocaine, methamphetamine, or another stimulant is repeatedly used in a way that becomes hard to control and causes harm or major risk. It is more than one episode of intoxication, a prescribed stimulant, or a positive drug test.

Why It Matters

Stimulants can trigger heart attack, stroke, dangerous overheating, abnormal rhythms, seizures, psychosis, severe agitation, and suicide risk. There is no FDA-approved medication for stimulant use disorder, but contingency management and other behavioral treatments work, and acute toxicity needs rapid, calm medical care.

Definition

Stimulant use disorder is a problematic pattern of amphetamine-type, cocaine, or other stimulant use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR diagnosis requires at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months and records the stimulant class and severity. Prescribed use, intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis, or toxicology alone does not establish the disorder.

Pathology

No laboratory, ECG, or brain image confirms stimulant use disorder. Repeated exposure can alter reward, attention, stress, sleep, and cue-response systems; acute injury reflects sympathetic overactivation, vasoconstriction, hyperthermia, agitation, dehydration, prolonged exertion, adulterants, and co-exposures.

Pathophysiology

  • Stimulants raise dopamine and other monoamine signaling through mechanisms that vary by drug. Reinforcement, craving, tolerance, and loss of control can develop while sleep deprivation and repeated binges amplify paranoia, impulsivity, and medical risk.

  • Acute intoxication may cause euphoria or anxiety, alertness, enlarged pupils, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, chest pain, agitation, repetitive behavior, or psychosis. Severe toxicity can progress to arrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, stroke, hyperthermia, seizure, rhabdomyolysis, and organ failure.

  • Withdrawal often produces fatigue, increased sleep or insomnia, vivid dreams, increased appetite, slowed or agitated movement, craving, and depressed mood. It is not usually a seizure-delirium withdrawal syndrome, but severe depression, suicidality, psychosis, dehydration, and comorbidity can require urgent care.

Etiology

Risk reflects stimulant availability and potency, rapid routes of use, neurobiologic vulnerability, trauma, untreated ADHD or other psychiatric illness, sleep disruption, pain or fatigue demands, sexual and social context, unstable housing, criminalization, and treatment barriers. Prescribed stimulant treatment for a supported diagnosis does not by itself cause or prove stimulant use disorder.

Risk Factors

  • Prior stimulant use disorder, escalating frequency or route, binge patterns, injection or smoking, fentanyl-contaminated supply, or prior stimulant toxicity

  • Sleep deprivation, dehydration, hot environments, prolonged exertion, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, pregnancy, and concurrent serotonergic or sympathomimetic substances

  • Depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, ADHD, trauma, suicide risk, unstable housing, and limited access to treatment

  • Transition from a controlled setting or reduced use followed by return to an unpredictable supply

Signs Symptoms

  • Impaired control, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, substantial time spent using or recovering, role failure, risky use, and continued use despite medical, psychiatric, relationship, or occupational harm

  • Intoxication: alertness or euphoria, anxiety, irritability, sweating, enlarged pupils, fast pulse, high blood pressure, reduced appetite, repetitive behavior, agitation, suspiciousness, or hallucinations

  • Severe toxicity: chest pain, severe headache, hyperthermia, arrhythmia, seizure, focal deficit, delirium, extreme agitation, muscle breakdown, or collapse

  • Withdrawal: crash, fatigue, sleep change, increased appetite, vivid dreams, slowed or restless movement, craving, depression, anhedonia, or suicidal thinking

Diagnostics

  • Ask about the exact stimulant and formulation, route, amount, last use, binge and sleep pattern, prescribed access, fentanyl or other adulterant exposure, co-use, prior toxicity, psychosis or mood symptoms, overdose, pregnancy, and each use-disorder feature over the same 12 months.

  • Confirm a clinically significant pattern with at least 2 DSM-5-TR features; identify cocaine, amphetamine-type, or other stimulant rather than assuming identical risk or treatment evidence across all stimulants.

  • Determine whether psychosis, mania, depression, suicidality, or cognitive change occurs only during intoxication or withdrawal, persists beyond the expected course, or preceded stimulant use. Collateral history and longitudinal observation may be necessary.

  • Use toxicology only for a defined clinical purpose. Routine panels may miss specific synthetic stimulants, prescribed medicines can explain results, fentanyl needs appropriate testing, and a result does not measure severity or prove the diagnosis.

Labs

  • No test diagnoses stimulant use disorder. In acute toxicity, select ECG and continuous cardiac monitoring, glucose, electrolytes, renal function, creatine kinase, troponin, blood gas, temperature, urinalysis, pregnancy testing, and imaging from symptoms and protocol.

  • Assess hydration, muscle pain and urine output because hyperthermia, prolonged agitation, restraint, or exertion can cause rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury.

  • Offer HIV, hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infection testing based on injection and sexual exposure, with consent and prevention linkage.

Assessment

Stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, cardiac rhythm, seizure, chest pain, stroke symptoms, trauma, and severe agitation first. Use a low-stimulation setting and de-escalation while obtaining exact substance, route, timing, co-exposures, sleep, hydration, medical history, pregnancy, psychosis, and suicide risk. After stabilization, assess diagnostic criteria, treatment preference, prior response, housing, and follow-up access.

Differential

Differentiate intoxication from panic, mania, primary psychosis, serotonin toxicity, anticholinergic poisoning, thyroid storm, sepsis, hypoglycemia, head injury, and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives. Differentiate withdrawal-related depression or psychosis from a primary mood or psychotic disorder using onset, prior history, persistence, and sober observation; the conditions can coexist.

Treatments

  • Use contingency management as a primary component of treatment when available. Cognitive behavioral therapy, community reinforcement, and the Matrix Model are evidence-supported behavioral approaches that can be combined with contingency management and practical recovery support.

  • No medication is FDA approved specifically for stimulant use disorder. An addiction specialist may consider off-label bupropion plus naltrexone for selected amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder, or other condition-specific options, only after substance subtype, concurrent opioids, seizure and eating-disorder risk, organ function, pregnancy, interactions, and patient goals are reviewed.

  • For acute hyperadrenergic toxicity or severe agitation, use monitored emergency care; benzodiazepines are representative first-line medicines in the ASAM/AAAP guideline, with further blood-pressure, cardiac, psychosis, temperature, and seizure management directed by the presentation and protocol.

  • Treat co-occurring depression, psychosis, ADHD, trauma, sleep problems, infections, wounds, nutrition, housing, and sexual health without requiring abstinence as a precondition for basic care.

Contraindications

  • Do not use a beta blocker or any sedating, antipsychotic, or blood-pressure medicine reflexively without considering the specific toxidrome, co-exposures, temperature, ECG, and current emergency guidance.

  • Bupropion is contraindicated with seizure disorder, current or prior anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa, and abrupt discontinuation of alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, or antiepileptic drugs; also review monoamine oxidase inhibitor timing and other label risks.

  • Naltrexone must not be started during current opioid use, opioid dependence, or acute opioid withdrawal; an opioid-free transition and product-specific liver assessment are required.

  • Do not interpret sleep, food, observation, or a short withdrawal stay as complete stimulant-use-disorder treatment.

Safety Considerations

  • Treat hyperthermia, chest pain, severe hypertension, arrhythmia, seizure, stroke signs, delirium, and dangerous agitation as time-sensitive emergencies; avoid prolonged struggling or unnecessary stimulation.

  • Assess suicide risk repeatedly during the crash and early abstinence because mood can worsen as intoxication resolves. Persistent psychosis, severe depression, or inability to care for self needs a higher level of care.

  • Discuss fentanyl contamination, naloxone, not using alone, sterile supplies, hydration without forced overhydration, cooling, sleep, and early help for chest pain or overheating.

  • Medication evidence varies by stimulant class and use pattern. Document when a treatment is off-label and monitor the condition it is intended to change.

Nursing Priorities

  • Reduce stimulation, use verbal de-escalation, obtain vital signs and temperature, place on cardiac monitoring when indicated, assess glucose and neurologic status, and escalate chest pain, seizure, hyperthermia, stroke signs, or severe agitation.

  • Document exact drug, route, timing, co-use, sleep, food and fluid intake, prescribed medications, toxicology limitations, mental status, psychosis content, suicide risk, and response to treatment.

  • Trend temperature, rhythm, blood pressure, pain, urine output, creatine kinase and renal measures when indicated, hydration, and mental status rather than relying on one apparently calm interval.

  • Before discharge, connect the patient to contingency-management-capable care when available, harm-reduction supplies, naloxone because contamination is possible, psychiatric follow-up, and a specific crisis plan.

Red Flags

  • Chest pain, severe headache, focal weakness, seizure, syncope, dangerous arrhythmia, severe hypertension, or signs of aortic or cardiac emergency

  • Hyperthermia, rigidity, severe agitation, prolonged struggle, dark urine, muscle pain, oliguria, or worsening acidosis

  • Command hallucinations, violent or suicidal intent, delirium, inability to care for self, or psychosis that persists beyond the expected substance course

  • Pregnancy with toxicity, bleeding, labor symptoms, reduced fetal movement, or inability to maintain safety; suspected mixed exposure or fentanyl contamination

Complications

  • Myocardial ischemia, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, aortic emergency, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, seizure, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and acute kidney injury

  • Psychosis, depression, suicide, trauma, violence exposure, sleep deprivation, cognitive impairment, malnutrition, dental disease, and skin injury

  • HIV, viral hepatitis, bacterial infection, sexually transmitted infection, and pregnancy complications related to exposure patterns and barriers to care

  • Loss of housing, employment, finances, custody, relationships, and treatment engagement, compounded by stigma and criminalization

Prognosis

Stimulant use disorder can recur, but sustained improvement is possible. Contingency management has the strongest current treatment evidence, and outcomes improve when co-occurring psychiatric, medical, housing, and safety needs are treated. Persistent psychosis or cognitive symptoms need reassessment rather than an assumption that every symptom will resolve immediately.

Prevention

  • Use nonpunitive screening, early brief intervention, and secure, indication-based prescribing and monitoring for prescribed stimulants without stigmatizing appropriate treatment.

  • Provide fentanyl-test and naloxone education where permitted, safer-use supplies, infection prevention, cooling and hydration education, and rapid access to treatment.

  • Treat ADHD, mood, trauma, sleep, pain, and social instability with evidence-based care so stimulant use is not the only available coping strategy.

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for chest pain, seizure, severe headache, one-sided weakness, overheating, collapse, or dangerous confusion.

  • The crash can bring severe depression or suicidal thoughts; tell someone and get urgent help rather than trying to sleep through unsafe symptoms alone.

  • Contingency management is a real evidence-based treatment. There is no FDA-approved stimulant-use-disorder medicine, so any proposed medication should have a clear off-label reason and safety plan.

  • Illicit stimulants may contain fentanyl. Carry naloxone, avoid using alone, and do not assume a familiar-looking supply is predictable.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and postpartum care should use coordinated obstetric, addiction, and psychiatric assessment; do not extrapolate off-label medication evidence without a patient-specific risk-benefit review.

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate behavioral care, confidentiality and family involvement when safe, school support, and assessment for exploitation, trauma, ADHD, depression, and suicide risk.

  • Older adults and patients with cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, renal, or psychiatric disease have higher toxicity and interaction risk and may present atypically.

  • People who use stimulants in sexual contexts need nonjudgmental consent, assault, HIV and STI, hydration, sleep, and medication-interaction assessment.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not wait for a toxicology result before treating a clinical stimulant emergency.

  • Stimulant withdrawal is not usually a seizure-delirium syndrome, but depression and suicide risk can be life-threatening.

  • Contingency management is first-line evidence-based care; food and sleep alone are not complete treatment.

  • No medication is FDA approved for stimulant use disorder; bupropion plus naltrexone is a selected off-label specialist option, not a universal regimen.
