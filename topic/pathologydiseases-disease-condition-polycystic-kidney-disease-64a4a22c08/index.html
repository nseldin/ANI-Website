Definition

Polycystic kidney disease develops when inherited tubular signaling defects produce enlarging fluid-filled cysts that distort renal tissue and may affect vessels, liver, and cardiac valves. The characteristic pattern is family history, hypertension, flank pain, hematuria, recurrent infection or stones, and bilateral enlarged cystic kidneys. Confirm or stage it with age- and genotype-aware renal ultrasonography or MRI; distinguish it from simple cysts, acquired cystic disease, other inherited ciliopathies, and obstructive cystic change by distribution, family history, and extrarenal findings. The priority is to control blood pressure and cardiovascular risk because progressive kidney failure and severe hypertension can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Urine output change, edema, thirst, dry mucosa, dizziness, flank/suprapubic pain, dysuria, confusion, weakness, weight change, or abnormal heart rhythm can reveal renal, fluid, or electrolyte stress.

  • Escalate anuria, severe potassium abnormality, pulmonary edema, seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, or worsening acidosis.

Diagnostics

  • age- and genotype-aware renal ultrasonography or MRI

  • serial blood pressure, eGFR, albuminuria, urinalysis, and kidney-volume risk assessment

  • selective intracranial aneurysm screening based on family history, symptoms, occupation, or treatment planning

  • Distinguish Polycystic kidney disease from simple cysts, acquired cystic disease, other inherited ciliopathies, and obstructive cystic change by distribution, family history, and extrarenal findings.

Labs

  • creatinine and BUN rise when filtration worsens

  • potassium may rise with kidney failure

  • urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is concerning

  • WBC/nitrites/leukocyte esterase support UTI

Treatments

  • control blood pressure and cardiovascular risk

  • treat infection, stones, pain, and hematuria without nephrotoxic shortcuts

  • consider disease-modifying therapy for appropriately selected rapidly progressive autosomal dominant disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for sudden worst headache, focal deficit, syncope, fever with persistent flank pain, gross hematuria with clots or retention, severe uncontrolled pressure, rapidly worsening kidney function, or peritoneal signs because aneurysmal bleeding, cyst infection or rupture, obstruction, or vascular emergency may be present.

  • Assess family history, blood pressure, kidney function, flank or abdominal pain, hematuria, urinary infection, stones, abdominal fullness, pregnancy plans, cardiac history, and sudden neurologic symptoms because ADPKD affects kidneys and selected extrarenal tissues across generations.

  • Trend pressure, weight, edema, urine albumin and blood, creatinine and filtration, potassium, pain pattern, infection signs, and ordered imaging or progression markers because hypertension and cyst growth drive kidney decline before late symptoms emerge.

  • Administer prescribed pressure and disease-modifying therapy while monitoring volume, sodium, liver tests, urine output, pregnancy considerations, and interactions because treatment can slow progression but may cause aquaresis or other organ toxicity.

  • Obtain urine and blood cultures before antibiotics when cyst infection is suspected, manage stones and pain without routine NSAID exposure, and coordinate nephrology and genetic counseling because infected cyst penetration and inherited risk require specialized decisions.

Complications

  • progressive kidney failure and severe hypertension

  • infected or bleeding cysts and nephrolithiasis

  • intracranial aneurysm rupture or liver cyst burden

Red Flags

  • Sudden thunderclap headache, focal neurologic deficit, seizure, or syncope

  • Fever with persistent focal flank or abdominal pain despite initial treatment

  • Gross hematuria with clots, urinary retention, hypotension, or falling hemoglobin

  • Severe uncontrolled hypertension, rapid kidney decline, or acute peritoneal findings

Patient Education

  • Teach home blood-pressure tracking, hydration and sodium guidance individualized to the treatment plan, and avoidance of routine NSAIDs because pressure and nephrotoxins accelerate loss of kidney reserve.

  • Explain that genetic counseling can help adult relatives make informed testing, pregnancy, donor, and screening decisions without assuming every family member has the same course.

Nclex Traps

  • Not every renal cyst indicates inherited polycystic disease

  • Sudden worst-ever headache requires emergency evaluation for subarachnoid hemorrhage
