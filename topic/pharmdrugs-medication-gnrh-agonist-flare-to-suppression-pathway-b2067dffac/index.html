Used To Treat

Formulation-specific treatment of prostate cancer, endometriosis, uterine fibroids with planned add-back regimens, central precocious puberty, breast cancer in selected premenopausal patients, and pituitary suppression in fertility protocols.

Description

Leuprolide and goserelin are GnRH agonists, yet continuous exposure ultimately suppresses the axis that GnRH normally activates. The first doses stimulate pituitary GnRH receptors and transiently raise LH, FSH, testosterone, or estradiol; this is the flare. Continued nonpulsatile exposure causes receptor desensitization and internalization, so gonadotropins and gonadal steroids fall to castrate or hypoestrogenic ranges. The time sequence explains why cancer pain, urinary obstruction, vaginal bleeding, or endometriosis symptoms can briefly worsen before improvement and why bone loss, hot flashes, sexual dysfunction, and metabolic effects appear during sustained suppression.

Mechanism

Physiologic GnRH arrives in pulses. Pulse frequency and amplitude allow pituitary gonadotrophs to repeatedly signal through Gq/11, phospholipase C, inositol trisphosphate, calcium, and gonadotropin release. Long-acting leuprolide or goserelin removes the recovery interval. Initial receptor occupancy increases LH and FSH, but persistent stimulation uncouples signaling, internalizes receptors, and reduces GnRH-receptor expression. LH and FSH then decline, reducing Leydig-cell testosterone and ovarian estradiol/progesterone production. The lower sex-steroid state slows hormone-sensitive tissue but also accelerates bone resorption and alters vasomotor, mood, glucose, lipid, sexual, and cardiovascular physiology. Pulsatile GnRH can stimulate fertility; continuous agonist therapy suppresses it.

Boxed Warning

There is no shared class boxed warning, but the initial flare can be dangerous in advanced prostate cancer with vertebral metastases, urinary obstruction, or spinal-cord risk and in other hormone-sensitive disease. Long-term suppression can decrease bone mineral density, prolong QT in susceptible androgen-deprivation settings, worsen glucose control or cardiovascular risk, and rarely precipitate pituitary apoplexy. Pregnancy must be excluded for reproductive-age use because these drugs can harm pregnancy and do not reliably prevent conception during the initial flare. Product depot, dose, interval, and indication are not interchangeable.

Adverse Effects

  • Hot flashes, sweating, headache, mood change, decreased libido, vaginal dryness, erectile dysfunction, injection-site reaction, and initial symptom flare

  • Bone loss or fracture, hyperglycemia, cardiovascular events, QT prolongation, severe depression, tumor-flare complications, ovarian cyst/OHSS in fertility combinations, or pituitary apoplexy

Contraindications

  • Pregnancy, serious hypersensitivity to GnRH analogues, and any product-specific restriction; evaluate unexplained neurologic symptoms or pituitary mass risk.

  • Before cancer use, identify spinal metastasis, urinary obstruction, severe bone pain, or hypercalcemia that may require flare protection and close observation.

Nursing Essentials

  • Teach the timeline: symptoms may worsen first, then hormone levels fall. Early flare is not proof that the drug failed, but red-flag compression or obstruction symptoms are emergencies.

  • Track depot date and formulation precisely. A missed long-acting dose can permit hormonal recovery, while duplicate depot administration prolongs suppression.

Interactions

  • Other QT-prolonging or electrolyte-altering drugs can compound arrhythmia risk in androgen-deprivation therapy.

  • Other bone-depleting medicines, glucocorticoids, smoking, and alcohol increase skeletal risk; fertility gonadotropins can produce an intentionally different ovarian response.

Key Labs

  • Pregnancy testing when relevant, testosterone or estradiol as indicated, PSA or disease marker, glucose/A1c, lipids, electrolytes, bone-density risk, and liver/renal context

  • Symptoms of cord compression or obstruction, hot flashes, mood, sexual effects, bleeding pattern, injection-site injury, fracture risk, and adherence to depot schedule

Nclex Traps

  • An agonist can suppress a receptor system when it is delivered continuously and causes desensitization; agonist does not always mean sustained activation.

  • Pulsatile GnRH promotes gonadotropin release, while continuous depot GnRH agonist exposure suppresses release after the initial flare.
