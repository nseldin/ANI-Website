Risk Factors

  • PCA embolism or atherosclerotic occlusion threatens occipital cortex.

  • Large MCA stroke can injure optic radiations along with language or attention networks.

  • Coexisting neglect or poor insight increases safety risk because the patient may not compensate for the missing field.

Signs Symptoms

  • The patient bumps into objects, misses words on one side, cannot find items, or reports that half the scene is absent or blurred.

  • Testing each eye separately shows the same side of space missing in both eyes.

  • Occipital stroke may preserve strength, while temporal or parietal extension adds memory, language, sensory, or neglect findings.

Diagnostics

  • Confrontation fields must test all quadrants in each eye because asking whether vision is blurry is insufficient.

  • Test neglect and extinction separately because an attentional deficit changes environmental awareness but is not identical to field loss.

  • Use urgent brain and vascular imaging for sudden onset, with MRI often more sensitive for small posterior infarction.

Labs

  • Glucose helps exclude a metabolic mimic of visual confusion.

  • No lab localizes a visual field; examination and imaging establish the post-chiasmal lesion.

  • Cardiac rhythm and vascular studies identify the embolic or arterial source for secondary prevention.

Treatments

  • Evaluate sudden disabling field loss through the acute reperfusion pathway because isolated vision loss can still destroy independence.

  • Use scanning training, visual anchors, mobility and occupational therapy because the lost field must be compensated for deliberately.

  • Restrict driving until formal evaluation and jurisdiction-specific clearance because scanning in a clinic may not guarantee road safety.

Nursing Priorities

  • Test each visual field by confrontation with one eye covered, compare both sides, and assess pupils, acuity, gaze, neglect, reading, and onset because retrochiasmal field loss differs from monocular eye disease and inattention.

  • Activate emergency stroke evaluation for sudden homonymous field loss and document last-known-well, glucose, anticoagulants, headache, and associated deficits because an isolated visual deficit may still qualify as disabling cerebral ischemia.

  • Keep pathways uncluttered, place items within the seeing field initially, supervise mobility, and teach deliberate head and eye scanning because lost field awareness causes collisions, falls, and unsafe driving.

  • Monitor visual-field pattern, gaze, pupils, headache, NIH Stroke Scale trend, reading, mobility, swallowing, and new neurologic findings because extension, edema, hemorrhage, or seizure may alter the original localization.

  • Escalate for sudden or expanding field loss, new weakness or aphasia, NIH Stroke Scale increase, painful red eye, unequal pupils, severe headache, vomiting, or reduced consciousness because stroke progression and ocular or intracranial emergencies need urgent differentiation.

Complications

  • Falls, collisions, missed food or medication, reading difficulty, and unsafe driving can persist.

  • Poor awareness or coexisting neglect can limit rehabilitation because the patient does not consistently scan.

  • Bilateral occipital injury can cause cortical blindness and severe dependence.

Contraindications

  • Do not call a homonymous field cut monocular blindness.

  • Do not assume normal pupils exclude cortical visual loss.

  • Do not combine neglect and hemianopia into one diagnosis without separate testing because their rehabilitation strategies differ.

Red Flags

  • Sudden loss of the same side of vision in both eyes or abrupt navigation difficulty

  • Expanding field loss with weakness, aphasia, neglect, gaze deviation, or ataxia

  • Severe headache, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, seizure, or reduced consciousness

  • Painful red eye, abrupt monocular blindness, flashing lights, or a curtain-like visual loss

Patient Education

  • Do not drive until formally cleared because homonymous field loss can hide vehicles, pedestrians, and hazards despite normal central acuity.

  • Practice systematic scanning toward the missing field before walking, reading, or reaching because compensation must become deliberate before it becomes reliable.

Nclex Traps

  • Homonymous means the same visual side is lost in both eyes.

  • Field loss is not neglect, though both can coexist.

  • Isolated hemianopia can be a disabling stroke despite a low NIHSS.

Name

Homonymous hemianopia and visual-field localization

Category

Neuro-ophthalmic stroke localization

Definition

Homonymous hemianopia is a neurologic vision loss caused by injury behind the optic chiasm, which removes the same half of visual space from both eyes. A left post-chiasmal lesion in the optic tract, lateral geniculate body, optic radiations, or occipital cortex causes right visual-field loss in both eyes, while a corresponding right-sided lesion causes left visual-field loss. It differs from monocular blindness, which usually localizes to the eye, retina, or optic nerve before the chiasm.

Etiology

PCA occipital infarction is classic, while MCA optic-radiation injury, hemorrhage, tumor, trauma, seizure, migraine aura, or demyelination can create a similar field deficit. Sudden persistent loss is treated as stroke until urgent assessment proves otherwise.

Pathology

At the optic chiasm, nasal retinal fibers cross while temporal retinal fibers remain ipsilateral. This sorting makes each post-chiasmal pathway carry the contralateral, or opposite, visual hemifield from both eyes, so the left pathway processes the right field and the right pathway processes the left field. Injury anywhere in one post-chiasmal pathway therefore removes matching halves of visual space from both eyes.

Pathophysiology

Optic-tract lesions may add a relative afferent pupillary defect, while optic-radiation lesions can produce quadrant-predominant loss depending on temporal or parietal involvement. Occipital lesions often create highly congruous field cuts, and bilateral occipital injury can cause cortical blindness despite normal pupils and ocular structures. Neglect can mimic or compound the disability, but neglect varies with attention and bilateral competition whereas a primary field cut remains when each side is tested separately.
