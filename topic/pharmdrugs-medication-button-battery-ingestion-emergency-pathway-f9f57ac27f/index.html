Used To Treat

Known or suspected button or coin-cell ingestion, especially an unwitnessed pediatric exposure or presumed coin, plus batteries lodged in the nose or ear where local electrical injury continues.

Description

A button battery is an electrolyte-containing electrochemical cell whose current generates hydroxide at the negative pole when lodged in moist esophageal tissue, producing a rapidly deepening alkaline burn. This is an electrical-injury emergency, not simply a foreign body, because damage continues even when the battery is not leaking and can no longer power a device. Severe burns can develop in about two hours, and an initially asymptomatic child may later develop perforation, vocal-cord paralysis, tracheoesophageal fistula, mediastinitis, or an aorto-esophageal fistula with fatal hemorrhage. Every presumed coin deserves battery consideration. AP imaging may show a double rim or halo, and a lateral image may show a step-off, but technique can hide these signs. Honey given to an eligible child can slow pH rise while traveling to care, but it cannot stop injury and must never delay imaging or removal. Esophageal batteries require immediate endoscopic removal and careful post-removal injury mapping because tissue damage can progress after the battery is gone.

Mechanism

When both poles contact conductive tissue, electrolysis reduces water and generates hydroxide next to the negative pole. Local pH can rise rapidly, saponifying cell membranes and penetrating through the esophageal wall. Pressure and leakage contribute less than the electrical circuit for most modern lithium coin-cell injuries. Honey and sucralfate coat the battery and partially buffer local alkalinity, reducing the rate and depth of injury in experimental models; they are mitigation, not antidotes. Removing the battery breaks the circuit, while specialist dilute acetic-acid irrigation after removal can neutralize residual tissue-surface alkalinity under direct visualization. Necrotic tissue continues evolving, and proximity to the trachea, recurrent laryngeal nerves, spine, and great vessels explains delayed airway, fistula, infection, neurologic, and catastrophic bleeding complications.

Administration Timing

  • Call the National Battery Ingestion Hotline or Poison Help and go to the emergency department immediately; call 911 for breathing difficulty, drooling, inability to swallow, chest pain, bleeding, collapse, or severe symptoms. Do not induce vomiting.

  • If a lithium coin cell may have been swallowed within 12 hours, the child is at least 12 months old, can swallow, and honey is immediately available, give 10 mL every 10 minutes for up to six doses while en route. Give nothing else, keep NPO after honey, and do not delay transport; honey is unsafe under 12 months and is not used when swallowing is unsafe or timing exceeds 12 hours.

  • Obtain immediate x-rays that include the entire neck, esophagus, and abdomen, with AP and lateral views for an esophageal object. Remove an esophageal battery immediately by endoscopy regardless of fasting or honey; inspect injury and use post-removal imaging, airway, surgical, and feeding plans based on depth and proximity to vessels.

Nursing Essentials

  • Ask about unwitnessed access, device and replacement battery, imprint code, approximate diameter, time last seen well, symptoms, prior esophageal disease, and possible magnet or second battery. Check both ears and nostrils when location is uncertain because a battery can lodge outside the gastrointestinal tract and continue causing local electrical injury.

  • Keep the child NPO except guideline-eligible honey, minimize delays between radiology, anesthesia, ENT or GI, and surgery, and document battery orientation because the negative pole predicts the side of greatest injury.

  • After removal, monitor voice, stridor, cough with swallowing, fever, chest pain, hematemesis, melena, occult blood, neurologic findings, respiratory status, and nutrition because necrosis, airway injury, infection, fistula, and bleeding can emerge after the battery is gone. Treat even a small sentinel bleed as possible great-vessel fistula and activate cardiothoracic or vascular rescue immediately because minor bleeding can precede catastrophic hemorrhage.

Key Labs

  • Imaging is the decisive early test: include the full neck, chest or esophagus, and abdomen because symptoms and a limited film can miss an esophageal or more distal battery; use AP and lateral views to distinguish halo and step-off features and determine pole orientation. After the battery has been removed, contrast-enhanced CT or MRI can assess proximity to major vessels and an evolving fistula because deep injury may extend beyond what endoscopy shows; MRI must never be interpreted as an option while a battery may remain in place.

  • Obtain CBC, type and screen, inflammatory and metabolic studies when complications are possible because evolving hemorrhage, infection, and tissue injury may require rapid intervention, but normal labs cannot exclude deep local injury. Use endoscopic depth, anatomic position, symptoms, and serial imaging to determine observation and feeding because these findings better reflect local injury trajectory.

Adverse Effects

  • Complications include esophageal perforation, tracheoesophageal or aorto-esophageal fistula, exsanguination, mediastinitis, aspiration, vocal-cord paralysis, tracheal stenosis or malacia, empyema, spondylodiscitis, stricture, feeding difficulty, and death.

  • Do not give honey under 12 months because infant botulism risk is concentrated in that age group, and do not give it when swallowing is unsafe because aspiration can occur. Endoscopy, anesthesia, and post-removal irrigation carry procedural risks, but delay with an esophageal battery is substantially more dangerous.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce vomiting, wait for symptoms, assume a round object is a coin, or delay removal because the patient ate or received honey. Do not give honey to an infant under 12 months, after more than 12 hours, or to anyone unable to swallow safely.

  • Do not push an esophageal battery into the stomach as a substitute for removal, and do not assume removal ends injury. Artist or household neutralizers are not appropriate; post-removal acetic irrigation is a specialist endoscopic protocol.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Any esophageal battery, airway symptom, bleeding, fever, perforation sign, severe mucosal injury, battery near a great vessel, magnet co-ingestion, or symptomatic battery beyond the esophagus requires specialty admission and intervention. Sentinel bleeding days to weeks later is an emergency even after successful removal.

Interactions

  • A co-ingested magnet increases entrapment and pressure-necrosis risk and changes retrieval decisions. Prior esophageal surgery, stricture, or motility disorder increases lodging risk.

  • Honey and sucralfate reduce alkaline injury only temporarily; neither replaces removal. Food, routine drinks, emesis, and delayed imaging interfere with definitive care without stopping current generation.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children younger than six

    Note

    Large lithium coin cells lodge readily in a small esophagus, and unwitnessed symptoms can mimic infection or ordinary feeding problems.

  • Population

    Infants younger than twelve months

    Note

    Honey is contraindicated because of botulism risk, while the esophageal injury risk from a battery remains an immediate emergency.

  • Population

    Prior esophageal disease

    Note

    Stricture, surgery, dysmotility, or congenital anatomy increases lodging and means even a smaller battery needs prompt localization.

Evidence Limitations

  • Honey, sucralfate, and post-removal acetic irrigation are supported by experimental and clinical-consensus evidence rather than randomized outcome trials. The National Capital Poison Center guideline is the operational standard, with surveillance individualized by injury anatomy.

Nclex Traps

  • A child can have a destructive esophageal battery injury before symptoms appear.

  • Honey slows alkaline injury only in narrowly eligible patients and never replaces immediate removal.

  • A small sentinel bleed after removal can precede catastrophic aorto-esophageal hemorrhage.

Regulatory Status

This is an emergency procedural pathway. Honey is a guideline-directed prehospital mitigation, sucralfate is specialist directed, and acetic-acid irrigation is performed only under endoscopic protocol; none replaces immediate esophageal removal.

Boxed Warning

BUTTON-BATTERY WARNING: treat every possible coin-cell ingestion as an emergency because an esophageal battery can burn deeply within two hours without symptoms. Call Poison Help or the Battery Hotline, give honey only when guideline eligible, obtain immediate full-tract x-rays, and remove an esophageal battery without delay.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
