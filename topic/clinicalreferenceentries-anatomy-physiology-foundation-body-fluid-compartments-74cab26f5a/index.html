Summary

Body water is divided into intracellular fluid inside cells and extracellular fluid outside them. Extracellular fluid includes interstitial fluid between cells, plasma within vessels, and smaller specialized spaces. Water crosses cell membranes according to effective osmolality, while sodium balance, vascular pressures, proteins, endothelial permeability, lymph flow, heart function, and kidney function determine how extracellular fluid distributes.

Quick Answer

Roughly two thirds of body water is intracellular and one third extracellular in a typical adult, but age, sex, adiposity, pregnancy, and illness change the proportions. Sodium dominates extracellular fluid and potassium intracellular fluid. A patient can have edema or ascites yet poor effective circulation because visible fluid may be outside the vascular space or the heart and vessels may not deliver it forward.

Sections

    • Compartments and location

    • Total body water is not one freely mixed pool. Intracellular fluid occupies the cytoplasm of cells and holds most body water. Extracellular fluid includes plasma, interstitial fluid, and transcellular fluids such as cerebrospinal, pleural, peritoneal, synovial, ocular, and gastrointestinal secretions. Plasma and interstitial fluid are separated by capillary endothelium; extracellular and intracellular fluid are separated by cell membranes. Infants generally have a larger water fraction, older adults and people with more adipose tissue a smaller one. These differences influence dehydration speed, drug distribution, and tolerance of losses.

    • Solute patterns and water movement

    • Extracellular fluid is rich in sodium and chloride, while intracellular fluid is rich in potassium, phosphate, magnesium, and negatively charged proteins. Pumps maintain these unequal concentrations, but water can move rapidly enough that effective osmolality tends to equilibrate across cell membranes. If extracellular tonicity falls, water enters cells; if it rises, water leaves them. Isotonic sodium loss primarily contracts extracellular volume, while pure water loss raises tonicity and draws water from cells. This distinction explains why the same blood pressure or weight finding can require different fluid and electrolyte reasoning.

    • Plasma-interstitial exchange

    • Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid across capillaries, plasma oncotic pressure from proteins such as albumin opposes loss, endothelial permeability determines what escapes, and lymphatics return excess interstitial fluid and protein. Increased venous pressure in heart failure, reduced albumin in advanced liver or nephrotic disease, capillary leak in sepsis or burns, and lymph obstruction can all cause edema by different routes. Because interstitial fluid does not directly support arterial pressure, marked edema can coexist with low effective circulating volume, kidney sodium retention, and poor organ perfusion.

    • Regulation of volume and concentration

    • Thirst and antidiuretic hormone regulate water relative to solute. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and sympathetic pathways promote sodium retention and vascular responses when renal perfusion or effective arterial filling falls. Natriuretic peptides favor sodium excretion when cardiac chambers stretch, but their signal may not overcome advanced heart or kidney failure. Kidneys adjust urine volume and concentration through filtration, tubular transport, medullary gradients, and collecting-duct water permeability. Regulation targets both concentration and effective circulation; those goals can conflict when a patient is edematous but underperfused.

    • Failure consequences

    • Extracellular volume depletion reduces venous return, stroke volume, organ perfusion, and urine output. Excess extracellular volume can cause hypertension, edema, ascites, or pulmonary congestion. Hypertonicity shrinks brain cells and can cause thirst, weakness, confusion, or hemorrhagic vascular stress; hypotonicity causes cellular swelling, with the brain limited by the skull. Third spacing traps fluid where it does not support circulation. Rapid correction after chronic osmotic adaptation can injure the nervous system because brain cells have adjusted their intracellular osmoles over time.

    • Clinical connections

    • Hemorrhage, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, osmotic diuresis, burns, sepsis, heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, kidney failure, SIADH, diabetes insipidus, adrenal disease, and medications each produce different compartment patterns. Isotonic crystalloids expand extracellular fluid; only a fraction remains intravascular after distribution. Blood products restore selected circulating components. Diuretics reduce sodium-linked extracellular volume but can worsen underfilling or electrolytes. Albumin, hypertonic saline, dialysis, and fluid restriction have mechanism-specific roles. Serum sodium, osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium, hematocrit, albumin, creatinine, BUN, glucose, and imaging help define the pattern.

    • Nursing assessment

    • Measure daily weight under consistent conditions, strict intake and output, urine output, orthostatic symptoms when safe, heart rate, blood pressure, pulse quality, capillary refill, mental status, mucous membranes, thirst, skin and dependent edema, jugular veins, lung sounds, oxygen requirement, abdominal girth, and functional tolerance. Compare values with baseline and treatment timing. A single skin-turgor finding is weak evidence in older adults. Weight is often the most sensitive practical trend for net fluid change, but it cannot reveal whether the change is intravascular, interstitial, intracellular, or third-spaced without the rest of the assessment.

    • Nursing actions and why

    • Match intervention to mechanism. Replace ordered isotonic volume for true extracellular depletion and reassess perfusion, lungs, and urine output after each step. Implement sodium or fluid restriction when prescribed for water-retaining or congestive states and explain that hidden fluids count. Administer diuretics with blood pressure, renal function, electrolyte, weight, and symptom monitoring. Protect edematous skin, reposition, elevate when appropriate, and prevent falls during rapid diuresis or orthostasis. Escalate when a fluid plan improves one compartment marker while worsening breathing, perfusion, sodium, or kidney function.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Hypotension with confusion, cool mottled skin, weak pulses, oliguria, rising lactate, or syncope indicates threatened effective circulation. Acute dyspnea, pink frothy sputum, hypoxemia, or rapidly increasing oxygen need suggests pulmonary edema. Seizure, severe headache, vomiting, marked confusion, or coma with sodium or osmolality disturbance requires urgent management. Rapid abdominal distention with respiratory or renal compromise can signal severe ascites or compartment pressure. A large unmonitored output after obstruction relief, diabetes insipidus, or diuretic therapy can quickly produce circulatory and electrolyte collapse.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Edema is not proof of intravascular fullness. Dehydration is often used loosely, but water deficit with hypertonicity differs from isotonic volume depletion. Sodium concentration is a ratio of sodium to water, not a direct measurement of total sodium stores. Intake and output can be inaccurate, so trend weight and clinical response. Normal blood pressure does not exclude compensated volume loss. Third-spaced fluid is in the body but not effectively supporting circulation. Never choose a fluid solely from the word 'dry' or 'swollen'; identify tonicity, perfusion, organ function, ongoing losses, and the cause.
