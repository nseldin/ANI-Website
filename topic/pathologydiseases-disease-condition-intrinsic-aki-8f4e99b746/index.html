Definition

Intrinsic AKI is an abrupt loss of kidney function caused by injury within renal parenchyma. The central task is to identify whether the dominant compartment is tubular, interstitial, glomerular, or vascular because the urgency, testing, and treatment differ substantially.

Pathology

Tubular injury disrupts epithelial transport and flow; interstitial disease produces edema and inflammatory infiltration around tubules; glomerular injury disrupts capillary loops and filtration barrier; vascular disease compromises renal micro- or macro-circulation. Several compartments can be injured simultaneously in sepsis, systemic inflammation, or severe hypertension.

Pathophysiology

Intrinsic lesions reduce GFR through cell injury, inflammatory swelling, capillary occlusion, altered arteriolar tone, tubular obstruction, backleak, or loss of filtration surface. Urine sediment becomes a bedside window into the compartment: granular casts suggest tubular injury, red-cell casts and dysmorphic cells suggest glomerular bleeding, white-cell casts suggest interstitial or upper-tract inflammation, and bland urine can occur in vascular or early disease. Because serum creatinine only reports filtration, a kidney biopsy may be needed when distinguishing treatable immune or interstitial disease would change management.

Etiology

Acute tubular injury is most common in hospitalized patients. Other causes include drug- or infection-associated interstitial nephritis, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, immune-complex disease, thrombotic microangiopathy, malignant hypertension, atheroembolism, renal infarction, cortical necrosis, pyelonephritis, and infiltrative disease.

Risk Factors

  • Shock, sepsis, surgery, pigments, or nephrotoxic exposure

  • New antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, NSAIDs, immune therapy, or other interstitial-nephritis triggers

  • Autoimmune disease, infection, monoclonal gammopathy, or pulmonary-renal symptoms

  • Severe hypertension, embolic disease, thrombocytopenia/hemolysis, pregnancy complications, or transplant

Signs Symptoms

  • Oliguric or nonoliguric creatinine rise

  • Hematuria, proteinuria, edema, or hypertension with glomerular disease

  • Fever, rash, arthralgia, pyuria, or eosinophilia can occur in interstitial nephritis but the classic triad is often absent

  • Flank pain, livedo, ischemic findings, neurologic changes, or thrombocytopenia with vascular disease

Diagnostics

  • Perform urinalysis and expert sediment review early

  • Match medication and systemic-disease chronology to the renal change

  • Order targeted complements, antibodies, cultures, hemolysis studies, electrophoresis, imaging, or vascular testing rather than broad untargeted panels

  • Consider kidney biopsy when diagnosis remains uncertain and a disease-specific treatment decision depends on tissue

Labs

  • Protein amount and sediment pattern help localize the lesion

  • Complement, ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA-related tests, infection studies, or monoclonal tests depend on the phenotype

  • CBC can show eosinophilia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or infection

  • FeNa and FeUrea cannot reliably exclude intrinsic injury

Treatments

  • Remove the inciting toxin or drug and treat infection, ischemia, or systemic disease

  • Use disease-specific immunosuppression, plasma exchange, blood-pressure control, or hematologic therapy only when the diagnosis supports it

  • Manage fluid, electrolyte, acid-base, nutrition, and medication-clearance consequences

  • Use kidney replacement therapy for refractory complications while diagnostic treatment proceeds

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess nephrotoxins, ischemia, sepsis, rash, infection, autoimmune symptoms, hemolysis, muscle injury, urine findings, and recent procedures because intrinsic AKI may arise from tubular, glomerular, interstitial, or vascular injury.

  • Trend creatinine, urine output, potassium, bicarbonate, phosphate, fluid balance, urine sediment, blood pressure, and medication exposure because complications can evolve before creatinine reaches its peak.

  • Stop or avoid nephrotoxins as ordered and adjust renally cleared medications with pharmacy because injured nephrons cannot eliminate drugs normally and additional exposure can prolong damage.

  • Obtain cause-directed blood, urine, imaging, and biopsy preparation when ordered because granular casts, protein and blood, eosinophilic features, or vascular clues lead to different time-sensitive treatments.

  • Escalate urgently for refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, anuria, uremic encephalopathy, pericarditic pain, or active glomerular sediment because kidney replacement or immunologic treatment may be immediately necessary.

Complications

  • Rapid irreversible nephron loss

  • Pulmonary hemorrhage or systemic vasculitis injury

  • Severe hypertension, thrombosis, or multiorgan microangiopathy

  • Hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, uremia, CKD, and kidney failure

Contraindications

  • Do not call all intrinsic AKI ATN

  • Do not rely on absence of fever, rash, eosinophilia, or casts to exclude interstitial or glomerular disease

  • Avoid empiric immunosuppression without appropriate exclusion of infection and diagnostic reasoning

Red Flags

  • Hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram change, weakness, or dysrhythmia

  • Pulmonary edema with rising oxygen requirement or respiratory distress

  • Anuria, rapidly rising creatinine, or severe persistent metabolic acidosis

  • Confusion, seizure, pericarditic pain, bleeding, or active nephritic sediment

Patient Education

  • Explain that intrinsic AKI means injury within kidney tissue, so treatment depends on whether tubules, glomeruli, interstitium, or vessels are affected.

  • Teach patients to avoid unreviewed NSAIDs and supplements and to report reduced urine, swelling, rash, dark urine, or severe weakness promptly.

Nclex Traps

  • Intrinsic AKI is a compartment-based differential, not a synonym for ATN

  • Urine sediment can localize disease but is not perfectly sensitive

  • The classic interstitial-nephritis triad is uncommon

  • A bland urine does not exclude serious vascular injury
