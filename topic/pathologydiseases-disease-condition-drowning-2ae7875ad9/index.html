Definition

Drowning develops when submersion causes aspiration, surfactant dysfunction, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, and hypoxemia that can continue after rescue. The characteristic pattern is cough, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, hypoxemia, abnormal lung sounds, or arrest after water exposure. Confirm or stage it with continuous oxygenation and neurologic assessment; distinguish it from syncope with secondary immersion, cervical trauma, hypothermia, aspiration, and toxic exposure by event history and focused testing. The priority is to begin rescue breathing and high-quality resuscitation when needed because acute respiratory distress syndrome and cerebral hypoxic injury can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • continuous oxygenation and neurologic assessment

  • blood gas, chest imaging, electrolytes, and temperature only as clinical severity warrants

  • trauma and cause evaluation for seizure, intoxication, arrhythmia, or unsafe environment

  • Distinguish Drowning from syncope with secondary immersion, cervical trauma, hypothermia, aspiration, and toxic exposure by event history and focused testing.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • begin rescue breathing and high-quality resuscitation when needed

  • support oxygenation and ventilation and prevent further heat loss

  • observe symptomatic patients and treat pulmonary or neurologic complications rather than giving routine prophylactic antibiotics

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for apnea, persistent hypoxemia, frothy sputum, increasing work of breathing, hypotension, arrhythmia, seizure, or decreasing consciousness because pulmonary edema, aspiration injury, and hypoxic brain injury can progress rapidly.

  • Ensure rescuer safety, remove the person from the water, and begin rescue breathing and cardiopulmonary resuscitation according to training because drowning arrest begins with hypoxia and ventilation is central to resuscitation.

  • Provide high-concentration oxygen and assess airway, respiratory effort, breath sounds, saturation, temperature, circulation, and neurologic status because aspiration and lung injury can worsen after initial rescue.

  • Remove wet clothing, prevent further heat loss, and rewarm in a controlled manner because hypothermia worsens coagulopathy and complicates rhythm and neurologic assessment.

  • Maintain cervical-spine precautions only when trauma is suspected and avoid routine abdominal thrusts because unnecessary maneuvers delay ventilation and can provoke vomiting and aspiration.

Complications

  • acute respiratory distress syndrome and cerebral hypoxic injury

  • hypothermia can complicate Drowning.

  • arrhythmia can complicate Drowning.

  • multiorgan failure can complicate Drowning.

  • delayed respiratory deterioration after an apparently successful rescue

Red Flags

  • Apnea, cardiac arrest, or persistent cyanosis

  • Increasing oxygen need, crackles, or pink frothy sputum

  • Hypotension, unstable rhythm, or severe hypothermia

  • Seizure, confusion, or declining consciousness after rescue

Patient Education

  • Explain that a person who seems better can still develop breathing problems after aspiration, so emergency evaluation and observation are important after significant symptoms.

  • Teach water barriers, close active supervision, life-jacket use, swimming skills, and cardiopulmonary-resuscitation training because prevention and early ventilation save lives.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not use abdominal thrusts to remove aspirated water before ventilation

  • Any persistent cough, breathing difficulty, confusion, or sleepiness after submersion needs medical assessment
