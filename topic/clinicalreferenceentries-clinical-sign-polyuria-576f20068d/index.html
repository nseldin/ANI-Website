Definition

Polyuria means the body produces an abnormally large total volume of urine. In adults, more than 3 liters or about 50 milliliters per kilogram over 24 hours is a commonly used definition; frequent small voids are not polyuria.

Plain Language

Polyuria means making too much urine overall, not just going to the bathroom often.

Why It Matters

Polyuria can cause dehydration and dangerous sodium or potassium changes and may signal uncontrolled diabetes, impaired arginine-vasopressin action, excessive fluid intake, a medication effect, or post-obstructive or kidney-injury recovery. Measure intake and output and check glucose, sodium, volume status, and urine concentration before changing fluids.

Associated Conditions

  • osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia or other solute load, impaired arginine-vasopressin release or kidney response, primary polydipsia, diuretics, excess intravenous fluid, post-obstructive diuresis, or recovery from acute kidney injury

Assessment Method

Measure actual timed urine volume, preferably across 24 hours, and paired intake rather than relying on void count; assess thirst, onset, weight and volume status, vital signs, glucose, serum sodium and osmolality, urine osmolality or specific gravity, medications and fluids, recent obstruction relief or kidney injury, neurologic history, pregnancy, and electrolyte trends.

Positive Finding

Total urine volume is persistently excessive for body size and context; in adults, more than 3 liters or about 50 milliliters per kilogram over 24 hours is a commonly used definition.

Mechanism

Excess filtered solute holds water in the tubules, suppressed arginine vasopressin or renal resistance reduces collecting-duct water reabsorption, excessive intake increases excretion, and post-obstructive or recovering tubules may temporarily waste water and electrolytes.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Polyuria to focus evaluation of osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia or other solute load, impaired arginine-vasopressin release or kidney response, primary polydipsia, diuretics, excess intravenous fluid, post-obstructive diuresis, or recovery from acute kidney injury and the reviewed alternatives: urinary frequency, nocturia, primary polydipsia, diuretic effect. Evidence Limitations

Frequent urination without a large 24-hour total is not polyuria; short intervals, diuretics, large fluid loads, recovery states, missed collections, and adult versus pediatric thresholds alter interpretation. Therefore, Polyuria cannot confirm or exclude osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia or other solute load, impaired arginine-vasopressin release or kidney response, primary polydipsia, diuretics, excess intravenous fluid, post-obstructive diuresis, or recovery from acute kidney injury by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as urinary frequency, nocturia, primary polydipsia, diuretic effect.

False Negative Situations

Polyuria may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia or other solute load, impaired arginine-vasopressin release or kidney response, primary polydipsia, diuretics, excess intravenous fluid, post-obstructive diuresis, or recovery from acute kidney injury.
