Contraindications

  • Uric-acid kidney stones, significant blood dyscrasias, age under two years, and serious hypersensitivity; salicylate coadministration antagonizes uricosuria and is listed as contraindicated in current product labeling.

  • Clarify eGFR, stone history, hydration, ulcer history, G6PD/hematologic disease, acute-flare status, pregnancy, and every renally secreted medication.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain serum urate, renal function, CBC, urinalysis and stone history; teach hydration and renal-colic/hematuria warning signs, then titrate by serum-urate response.

  • Perform a full interaction review before use. When given to prolong a beta-lactam, verify that increased antibiotic exposure is intentional and watch for concentration-related neurotoxicity or other adverse effects.

Interactions

  • Raises or prolongs exposure to penicillins and some cephalosporins, methotrexate, selected antivirals, sulfonylureas, NSAIDs, and other organic-anion transporter substrates; clinical consequences vary by drug.

  • Salicylates and pyrazinamide antagonize uricosuria. Probenecid is a strong OAT3 inhibitor that approximately doubles baricitinib exposure and therefore triggers a baricitinib dose reduction.

Key Labs

  • Serum urate, creatinine/eGFR, CBC, urinalysis and urine symptoms; drug-specific concentrations or toxicity monitoring for interacting substrates

  • Flare frequency, hydration, stone/renal-colic symptoms, rash or systemic hypersensitivity, and objective gout burden

Nclex Traps

  • Probenecid makes the kidney excrete urate; allopurinol and febuxostat reduce urate production. Those are different pathways with different renal and interaction consequences.

  • The penicillin interaction can be deliberate, but the same transport blockade can unintentionally raise methotrexate or baricitinib exposure.

Used To Treat

Hyperuricemia associated with gout and gouty arthritis when a uricosuric approach is appropriate, and deliberate prolongation of selected penicillin or other beta-lactam concentrations by reducing renal tubular secretion. It is less effective with advanced kidney dysfunction and can increase urinary uric-acid stones.

Mechanism

Renal urate handling is a balance of glomerular filtration, proximal secretion, and extensive postsecretory reabsorption. URAT1 exchanges luminal urate for intracellular organic anions, and OAT4 contributes apical reabsorption. At therapeutic uricosuric exposure, probenecid inhibits these reabsorptive transporters, increasing fractional urate excretion and lowering serum urate. It also inhibits basolateral OAT1/OAT3 and related tubular organic-anion pathways used to move penicillin, methotrexate, some antivirals, and many metabolites from blood into urine. Blocking secretion prolongs beta-lactam levels but can also raise toxicity from other substrates. Low-dose salicylates oppose the uricosuric effect through competing renal urate transport, while pyrazinamide promotes urate retention. Reduced GFR diminishes the filtered urate available for excretion and makes probenecid less effective.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not use in patients with uric-acid kidney stones, significant blood dyscrasias, or children younger than two years; avoid during clinically important hypersensitivity. Increased urinary urate can cause renal colic, hematuria, and stones, so assess stone risk and maintain appropriate hydration. Probenecid substantially changes renal handling of many drugs, including beta-lactams and methotrexate. Salicylates antagonize its uricosuric action. Severe renal impairment reduces efficacy. Current gout guidance does not require routine urine alkalinization for every patient even though older product labeling discusses it; individualize rather than applying a blanket instruction.

Description

Probenecid is a proximal-tubule transport inhibitor with two clinically distinct uses. On the urine-facing side of the tubular cell, it inhibits urate reabsorption through transporters including URAT1 and OAT4, so more filtered urate stays in urine and serum urate falls. On the blood-facing and secretory pathway, it inhibits organic anion transporters such as OAT1 and OAT3, slowing renal secretion of penicillin and several other drugs. Thus it can treat gout or intentionally boost another medicine's exposure. The same mechanism explains its limitations: it needs adequate filtration and urine flow, raises urinary urate enough to provoke stones or renal colic, and changes concentrations of unrelated organic acids. It lowers the chronic urate pool but does not directly quiet the neutrophilic inflammation of an acute flare.

Adverse Effects

  • Gout flares, headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, flushing, urinary frequency, and rash

  • Uric-acid stones, renal colic, hematuria, nephrotic syndrome, anaphylaxis, severe skin reaction, hepatotoxicity, hemolytic or aplastic anemia, leukopenia, and interacting-drug toxicity
