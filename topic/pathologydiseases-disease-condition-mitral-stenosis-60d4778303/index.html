Definition

Mitral stenosis develops when commissural fusion or calcific narrowing obstructs left-atrial emptying, raising pulmonary pressure and limiting ventricular filling. The characteristic pattern is exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, hemoptysis, atrial fibrillation, an opening snap, and a low-pitched diastolic rumble. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for valve area, gradient, morphology, atrial size, thrombus probability, and pulmonary pressure; distinguish it from left-atrial myxoma, cor triatriatum, severe regurgitation, and lung disease by echocardiographic anatomy and hemodynamics. The priority is to control congestion and heart rate, especially during atrial fibrillation because pulmonary edema can complicate Mitral stenosis. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for valve area, gradient, morphology, atrial size, thrombus probability, and pulmonary pressure

  • ECG and rhythm monitoring

  • transesophageal echo before valvotomy or cardioversion when left-atrial thrombus is a concern

  • Distinguish Mitral stenosis from left-atrial myxoma, cor triatriatum, severe regurgitation, and lung disease by echocardiographic anatomy and hemodynamics.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • control congestion and heart rate, especially during atrial fibrillation

  • anticoagulate when indicated for atrial fibrillation or embolic risk

  • perform balloon commissurotomy or surgery for appropriate severe symptomatic disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for acute pulmonary edema, rapid atrial fibrillation with instability, hemoptysis with respiratory distress, syncope, chest pain, or a new focal neurologic deficit because commissural fusion or calcific narrowing obstructs left-atrial emptying, raising pulmonary pressure and limiting ventricular filling.

  • Assess dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, edema, oxygenation, blood pressure, and exercise tolerance because a narrowed mitral valve raises left-atrial and pulmonary pressure before forward output falls.

  • Trend heart rate and rhythm and obtain an electrocardiogram for new palpitations because atrial fibrillation removes atrial filling support and fast rates shorten flow time across the stenotic valve.

  • Administer prescribed rate-control, diuretic, and anticoagulant therapy with blood-pressure, renal, electrolyte, and bleeding checks; avoid unreviewed fluid loading because excess volume worsens pulmonary congestion.

  • Assess for hemoptysis, infection, pregnancy, anemia, thyroid disease, and medication nonadherence because each can increase flow demand or precipitate decompensation.

  • Perform focused stroke and peripheral-embolism assessments when symptoms change and coordinate echocardiography and valve-team evaluation for progressive limitation because commissural fusion or calcific narrowing obstructs left-atrial emptying, raising pulmonary pressure and limiting ventricular filling.

Complications

  • pulmonary edema can complicate Mitral stenosis.

  • pulmonary hypertension can complicate Mitral stenosis.

  • right-heart failure can complicate Mitral stenosis.

  • atrial thrombus can complicate Mitral stenosis.

  • stroke can complicate Mitral stenosis.

  • pregnancy decompensation can complicate Mitral stenosis.

Red Flags

  • Severe dyspnea, frothy sputum, diffuse crackles, or rapidly rising oxygen need

  • Rapid irregular rhythm with hypotension, chest pain, altered mentation, or syncope

  • New unilateral weakness, aphasia, vision loss, or a cold painful limb

  • Significant hemoptysis, fever with hemodynamic change, or pregnancy-related decompensation

Patient Education

  • Explain that the narrowed valve slows blood leaving the left atrium, so fast heart rates and excess fluid quickly raise pressure backward into the lungs.

  • Teach pulse awareness, anticoagulant safety when prescribed, infection prevention, and early reporting of worsening breathlessness, palpitations, coughing blood, fainting, or stroke symptoms.

Nclex Traps

  • Tachycardia worsens stenosis by shortening diastolic filling time

  • Do not cardiovert atrial fibrillation without addressing thrombus risk
