Definition

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive coccus that commonly lives on human skin and in the nose without causing disease, yet can become an aggressive pathogen when it enters damaged skin, deeper tissue, the bloodstream, lungs, bone, joints, or implanted devices. Colonization is therefore not the same as infection: the organism may be present without tissue injury, while infection means it is multiplying in a site and provoking local or systemic harm.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    S. aureus is a bacterial species in the genus Staphylococcus. The abbreviation S. aureus names the organism; MRSA and MSSA describe methicillin-resistant and methicillin-susceptible phenotypes and must not be treated as synonyms for every S. aureus isolate. That distinction matters because resistance testing changes antibiotic choices without changing the organism's species identity.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The cells are spherical, Gram positive, non-spore-forming, nonmotile, and facultatively anaerobic. They characteristically divide in several planes and can appear as clusters. A thick peptidoglycan cell wall retains crystal violet during Gram staining, while facultative anaerobic metabolism lets the organism grow with or without oxygen. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but morphology alone cannot prove species identity or antimicrobial susceptibility.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Humans are the major reservoir, especially colonized skin and anterior nares. Transmission occurs mainly when contaminated hands, draining wounds, shared equipment, or other contaminated surfaces move the organism to a susceptible site. Endogenous infection is also common: a person's own colonizing strain can enter through a wound, catheter, surgical site, or other barrier break. Because colonization can persist, a single species-wide incubation period is not meaningful.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Disease begins when barrier disruption or a vulnerable host gives the organism access to tissue. Adhesins promote attachment, enzymes and cytotoxic factors support invasion and tissue injury, and immune-evasion factors help survival. Protein A binds the Fc portion of immunoglobulin G, orienting antibodies in a way that interferes with effective opsonization and phagocytosis. Biofilm formation on devices can further protect organisms from host defenses and make eradication difficult.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    S. aureus can cause purulent skin and soft-tissue infection, cellulitis, abscess, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, endocarditis, bloodstream infection, and sepsis. Toxin-mediated syndromes also occur, but a toxin syndrome and an invasive infection are different mechanisms. Rapid escalation is warranted for hypotension, altered mental status, rapidly spreading tissue injury, severe pain, respiratory compromise, or other sepsis findings.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    A Gram stain may show Gram-positive cocci in clusters and can guide the early differential, but culture or another validated identification method is required for species-level confirmation. Susceptibility testing is essential because an isolate's methicillin phenotype cannot be inferred from appearance. Specimens must come from the clinically relevant site; superficial colonization can otherwise be mistaken for the cause of deeper disease.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Standard precautions apply to every patient. Contact precautions may be added for MRSA or uncontrolled draining wounds according to the setting and current facility guidance. Hand hygiene, appropriate wound containment, environmental cleaning, aseptic device care, and prompt removal of unnecessary invasive devices reduce spread and entry opportunities. Antibiotics should be selected for the infection site and susceptibility result rather than for the organism name alone.
