Summary

A low-grade fever is a mild temperature elevation. A practical adult NCLEX study anchor is about 37.5-38.0 C / 99.5-100.4 F, but facility definitions, route, baseline, age, pregnancy status, immune status, and clinical context matter.

Quick Answer

A **low-grade fever** is a mild temperature elevation, commonly about **37.5-38.0 C / 99.5-100.4 F** in adult study language. It is not automatically an emergency, but it becomes important when paired with infection risk, neutropenia, immunosuppression, post-op status, pregnancy/postpartum concerns, central line or wound symptoms, or clinical deterioration.

Sections

    • Definition / range

    • Use **about 37.5-38.0 C / 99.5-100.4 F** as a practical NCLEX study anchor for low-grade fever. Some references define fever at **>=38.0 C / 100.4 F**, so always compare with facility policy, temperature route, baseline, and the client's condition.

    • Why nurses care

    • Low-grade fever can be an early cue for infection, inflammation, transfusion reaction, medication reaction, post-op complication, autoimmune flare, dehydration, heat illness, or normal variation depending on the situation. Trend it with heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, pain, mental status, WBC/ANC, cultures, wounds, lines, and symptoms.

    • High-risk clients

    • Escalate sooner for **neutropenia**, immunosuppression, chemotherapy, newborns or young infants, older adults with new confusion or weakness, pregnancy/postpartum infection symptoms, central venous catheters, fresh surgical wounds, burns, and suspected sepsis.

    • NCLEX trap

    • Do not overreact to one mild number by itself, but do not dismiss a low-grade fever when the clinical story is unsafe. The exam cares about **trend + risk + source + perfusion**, not temperature alone.
