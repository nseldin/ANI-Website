Definition

Postrenal AKI is reduced kidney function caused by obstruction of urine flow downstream from the nephrons. It usually requires bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a solitary functioning kidney, but bladder-outlet obstruction can impair both kidneys at once.

Pathology

Urine accumulates proximal to the blockage, raising tubular and Bowman-space hydrostatic pressure. Hydronephrosis and hydroureter can develop, but early obstruction, dehydration, or retroperitoneal encasement may produce little visible dilation.

Pathophysiology

Rising upstream pressure opposes glomerular filtration, while local vasoconstriction, inflammation, and tubular dysfunction deepen the GFR decline over time. Prolonged obstruction causes interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, concentrating defects, infection risk, and irreversible nephron loss. After relief, retained solute and impaired concentrating ability can drive postobstructive diuresis; large water, sodium, potassium, and magnesium losses can then cause hypotension and electrolyte disturbance even as creatinine improves.

Etiology

Causes include prostate enlargement or cancer, neurogenic bladder, urethral stricture, pelvic or retroperitoneal tumor, stones, clots, sloughed papillae, fungal balls, retroperitoneal fibrosis, catheter blockage, and surgical or radiation injury. Intermittent or partial obstruction may produce fluctuating urine output rather than complete anuria.

Risk Factors

  • Older age with prostate disease

  • Pelvic, bladder, cervical, prostate, or retroperitoneal malignancy

  • Stones, clots, recurrent retention, neurogenic bladder, or urethral disease

  • Recent pelvic surgery, radiation, instrumentation, or a solitary kidney

Signs Symptoms

  • Anuria, oliguria, intermittent flow, hesitancy, weak stream, incomplete emptying, or overflow

  • Suprapubic fullness, flank pain, renal colic, or painless retention

  • Hematuria, recurrent UTI, fever, or confusion in vulnerable older adults

  • Polyuria after decompression

Diagnostics

  • Inspect catheter tubing and perform bladder assessment or bladder scan when retention is possible

  • Use renal and bladder ultrasound for hydronephrosis, kidney size, and residual urine

  • Use CT or targeted urologic imaging when stones, mass, or retroperitoneal disease is suspected

  • Do not stop evaluation solely because hydronephrosis is absent when clinical suspicion remains high

Labs

  • Creatinine and potassium can rise rapidly in complete bilateral obstruction

  • Urinalysis may show blood, infection, crystals, or little abnormality

  • Post-decompression urine can contain high sodium and water losses

  • Serial electrolytes, magnesium, volume status, and creatinine guide postobstructive monitoring

Treatments

  • Relieve the obstruction with catheterization, stent, nephrostomy, endoscopic, surgical, or disease-specific management

  • Treat infection and sepsis promptly while securing drainage

  • Manage postobstructive fluid and electrolyte losses according to the measured trajectory

  • Use urgent kidney replacement therapy when dangerous complications cannot wait for or do not resolve with decompression

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess bladder fullness, voiding symptoms, catheter patency, prostate or pelvic history, stones, malignancy, medications, and flank pain because obstruction may occur at the bladder outlet, ureters, or collecting system.

  • Trend urine output, bladder scan, creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, pain, hematuria, and imaging because partial or bilateral obstruction can cause severe kidney injury despite intermittent urine passage.

  • Relieve lower-tract obstruction with ordered catheterization using aseptic technique and verify free drainage because a kinked, blocked, or misplaced catheter can perpetuate pressure and infection.

  • After decompression, monitor hourly urine, blood pressure, weight, sodium, potassium, magnesium, and volume status because postobstructive diuresis can cause rapid hypovolemia and electrolyte loss.

  • Escalate urgently for infected obstruction, anuria with severe hyperkalemia, uncontrolled pain, solitary-kidney obstruction, falling pressure after decompression, or persistent blockage because drainage and specialist intervention are time critical.

Complications

  • Pyelonephritis, urosepsis, or pyonephrosis

  • Postobstructive diuresis with hypovolemia and electrolyte depletion

  • Persistent hydronephrosis, tubular atrophy, and CKD

  • Bladder injury, recurrent stones, or permanent kidney loss

Contraindications

  • Do not assume a patent-looking catheter is draining without inspecting tubing, position, and bladder volume

  • Do not exclude obstruction solely from one nondilated ultrasound

  • Avoid unmonitored decompression follow-up when urine output becomes very large

Red Flags

  • Fever or sepsis with hydronephrosis, stone, or obstructed infected urine

  • Anuria with hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, or rapidly rising creatinine

  • Solitary-kidney or bilateral obstruction with worsening renal function

  • Massive postobstructive urine output with hypotension or electrolyte loss

Patient Education

  • Explain that obstruction raises pressure behind the blockage and can reduce filtration even when the kidney tissue was initially healthy.

  • Teach patients to seek urgent care for inability to urinate, fever with flank pain, reduced urine, or severe suprapubic pressure because delayed drainage can cause sepsis and permanent injury.

Nclex Traps

  • Bladder outlet obstruction can affect both kidneys

  • Hydronephrosis may be absent early

  • Relief of obstruction can trigger dangerous diuresis

  • Always check catheter obstruction before assuming new renal failure
