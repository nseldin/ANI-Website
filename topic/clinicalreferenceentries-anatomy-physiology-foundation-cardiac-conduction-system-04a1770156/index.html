Summary

The cardiac conduction system is a network of specialized excitable cells that initiates and distributes each heartbeat so the atria contract before the ventricles and the ventricles contract in a coordinated pattern. The normal route is sinoatrial node through atrial tissue to the atrioventricular node, then the His bundle, right and left bundle branches, and Purkinje network. Electrical activity triggers contraction but is not the same thing as effective mechanical pumping, which is why a rhythm can appear on a monitor without a palpable pulse.

Quick Answer

The SA node in the right atrium is the usual pacemaker. The impulse spreads across both atria, pauses physiologically at the AV node to permit ventricular filling, then crosses the fibrous skeleton through the His-Purkinje system to depolarize the ventricles rapidly from apex toward the base. Autonomic input changes firing and conduction but does not create the basic pathway. On the ECG, the P wave represents atrial depolarization, the PR interval includes atrial-to-ventricular conduction, the QRS represents ventricular depolarization, and the T wave reflects ventricular repolarization.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The sinoatrial node sits high in the right atrium near the superior vena cava. Impulses spread through atrial myocardium and preferential conduction routes, including toward the left atrium. The atrioventricular node lies low in the interatrial septal region near the tricuspid annulus. The fibrous skeleton electrically insulates atria from ventricles, leaving the AV node and His bundle as the normal bridge. The His bundle divides into right and left bundle branches along the interventricular septum; the left branch has fascicles. Purkinje fibers distribute the signal through ventricular subendocardium. The anatomy explains why injury at different levels produces distinct ECG and pacing consequences.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: why sequence matters

    Text

    Pacemaker cells slowly depolarize on their own until threshold triggers an action potential. The fastest functioning pacemaker normally suppresses slower backup sites. Atrial activation first pushes the final portion of filling into the ventricles. AV nodal delay then prevents atria and ventricles from contracting simultaneously. Fast His-Purkinje conduction activates broad ventricular myocardium nearly together, creating efficient pressure rather than a slow wave of scattered contraction. If conduction is delayed through diseased ventricular muscle, the QRS widens and contraction becomes less synchronous. If impulse generation stops, a slower junctional or ventricular escape rhythm may preserve life but often cannot support normal demand.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Sympathetic stimulation increases SA-node firing, AV conduction, and myocardial excitability through adrenergic signaling; parasympathetic vagal activity slows SA firing and AV nodal conduction. Electrolytes shape membrane voltage: potassium abnormalities can impair conduction or repolarization, calcium affects nodal action potentials and contraction, and magnesium influences electrical stability. Ischemia, hypoxia, acidosis, temperature, hormones, and medications can alter automaticity or conduction. The conduction system must be interpreted with the mechanical system: electrical timing governs when contraction occurs, while chamber filling, contractility, valves, and vascular resistance determine whether that contraction produces useful circulation.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    SA-node dysfunction may cause inappropriate bradycardia, pauses, fatigue, presyncope, or syncope. AV block ranges from delayed conduction to intermittent dropped beats to complete dissociation, where atria and ventricles follow separate pacemakers. Bundle-branch block delays activation of one ventricle and widens the QRS. Reentry can sustain tachyarrhythmia when an impulse circulates through tissue with different conduction and refractory properties. Ectopic automaticity produces premature or rapid rhythms. Very fast rates shorten filling and increase oxygen demand; very slow rates reduce beats per minute. Either can lower cardiac output, especially in a patient with limited stroke-volume reserve.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Beta blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, digoxin, adenosine, and many antiarrhythmics influence nodal firing, conduction, refractoriness, or ventricular depolarization; the same mechanism that treats one rhythm can cause bradycardia or block. Sodium-channel-blocking toxicity can widen QRS, while QT-prolonging drugs delay repolarization and raise torsades risk. Ischemia, myocarditis, infiltrative disease, fibrosis, surgery, and age-related degeneration can injure conduction tissue. Pacemakers treat selected symptomatic bradyarrhythmias or blocks by providing timed impulses; defibrillation stops certain lethal rhythms so an organized pacemaker can resume, but it does not supply ongoing circulation by itself.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    A 12-lead ECG localizes rate, rhythm, intervals, axis, chamber activation, ischemic patterns, and conduction delay at one moment. Continuous telemetry detects intermittent events but must be checked against the patient and electrode quality. Ambulatory monitors extend the observation window; electrophysiology studies map activation and may guide ablation. Electrolytes, oxygenation, acid-base status, thyroid testing, drug concentrations, and cardiac biomarkers answer causes or consequences. A pulse assessment determines whether electrical activity produces mechanical output. Artifact can resemble ventricular tachycardia, and a normal short ECG does not exclude an intermittent block or tachyarrhythmia.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    When a rhythm changes, assess responsiveness, airway, breathing, pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, chest discomfort, dyspnea, and neurologic symptoms before becoming absorbed in the monitor. Verify leads and obtain a diagnostic ECG when feasible, then compare rate, regularity, P waves, PR interval, QRS width, and QT-related risk with prior tracings. Review new medicines, dose changes, kidney or liver dysfunction, and electrolyte losses. Follow emergency algorithms for unstable rhythms, prepare pacing or cardioversion when ordered, and document symptom-rhythm correlation. For a pacemaker, assess capture, sensing, incision or access site, device precautions, and patient understanding rather than relying only on a displayed heart rate.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Treat pulseless ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation as cardiac arrest. Escalate immediately for symptomatic bradycardia with hypotension, altered mental status, ischemic chest discomfort, shock, or acute heart failure; unstable tachycardia with the same findings; new high-grade or complete AV block; prolonged pauses with syncope; or wide-complex rhythm after overdose. New conduction disturbance during myocardial infarction, myocarditis, procedure, or severe electrolyte abnormality may deteriorate rapidly. A paced rhythm with loss of capture and poor perfusion is an emergency. Never assume that an organized tracing means the patient has a pulse.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    The SA node starts the normal impulse, but the brain does not command every beat. The AV-node pause is useful, not a malfunction. The ECG records voltage changes at the body surface; it does not directly show contraction force or coronary blood flow. The QRS hides atrial repolarization and does not represent ventricular contraction itself. Asystole is not a shockable rhythm. Atrial fibrillation can produce a ventricular rate that is slow, controlled, or rapid; the atrial name alone does not determine stability. Always treat the patient, verify the rhythm, and connect an intervention to where it acts in the conduction pathway.
