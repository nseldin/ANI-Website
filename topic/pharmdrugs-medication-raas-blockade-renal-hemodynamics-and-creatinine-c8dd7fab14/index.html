Used To Treat

This card interprets creatinine and potassium after ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, or aliskiren initiation. It separates an expected hemodynamic eGFR dip from progressive AKI caused by volume depletion, NSAIDs, sepsis, severe hypotension, obstruction, or renal-artery stenosis.

Description

The glomerulus filters blood through a pressure gradient controlled partly by afferent inflow and efferent outflow. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, preserving glomerular capillary pressure when kidney perfusion falls. A RAAS blocker removes that support, so intraglomerular pressure and GFR can fall soon after treatment begins even when the drug is not directly poisoning renal cells. That early hemodynamic change can coexist with long-term kidney protection because lower intraglomerular pressure reduces albumin leakage, capillary barotrauma, and nephron scarring. The key question is magnitude and context. KDIGO notes that an eGFR fall under about 30% can often be observed while therapy continues, whereas a fall of 30% or more exceeds expected variability and warrants evaluation. The number is an evaluation threshold, not permission to ignore symptoms, oliguria, shock, or rapidly changing labs.

Mechanism

Prostaglandins help dilate the afferent arteriole; angiotensin II helps constrict the efferent arteriole. NSAIDs reduce afferent prostaglandin support, diuretics or GI losses reduce circulating volume, and RAAS blockade dilates the efferent arteriole. Combining all three can sharply lower glomerular capillary pressure, producing the triple-whammy AKI pattern. Bilateral renal-artery stenosis creates another dependence: limited inflow makes angiotensin-II-mediated efferent constriction crucial for filtration. Blocking it can unmask a large creatinine rise. Reduced aldosterone simultaneously decreases distal potassium secretion, so creatinine and potassium often change together. In stable albuminuric CKD, however, lower intraglomerular pressure reduces protein passage and long-term nephron injury, explaining why the same mechanism can be protective over months to years.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: The physiology card has no boxed warning, but the drugs it covers carry fetal-toxicity warnings. Oliguria, progressive creatinine rise, severe hyperkalemia, symptomatic hypotension, or an eGFR fall of 30% or more requires prompt clinical evaluation rather than automatic continuation or automatic permanent discontinuation.

Adverse Effects

  • Expected or manageable findings can include a modest early creatinine rise, lower eGFR, mild pressure reduction, and potassium increase.

  • Pathologic findings include progressive azotemia, oliguria, severe hyperkalemia, shock, pulmonary edema from renal failure, or dialysis-requiring AKI.

Contraindications

  • Do not use this teaching threshold to continue a drug through shock, severe volume depletion, anuria, rapidly progressive AKI, or dangerous hyperkalemia.

  • Suspected bilateral renal-artery stenosis, solitary-kidney renal-artery stenosis, severe decompensated heart failure, or concurrent nephrotoxic physiology requires individualized specialist assessment.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain baseline creatinine/eGFR and potassium, then repeat after initiation or dose change. Ask about vomiting, diarrhea, low intake, diuretics, NSAIDs, infection, urinary obstruction, and pressure symptoms.

  • Compare the new value with baseline and trend. Escalate a 30% or greater eGFR fall, major creatinine rise, oliguria, severe potassium elevation, or symptomatic hypotension while identifying reversible contributors.

Interactions

  • NSAID plus diuretic plus RAAS blocker is dangerous because each removes a different support for filtration. This is a mechanistic interaction, not merely three medications appearing on one list.

  • Potassium supplements, salt substitutes, trimethoprim, spironolactone/eplerenone, amiloride/triamterene, and CKD can magnify the aldosterone-related potassium rise.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR and potassium within about 2 to 4 weeks after initiation or dose change in stable CKD guidance, with earlier testing when illness, frailty, high baseline potassium, advanced CKD, or hypotension raises risk.

  • BUN, bicarbonate, sodium, urinalysis, urine albumin, urine output, pressure, weight, lactate, and renal imaging are selected by the clinical question.

Nclex Traps

  • ACE inhibitors can be kidney protective and still raise creatinine. Protection refers to lower long-term intraglomerular injury; the early value reflects a pressure change across the filter.

  • A 30% threshold is not a universal hold order. It prompts evaluation of cause, symptoms, trajectory, and the benefit the drug is providing.
