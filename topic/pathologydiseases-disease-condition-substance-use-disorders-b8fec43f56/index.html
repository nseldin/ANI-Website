Plain Language

Substance use disorders are treatable illnesses in which alcohol, medication, or drug use becomes hard to control and continues even though it is causing harm or serious risk. They are diagnosed from a pattern over time—not from one use, one overdose, one positive test, or a patient's character.

Why It Matters

These disorders can lead to fatal overdose, dangerous withdrawal, infection, injury, suicide, pregnancy and newborn harm, and loss of housing, work, or relationships. Identifying the exact substance and immediate syndrome changes what the nurse must do now, while rapid linkage to ongoing treatment and harm reduction lowers future risk.

Definition

Substance use disorders are a family of substance-specific diagnoses defined by a clinically significant pattern of impaired control, social or role impairment, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, or selected pharmacologic features. DSM-5-TR generally uses at least 2 qualifying features within the same 12 months and grades severity by criterion count, but exact criteria and recognized withdrawal syndromes differ by substance. ICD-11 uses categories such as harmful pattern of use and dependence with its own requirements, so DSM and ICD labels should not be treated as exact synonyms.

Pathology

There is no single lesion, screening score, toxicology result, amount, or frequency that diagnoses this entire group. Repeated exposure can change reward, stress, memory, learning, and control processes, while acute and chronic organ injury depends on the substance, formulation, potency, route, dose, duration, contaminants, co-exposures, and patient vulnerability. Physical dependence can occur during appropriate prescribed treatment and is not automatically a substance use disorder.

Pathophysiology

  • Repeated substance exposure can strengthen cue-triggered reward and habit learning, alter stress and motivation systems, and reduce control over use. These changes help explain craving and recurrence but do not make recovery impossible or remove the patient's role in shared decisions.

  • Intoxication and overdose are substance-specific: opioids and sedatives can suppress breathing; stimulants can cause severe sympathetic activation, chest pain, stroke, hyperthermia, or psychosis; inhalants can trigger fatal arrhythmia or hypoxia; and mixed exposure can obscure the expected pattern.

  • Withdrawal is also substance-specific. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium; opioid withdrawal is usually not directly fatal in a healthy adult but can cause dehydration and high overdose risk after lost tolerance; stimulant and cannabis withdrawal can bring serious depression or psychiatric risk; tobacco withdrawal is uncomfortable but not usually medically dangerous; DSM-5-TR does not define withdrawal criteria for other hallucinogen or inhalant use disorders.

  • Infection, pregnancy effects, trauma, malnutrition, organ disease, pain, psychiatric illness, and social instability can be causes, consequences, or coexisting problems. Treating one does not remove the need to assess the others.

Etiology

Etiology is multifactorial. Substance pharmacology and availability interact with genetic and developmental vulnerability, trauma and chronic stress, pain and sleep problems, mood, psychotic, attention, and anxiety disorders, family and peer context, housing and economic instability, criminalization, discrimination, and access to treatment. These factors influence risk and recovery but do not prove a diagnosis or justify blame.

Risk Factors

  • Prior substance use disorder, overdose, severe withdrawal, escalating exposure, rapid routes such as smoking or injection, polysubstance use, and return to use after tolerance has fallen

  • Concurrent opioids, alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medicines, stimulants, or other interacting substances; an unpredictable or contaminated illicit supply

  • Depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, ADHD, trauma, chronic pain, insomnia, suicide history, cognitive impairment, and another substance use disorder

  • Adolescence, pregnancy or postpartum transition, older age, lung, heart, liver, kidney, or neurologic disease, infection risk, unstable housing, incarceration, and disrupted treatment access

Signs Symptoms

  • Using more or longer than intended, unsuccessful attempts to reduce, craving, and substantial time obtaining, using, or recovering

  • Missing responsibilities, relationship conflict, giving up important activities, hazardous use, and continuing despite known physical or psychological harm

  • Tolerance or withdrawal when those features apply and are not solely expected during appropriate supervised treatment; either feature may be absent

  • Recurrent intoxication, overdose, falls, crashes, burns, infections, sleep or mood change, cognitive decline, poor nutrition, unexplained organ injury, or repeated emergency visits

  • Concealment, shame, medication or financial problems, unsafe caregiving, school or work decline, legal harm, or repeated difficulty continuing treatment

Diagnostics

  • Screen with a validated, age-appropriate instrument when available, then complete a private diagnostic interview. A positive screen identifies need for assessment and does not diagnose a disorder.

  • Name every substance, prescribed and nonprescribed formulation, route, amount, frequency, last use, source, expected effect, tolerance, withdrawal, overdose, co-use, and functional consequence; assess the substance-specific criteria during the same 12 months.

  • Separate the ongoing use disorder from current intoxication, poisoning or overdose, withdrawal, a substance-induced psychiatric disorder, prescribed physical dependence, and hazardous use that does not meet full diagnostic requirements.

  • Establish whether mood, psychotic, sleep, anxiety, or cognitive symptoms preceded use, occur only during intoxication or withdrawal, or persist during recovery. Co-occurring independent and substance-induced conditions are both possible.

  • Explain toxicology before testing when possible and interpret the assay, specimen, timing, detection window, prescribed medicines, contaminants, and false results. A positive or negative result does not measure severity, prove impairment, or replace consent-based history and examination.

Labs

  • No blood, urine, breath, hair, imaging, or organ test diagnoses the substance use disorder group. Choose testing from the immediate syndrome, suspected substance, route, co-exposures, pregnancy, and medical differential.

  • For acute illness, bedside glucose, oxygenation and ventilation assessment, ECG, electrolytes, renal and liver measures, blood gas, creatine kinase, pregnancy testing, toxicology, and trauma or chest or neurologic imaging may be indicated; do not delay stabilization for a confirmatory result.

  • Offer HIV, hepatitis B and C, sexually transmitted infection, pregnancy, tuberculosis, and other infection testing according to route and exposure, symptoms, consent, local epidemiology, and prevention needs.

  • Before a substance-specific medication, review the current substance exposure, withdrawal state, pregnancy, organ function, interactions, product label, and monitoring plan rather than applying one universal laboratory panel.

Assessment

Start with responsiveness, airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, rhythm, trauma, seizure, delirium, severe agitation, chest pain, stroke signs, infection, pregnancy concerns, suicidal or violent intent, and ability to remain safe. Then use person-first language to establish the exact exposure and timeline, prior overdose or severe withdrawal, diagnostic features, prescribed treatment, pain, psychiatric and medical comorbidity, housing, supports, patient goals, and barriers to continuous care.

Differential

Differentiate a substance use disorder from intoxication, poisoning, withdrawal, prescribed physical dependence, hazardous use without full disorder, medication adverse effects, substance-induced mood or psychotic disorder, delirium, primary psychiatric illness, seizure, head injury, stroke, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, infection, endocrine or metabolic disease, and neurocognitive disorder. A medical illness and a substance-related syndrome can coexist, so one explanation should not stop the other evaluation.

Treatments

  • Stabilize emergencies first with syndrome-specific care: airway and ventilation support, naloxone for suspected opioid respiratory depression, seizure and delirium protocols, cooling and monitored care for severe stimulant toxicity, poison or toxicology consultation for uncertain exposures, and treatment of trauma, infection, hypoglycemia, dehydration, and organ injury.

  • Use evidence-based behavioral care matched to the substance and patient, including motivational approaches, cognitive behavioral strategies, contingency management for stimulant use disorder, family-based or developmentally appropriate care, recovery coaching, and mutual-help or peer support chosen by the patient.

  • Medication is substance-specific, not a universal addiction pill. Methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone treat opioid use disorder under different exposure conditions; nicotine replacement, varenicline, and bupropion support tobacco cessation; no medication is FDA approved specifically for stimulant, cannabis, other hallucinogen, or inhalant use disorder, although selected off-label treatment may be considered by an appropriate specialist.

  • Treat co-occurring psychiatric, pain, sleep, infectious, obstetric, and chronic medical conditions at the same time. Do not require abstinence before providing primary care, wound care, infection treatment, prenatal care, housing support, or respectful nursing care.

  • Arrange a warm handoff to continuing treatment before discharge. Withdrawal management or short stabilization alone does not treat the ongoing disorder, and transitions from hospital, jail, detoxification, or residential care are high-risk periods.

Contraindications

  • Do not apply one withdrawal protocol to every substance. Abrupt alcohol or sedative cessation can be life-threatening, while other substances have different expected courses and monitoring needs.

  • Do not start naltrexone during current opioid use, physiologic opioid dependence, or acute opioid withdrawal; verify the required opioid-free transition and current product guidance.

  • Do not abruptly stop effective methadone or buprenorphine because of pregnancy, hospitalization, incarceration, recurrence, or benzodiazepine use; coordinate risk management and continuity.

  • Do not combine opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, sleep medicines, or other respiratory depressants without explicit clinical review, and do not assume a familiar illicit product has predictable contents.

  • Do not interpret treatment recurrence, refusal of counseling, prescribed dependence, or a positive test as permission for shaming, abandonment, punitive discharge, or denial of emergency care.

Safety Considerations

  • Overdose risk rises with mixed depressants, fentanyl or other contamination, using alone, prior overdose, and reduced tolerance after abstinence, hospitalization, incarceration, withdrawal treatment, or stopping medication. Provide naloxone when opioid exposure is possible and teach rescue breathing and emergency activation.

  • Injection and high-risk sexual exposure can transmit HIV, hepatitis, and bacterial infection. Offer sterile supplies and safe disposal where permitted, wound care, vaccination and testing, and prompt evaluation of fever, new murmur, severe back pain, focal weakness, or spreading infection.

  • Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, overdose intent, self-harm, violence, exploitation, trafficking, unsafe driving, access to lethal means, and dependent-person safety. Intoxication does not make a stated threat unimportant.

  • Use the least restrictive safe approach, reduce stimulation during agitation or perceptual distress, avoid unnecessary struggle during hyperthermic states, and protect staff and rescuer safety at contaminated inhalant or poisoning scenes.

  • Preserve confidentiality and explain its limits. Fear of punishment can delay disclosure of pregnancy, injection, contamination, recurrence, overdose, or unsafe relationships.

Nursing Priorities

  • Stabilize immediate medical and behavioral threats and begin substance-specific monitoring; trend breathing and ventilation, mental status, pupils when relevant, temperature, pulse, blood pressure, rhythm, glucose, hydration, urine output, pain, and neurologic findings.

  • Obtain a respectful, exact substance and medication history and document patient report, collateral source, objective findings, treatment response, capacity, pregnancy, infection exposure, suicide risk, and patient goals without labels such as addict, abuser, drug-seeking, clean, or dirty.

  • Reconcile prescribed methadone, buprenorphine, benzodiazepines, stimulants, pain medicines, and psychiatric drugs before holding, duplicating, or restarting them; verify last dose, formulation, prescriber or program, and transition plan.

  • Provide harm-reduction tools and teach-back: naloxone, avoiding solitary use and sedative mixing, sterile equipment, safer storage and disposal, impaired-driving prevention, hydration and heat warning signs, infection symptoms, and when to call emergency services.

  • Complete a warm handoff that confirms medication access, behavioral treatment, primary and psychiatric care, prenatal or pediatric follow-up when relevant, transportation, crisis contacts, housing or social support, and a rapid route back to care after recurrence.

Red Flags

  • Slow, irregular, or absent breathing; inability to awaken; cyanosis; gurgling; recurrent sedation; suspected opioid or mixed depressant overdose; or aspiration

  • Seizure, delirium, severe confusion, hallucinations with disorientation, rapidly worsening autonomic instability, or a history suggesting severe alcohol or sedative withdrawal

  • Hyperthermia, severe agitation, chest pain, severe hypertension, dangerous arrhythmia, severe headache, focal neurologic deficit, collapse, or dark urine

  • Fever with injection-site infection, new murmur, severe focal back pain, neurologic deficit, spreading tissue infection, or other possible invasive infection

  • Suicidal or violent intent, suspected intentional overdose, inability to remain safe, unsafe care of a dependent person, pregnancy with overdose or severe withdrawal, or acute obstetric symptoms

Complications

  • Fatal or nonfatal overdose, respiratory failure, aspiration, hypoxic brain injury, arrhythmia, myocardial injury, stroke, seizure, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and acute kidney injury

  • Withdrawal seizure or delirium with alcohol or sedatives; dehydration, psychiatric crisis, return to use, and overdose after lost tolerance with other substances

  • HIV, viral hepatitis, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, skin and soft-tissue infection, sexually transmitted infection, and vascular injury

  • Depression, psychosis, suicide, trauma, cognitive impairment, malnutrition, chronic lung, liver, kidney, cardiac, or neurologic disease, and pregnancy or newborn harm

  • Loss of housing, work, school, finances, custody, relationships, and treatment continuity, compounded by stigma, discrimination, and criminalization

Prognosis

Substance use disorders are treatable and often recurrent. Course depends on the substance, severity, overdose and withdrawal history, organ and psychiatric illness, pregnancy, housing, social support, and access to continuous evidence-based care. Recurrence is a reason to reassess medication, triggers, safety, supports, and level of care—not proof that treatment or the patient has failed.

Prevention

  • Use nonpunitive, age-appropriate screening and early intervention; prescribe controlled and sedating medicines for a clear indication with coordinated monitoring, secure storage, and a plan for reduction when appropriate.

  • Expand access to naloxone, medications for opioid and tobacco use disorders, contingency management and other behavioral care, sterile supplies, infection vaccination and testing, and nonstigmatizing prenatal and primary care.

  • Plan medication continuity, overdose prevention, infection care, and follow-up before every hospital, jail, detoxification, rehabilitation, or residential transition.

  • Treat pain, trauma, mood, psychosis, attention, anxiety, sleep, and social instability early so substance use is not the only available coping strategy.

Patient Education

  • A substance use disorder is a medical condition diagnosed from loss of control and harm over time. One use, one test, prescribed tolerance, or one overdose does not automatically establish it.

  • Call emergency services for slow breathing, inability to wake, seizure, severe confusion, overheating, chest pain, stroke symptoms, or suicidal behavior. Give naloxone and support breathing when opioid exposure is possible.

  • Withdrawal danger depends on the substance. Do not suddenly stop alcohol, benzodiazepines, or another sedative after regular heavy exposure without medical guidance.

  • Medication treatment is matched to the substance. Methadone or buprenorphine is treatment for opioid use disorder—not replacing one addiction with another—and withdrawal care alone is not continuing treatment.

  • After abstinence or reduced use, tolerance may fall and a previously used amount can become fatal. Avoid using alone or mixing depressants, carry naloxone when opioids may be present, and return to care early after a lapse.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and postpartum care must be nonpunitive and substance-specific. Methadone or buprenorphine is recommended for opioid use disorder and rapid opioid stopping is not routine; cannabis cessation is advised; tobacco counseling is central and medication decisions are individualized.

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate confidential screening within legal limits, family involvement when safe, school and peer context, safeguarding, and assessment of trauma, exploitation, neurodevelopmental conditions, driving, and suicide risk.

  • Older adults may show falls, confusion, medication interactions, or functional decline rather than a stereotyped presentation and need organ-function, cognition, polypharmacy, and withdrawal review.

  • Patients with serious mental illness, chronic pain, cognitive disability, unstable housing, incarceration, or limited English access need integrated care and reasonable supports rather than exclusion for ongoing use.

  • People leaving controlled settings need immediate medication continuity, naloxone, safer-use education, housing and transport support, and rapid follow-up because tolerance and treatment access can change abruptly.

Nclex Traps

  • Intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, and a substance use disorder are related but separate clinical determinations.

  • A positive toxicology result does not diagnose the disorder or prove current impairment; a negative result does not rule out an untested substance.

  • Alcohol and sedative withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium; do not apply that danger profile or treatment protocol to every substance.

  • Medication is substance-specific: no single medicine treats all substance use disorders, and withdrawal management alone is not ongoing treatment.

  • Use person-first language and treat immediate pain, infection, pregnancy, and psychiatric needs; ongoing use does not justify withholding basic care.
