Used To Treat

Emergency management of acute clinically important hyperkalemia, particularly ECG change, weakness, rapid rise, severe kidney failure, or cardiac arrest.

Description

Acute hyperkalemia depolarizes cardiac cells and can cause fatal conduction failure, so treatment must stabilize the membrane, shift potassium into cells, and remove potassium from the body. IV calcium stabilizes the cardiac membrane within minutes but does not lower serum potassium. In a patient with a pulse, insulin with glucose and an inhaled beta-2 agonist can shift potassium into cells temporarily. Dialysis, urinary excretion, gastrointestinal binders, or correction of the cause removes potassium from the body. Therefore every temporary shift needs repeat potassium and glucose monitoring and an elimination plan. Cardiac arrest is a separate evidence boundary: AHA guidance states that the effectiveness of IV calcium, IV sodium bicarbonate, and IV insulin with glucose for hyperkalemic arrest is not well established, and inhaled beta-2 agonists should not be treated as routine arrest therapy.

Mechanism

High extracellular potassium depolarizes the resting membrane potential, initially producing peaked T waves and eventually slowing conduction, widening QRS, creating a sine-wave pattern, and causing arrest. Calcium raises the threshold potential and restores the difference between resting and threshold potentials, stabilizing conduction without changing total potassium. Insulin stimulates Na/K-ATPase and beta-2 agonism increases cellular uptake, so serum potassium falls temporarily. Unless kidneys, gut, or dialysis remove potassium, it redistributes back out and the emergency recurs.

Administration Timing

  • Confirm a non-hemolyzed sample when safe, obtain ECG immediately, and give protocol-directed IV calcium without waiting for repeat testing when severe hyperkalemia has toxic ECG changes or instability.

  • For a patient with a pulse, begin protocol-directed intracellular shift with insulin/glucose and consider an inhaled beta-2 agonist as an adjunct; reserve bicarbonate for an appropriate acidemia or protocol context rather than routine non-arrest use.

  • During cardiac arrest, follow the resuscitation protocol and recognize that AHA considers the effectiveness of IV calcium, IV sodium bicarbonate, and IV insulin with glucose not well established; do not add an inhaled beta-2 agonist as routine arrest therapy.

  • Start definitive removal and repeat potassium and glucose at protocol intervals because shifting therapies wear off and insulin-related hypoglycemia can be delayed.

Nursing Essentials

  • Place the patient on continuous ECG, verify IV calcium formulation and insulin dose independently, and document glucose before treatment because medication error and hypoglycemia are preventable hazards.

  • Check glucose repeatedly for several hours and repeat potassium after shifts because an early normal value can rebound while insulin continues lowering glucose.

  • Identify and stop potassium sources and contributors such as supplements, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, mineralocorticoid antagonists, trimethoprim, tissue breakdown, or acidosis.

Key Labs

  • Repeat non-hemolyzed potassium, bedside and serial glucose, creatinine, bicarbonate or blood gas, magnesium, calcium, phosphate, CK when tissue injury is possible, and continuous ECG guide cause and recurrence.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyperkalemia causes weakness, paresthesia, conduction block, ventricular dysrhythmia, and arrest; therapy can cause calcium extravasation, hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, tachycardia, or volume overload.

Contraindications

  • Do not give insulin without a glucose-monitoring and rescue plan, and do not treat pseudohyperkalemia aggressively; toxic ECG change or arrest still requires immediate stabilization before every confirmatory result returns.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent ECG change, severe potassium elevation, oliguria, tissue breakdown, or rebound after shifting requires urgent nephrology and dialysis; arrest follows the resuscitation pathway.

Interactions

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, supplements, and succinylcholine can increase potassium or impair elimination.

  • Beta blockers blunt beta-agonist shift, while insulin, sulfonylureas, poor intake, and kidney failure increase hypoglycemia risk.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Kidney failure or missed dialysis

    Note

    Temporary shift cannot replace potassium removal, so early dialysis access and recurrence planning are central.

  • Population

    Children or low body weight

    Note

    Insulin, glucose, and calcium are weight and protocol dependent; independent calculation reduces severe medication error.

  • Population

    Diabetes, malnutrition, or low baseline glucose

    Note

    Prolonged bedside glucose surveillance is required because insulin-mediated glucose lowering can outlast its useful potassium shift, allowing hypoglycemia to appear late and persist.

Evidence Limitations

  • Emergency doses and evidence differ between cardiac arrest and patients with a pulse. AHA identifies uncertain effectiveness for IV calcium, IV sodium bicarbonate, and IV insulin with glucose during hyperkalemic arrest, while inhaled beta-2 agonist shifting belongs to patients with a pulse rather than routine arrest care; this map does not replace local adult or pediatric order sets.

Nclex Traps

  • Calcium protects the heart but does not lower potassium.

  • Insulin shifts potassium but does not remove it.

  • A normal repeat after a shift is temporary until an elimination plan succeeds.

Regulatory Status

This is an educational emergency pathway. Calcium, insulin, glucose, beta agonists, binders, and dialysis have separate U.S. indications; several acute hyperkalemia uses are guideline- and protocol-directed rather than product-labeled.

Boxed Warning

HYPERKALEMIA EMERGENCY: ECG change, severe weakness, or collapse requires immediate emergency care. Stabilize the membrane, shift potassium, and remove potassium as separate tasks; monitor glucose and repeat potassium because hypoglycemia and rebound can occur after apparent improvement.
