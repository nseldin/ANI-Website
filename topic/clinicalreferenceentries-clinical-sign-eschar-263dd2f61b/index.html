Definition

Eschar means firmly attached devitalized tissue (dead tissue), usually tan, brown, or black, covering part of a wound or injured skin.

Plain Language

Eschar is an attached layer of dead tissue over a wound.

Why It Matters

Eschar can hide the true wound depth and may result from pressure, poor blood flow, burns, or serious infection. It should not be peeled or removed casually; rapid progression, severe pain, fever, drainage, crackling under the skin, or instability requires urgent evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • adherent dead tissue produced by pressure injury, ischemia or infarction, burns, infection, vasculitis, calciphylaxis, trauma, or another cause of tissue necrosis

Assessment Method

Inspect without peeling or probing and document location, dimensions, color, dry or moist character, thickness, adherence, wound edges, drainage, odor, warmth, redness, swelling, tenderness or anesthesia, surrounding perfusion, pressure exposure, and systemic findings.

Positive Finding

Firmly adherent tan, brown, or black devitalized tissue covers part of a wound or injured skin and may conceal the wound bed and its true depth.

Mechanism

Tissue death from prolonged pressure, inadequate blood flow, thermal or chemical injury, infection, or severe inflammation leaves nonviable tissue that dries or coagulates into an adherent layer.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Eschar finding raises concern for adherent dead tissue produced by pressure injury, ischemia or infarction, burns, infection, vasculitis, calciphylaxis, trauma, or another cause of tissue necrosis because tissue death from prolonged pressure, inadequate blood flow, thermal or chemical injury, infection, or severe inflammation leaves nonviable tissue that dries or coagulates into an adherent layer. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Eschar does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Eschar to focus evaluation of adherent dead tissue produced by pressure injury, ischemia or infarction, burns, infection, vasculitis, calciphylaxis, trauma, or another cause of tissue necrosis and the reviewed alternatives: crust, dried blood, slough, hyperkeratosis, black pigmented lesion. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect without peeling or probing and document location, dimensions, color, dry or moist character, thickness, adherence, wound edges, drainage, odor, warmth, redness, swelling, tenderness or anesthesia, surrounding perfusion, pressure exposure, and systemic findings. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Eschar as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Color alone cannot identify cause, depth, infection, or viability beneath the surface; dried blood, thick crust, slough, and pigmented lesions can mimic eschar, and black eschar does not by itself diagnose anthrax or necrotizing infection. Therefore, Eschar cannot confirm or exclude adherent dead tissue produced by pressure injury, ischemia or infarction, burns, infection, vasculitis, calciphylaxis, trauma, or another cause of tissue necrosis by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • crust

  • dried blood

  • slough

  • hyperkeratosis

  • black pigmented lesion

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as crust, dried blood, slough, hyperkeratosis, black pigmented lesion.

False Negative Situations

Eschar may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of adherent dead tissue produced by pressure injury, ischemia or infarction, burns, infection, vasculitis, calciphylaxis, trauma, or another cause of tissue necrosis.

Modern Status

Current wound morphology, not a diagnosis; eschar can hide full-thickness tissue loss and make a pressure injury unstageable, while rapid progression with severe pain or systemic illness can signal limb- or life-threatening ischemia or infection. Safety Precautions

Observe Eschar with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect without peeling or probing and document location, dimensions, color, dry or moist character, thickness, adherence, wound edges, drainage, odor, warmth, redness, swelling, tenderness or anesthesia, surrounding perfusion, pressure exposure, and systemic findings. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not peel, probe, soften, or debride eschar without a specific wound and vascular plan because stable dry intact heel or ischemic-limb eschar may be intentionally left intact; urgently escalate spreading erythema, drainage, odor, fluctuance, fever, hypotension, pain out of proportion, bullae, crepitus, new numbness, or rapidly advancing discoloration.
