Plain Language

Electroconvulsive therapy, or ECT, is a medical procedure performed under general anesthesia in which a carefully controlled electrical stimulus produces a brief therapeutic seizure. It is used mainly for severe depression, bipolar depression, and catatonia when other treatment has not worked or a rapid response is needed.

Why It Matters

ECT can improve life-threatening depression, psychosis, catatonia, food or fluid refusal, and severe suicidal illness faster than many medicines, but it requires informed consent or other lawful authority, anesthesia assessment, physiologic monitoring, and honest counseling about confusion and memory loss. It is treatment—not punishment—and modern ECT uses anesthesia and muscle relaxation.

Definition

ECT is a noninvasive brain-stimulation procedure in which an ECT-trained team gives short general anesthesia and a muscle relaxant, places scalp electrodes, and delivers a controlled electrical stimulus to induce a monitored generalized cerebral seizure. The FDA class II QGH device category specifically covers catatonia or a severe major depressive episode associated with major depressive or bipolar disorder in patients age 13 and older who are treatment-resistant or need rapid response because of psychiatric or medical severity. Other clinical uses and device regulatory categories require specialist and jurisdiction-specific review.

Pathology

ECT's complete therapeutic mechanism is not known. The induced seizure produces neurochemical, network, endocrine and plasticity changes associated with improvement in severe mood and catatonic syndromes. Clinical response, not seizure appearance alone, determines benefit; stimulus dose, electrode placement, pulse width, anesthetic, medicines and individual seizure threshold affect efficacy and cognitive adverse effects.

Pathophysiology

  • The electrical stimulus is individually titrated above seizure threshold, producing a brief EEG-monitored seizure while anesthesia prevents awareness and neuromuscular blockade limits motor movement.

  • A short parasympathetic phase can slow heart rate, followed by sympathetic increases in heart rate and blood pressure; anesthesia and recovery teams anticipate and treat patient-specific cardiopulmonary risk.

  • Right unilateral and ultrabrief-pulse approaches may reduce cognitive adverse effects for some patients, while bilateral placement can be selected when efficacy or urgency outweighs that tradeoff; the ECT specialist individualizes technique.

Etiology

Not applicable as a diagnosis. ECT may be considered for severe or treatment-resistant major depressive episodes, bipolar depression, catatonia, psychotic depression, severe suicidal illness, malnutrition or refusal caused by psychiatric disease, postpartum psychosis, and selected mania, schizophrenia-spectrum or neuroleptic malignant syndrome presentations under specialist guidance.

Risk Factors

  • Severe depression with psychosis, catatonia, high suicide risk, inability to eat or drink, major functional collapse, or need for a rapid definitive response

  • Failure, intolerance or contraindication of appropriate medication and psychotherapy, or a strong previous ECT response

  • Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, intracranial, pulmonary, anesthesia, dental, aspiration, pregnancy or medication factors that increase procedural risk and require optimization

  • Baseline cognitive impairment, older age, bilateral electrode placement, treatment intensity, and individual susceptibility that may increase confusion or memory effects

Signs Symptoms

  • Persistent severe depressive symptoms, psychosis, stupor, mutism, posturing, refusal of food or fluids, profound psychomotor slowing, or life-threatening suicidal intent despite other care

  • Catatonia with immobility, mutism, negativism, posturing, echophenomena, or purposeless agitation after medical and neurologic causes are assessed

  • During recovery: temporary confusion, disorientation, headache, nausea, jaw or muscle soreness, fatigue, and memory difficulty

  • Concerning complications: prolonged seizure, delayed awakening, persistent hypoxia, aspiration, severe hypertension or hypotension, arrhythmia, chest pain, focal neurologic deficit, fall, or severe persistent confusion

Diagnostics

  • Confirm the target diagnosis, severity, urgency, prior treatment quality and response, alternatives, expected benefits, cognitive risks, relapse-prevention plan, consent or legal authority, and the patient's values.

  • Complete psychiatric, medical and anesthesia assessment with medication reconciliation, cardiovascular and neurologic history and examination, airway and dental review, baseline vital signs and cognitive or memory assessment, and pregnancy assessment when relevant.

  • Use ECG, CBC, electrolytes, kidney or other laboratory tests, imaging or specialist consultation according to age, comorbidity, anesthesia protocol and findings; there is no one universal pre-ECT laboratory panel.

  • Record stimulus parameters, electrode placement, anesthetic and muscle relaxant, EEG and motor seizure quality, physiologic response, recovery, adverse effects, and clinical response across sessions.

  • Assess orientation, new learning and autobiographical memory during the course and adjust technique, frequency or treatment plan when cognitive harm outweighs expected benefit.

Labs

  • Check tests required by the anesthesia and ECT protocol and the patient's conditions; commonly considered information includes electrolytes, glucose, kidney function, CBC, ECG, and pregnancy status when relevant, but requirements vary.

  • Correct important hypoxia, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, unstable glucose, untreated infection, intoxication, withdrawal or other reversible anesthesia and seizure risks before proceeding unless the specialist team determines an emergency benefit outweighs delay.

  • When lithium is continued or recently used, consider level, kidney function, hydration, interactions and risk of delirium or prolonged neuromuscular blockade under the psychiatrist and anesthesia plan.

Assessment

Before each treatment verify identity, consent or legal authority, target symptoms, interval medical change, NPO status under the anesthesia protocol, medicines taken or held by order, allergies, airway and dental concerns, pregnancy information when relevant, baseline vital signs and cognition, IV access and transport. During treatment monitor ECG, blood pressure, oxygenation, ventilation, EEG and motor response. Afterward monitor airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness, orientation, pain, nausea, injury, gait and memory until recovery criteria are met.

Differential

  • ECT versus repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, ketamine or esketamine, medication augmentation, psychotherapy, vagus nerve stimulation and other options that differ in urgency, anesthesia, seizure induction, evidence and adverse effects

  • Catatonia versus delirium, medication-induced parkinsonism, akinetic mutism, severe depression, neurologic disease and neuroleptic malignant syndrome

  • Expected brief recovery confusion versus delirium, prolonged seizure, hypoxia, stroke, medication toxicity or another acute neurologic event

  • Depressive pseudodementia, baseline neurocognitive disorder and treatment-related memory change, requiring repeated objective and patient-reported assessment

Treatments

  • A typical acute course is often given two or three times weekly for roughly 6 to 12 treatments, but number, frequency, electrode placement, pulse width and stopping point are individualized to response, urgency and adverse effects rather than fixed universally.

  • Use short general anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade with full oxygenation and monitoring. Representative agents include methohexital for brief anesthesia and succinylcholine for muscle relaxation when appropriate; propofol, etomidate, rocuronium or other alternatives may be selected for patient-specific seizure, cardiac, potassium or anesthesia considerations.

  • Continue or adjust antidepressant, mood-stabilizing, antipsychotic or other treatment according to diagnosis and the ECT team's plan; never independently hold lithium, benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants or cardiovascular medicines because effects and risks differ.

  • After acute response, use continuation pharmacotherapy, continuation or maintenance ECT, psychotherapy, or a combination to reduce relapse; the schedule is based on diagnosis, recurrence risk, prior response and preference.

  • Provide symptom, suicide, nutrition, catatonia, function and cognitive monitoring so treatment stops, changes or continues based on meaningful clinical outcomes.

Contraindications

  • Do not perform ECT without informed consent or other applicable lawful authorization and documentation; incapacity requires the jurisdiction's substitute-decision or court process except where a valid emergency pathway applies.

  • Do not treat ECT as punishment, behavioral control, a shortcut around diagnosis, or an automatic last step after one medication failure.

  • Do not proceed without specialist risk review and optimization for unstable cardiovascular disease, recent myocardial or cerebrovascular event, increased intracranial pressure or mass effect, unstable aneurysm, severe pulmonary disease, retinal risk, active delirium, or other major anesthesia concern.

  • Avoid succinylcholine when hyperkalemia or a condition causing exaggerated potassium release or malignant-hyperthermia susceptibility makes it unsafe; use an anesthesia-selected alternative rather than omitting muscle protection without a plan.

  • Do not promise that memory will be unaffected or permanently lost. Explain common short-term confusion and memory effects, possible persistent autobiographical gaps, technique tradeoffs, and monitoring.

Safety Considerations

  • Use a properly equipped treatment and recovery area with an ECT-credentialed prescriber, anesthesia professional, trained nursing team, oxygen, suction, airway and resuscitation equipment, physiologic and EEG monitoring, and an emergency plan.

  • Verify NPO and medication instructions, but withhold only what the authorized anesthesia or ECT plan specifies; unplanned withdrawal of benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants, lithium or other medicines can create harm.

  • Protect teeth and soft tissue, monitor ventilation until neuromuscular function returns, and manage expected autonomic blood-pressure and heart-rate changes.

  • After outpatient ECT, require responsible transport and follow the facility's restrictions on driving, operating machinery, alcohol, signing major documents and being alone after anesthesia.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm target symptoms, consent or legal authority, treatment number, allergies, interval medical and medication changes, NPO status, pregnancy information when relevant, baseline vital signs, cognition, IV access and transport.

  • Provide simple respectful teaching: the patient is anesthetized, receives a muscle relaxant, does not feel the stimulus, and the visible motor seizure is minimized while EEG monitors brain seizure activity.

  • During and after treatment monitor airway, oxygenation, ventilation, ECG, blood pressure, pulse, level of consciousness, orientation, seizure duration per protocol, pain, nausea, injury and fall risk.

  • Compare cognition and memory with baseline, invite patient and family observations with consent, and report worsening confusion, functional memory loss, delirium or neurologic findings before the next session.

  • Ensure relapse-prevention medication, follow-up, maintenance plan, suicide reassessment, nutrition and hydration plan, and discharge transportation are not omitted after improvement.

Red Flags

  • Persistent hypoxia or apnea, difficult ventilation, aspiration, chest pain, serious arrhythmia, extreme blood-pressure change, collapse or delayed neuromuscular recovery

  • Prolonged or recurrent seizure, failure to awaken as expected, new focal neurologic deficit, severe headache with neurologic change, or suspected stroke

  • Severe persistent confusion, delirium, marked functional memory loss, fall, agitation, mania or worsening suicidal state between treatments

  • Absent consent or legal authority, wrong patient or treatment parameters, violated NPO or medication plan, unrecognized hyperkalemia or malignant-hyperthermia risk, or no safe transport

Complications

  • Temporary confusion, disorientation, headache, nausea, muscle or jaw soreness, fatigue and transient blood-pressure or heart-rate changes

  • Anterograde memory difficulty during the course and retrograde autobiographical memory loss; most improves, but some gaps can be persistent and clinically important

  • Rare prolonged seizure, status epilepticus, aspiration, dental or soft-tissue injury, arrhythmia, cardiac or neurologic event, anesthesia reaction, malignant hyperthermia or death

  • Relapse of depression, catatonia or mood illness after an effective acute course when continuation treatment and follow-up are absent

Prognosis

ECT can produce rapid and substantial improvement, especially in severe depression, psychotic depression and catatonia, but individual response and cognitive effects vary. Improvement often begins during the first treatment weeks and must be measured against the target syndrome. Relapse is common without continuation treatment; maintenance medicine, psychotherapy or ECT may be needed.

Prevention

  • Use accurate public and consent education, baseline cognitive assessment, individualized electrode and pulse strategy, medication and anesthesia review, and repeated benefit-harm monitoring.

  • Treat severe mood illness before malnutrition, immobility, psychosis or suicidal risk becomes life-threatening while preserving shared decision making whenever possible.

  • Create a continuation and relapse-prevention plan before the acute course ends, including early warning signs, medicines, psychotherapy, maintenance ECT when indicated and rapid re-entry to care.

Patient Education

  • Modern ECT is performed under general anesthesia with a muscle relaxant. You are unconscious and do not feel the electrical stimulus; the team supports breathing and monitors your heart, blood pressure and brain seizure activity.

  • Ask why ECT is recommended now, the alternatives, expected number and frequency, electrode placement, likely benefit, memory risks, consent rules, and the plan to prevent relapse.

  • Short-term confusion, headache, nausea, soreness and memory trouble can occur. Report missing important personal memories, worsening confusion, chest pain, breathing problems, severe headache, weakness, persistent vomiting or worsening mood urgently.

  • Follow NPO, medicine, transportation and post-anesthesia instructions exactly. Do not independently stop a psychiatric, seizure, blood-pressure or blood-thinning medicine before treatment.

Special Populations

  • Adults with older age or medical frailty may respond well when medicines are poorly tolerated, but require careful cardiovascular, cognitive, fall, delirium, hydration and anesthesia planning.

  • FDA's class II QGH device category includes specified catatonia or severe depressive indications in patients age 13 and older who are treatment-resistant or need rapid response; adolescent use also requires developmental, family, consent and local legal expertise.

  • Pregnancy is not an automatic exclusion, but requires obstetric-anesthesia-psychiatry planning, positioning, aspiration, fetal and obstetric assessment appropriate to gestation, and comparison with untreated-illness risk.

  • Postpartum psychosis, psychotic depression, catatonia or severe suicidal illness may warrant earlier ECT consideration when rapid response is crucial; lactation and infant-care plans still need individualized coordination.

Nclex Traps

  • ECT is performed under anesthesia with muscle relaxation; the patient does not remain awake for an unmodified convulsion.

  • Severe, psychotic, suicidal or catatonic illness may justify ECT for rapid response; it is not reserved only after every possible medicine fails.

  • Memory loss must be taught and monitored honestly; it is neither guaranteed to be permanent nor safe to dismiss as always brief.

  • NPO and medication holds follow the anesthesia and ECT order; never independently hold lithium, benzodiazepines or anticonvulsants.

  • Acute response requires continuation treatment and relapse planning.
