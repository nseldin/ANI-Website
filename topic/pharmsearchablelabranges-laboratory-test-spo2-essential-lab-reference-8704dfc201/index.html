Name

SpO2

Range

SpO2 is a pulse oximeter's noninvasive estimate of arterial hemoglobin oxygen saturation, shown as a percentage; it is not a direct PaO2 measurement. The AARC 2022 acute-care guideline supports 94-98% for most hospitalized adults requiring supplemental oxygen and 88-92% for adults with COPD requiring supplemental oxygen (evidence level C). Those are context-specific adult oxygen-therapy targets, not universal normal values or automatic escalation cutoffs. Use the ordered and facility target, accounting for diagnosis, chronic baseline, age and population, altitude, oxygen device, and the reliability of the displayed signal.

Why

An abnormal or falling SpO2 result signals a change in estimated arterial oxygen saturation and raises concern for hypoxemia when it fits the symptoms and a reliable waveform; this can change oxygen titration and respiratory assessment. Important associations include pneumonia or pulmonary edema, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, airway or device problems, hypoventilation that has progressed to hypoxemia, low inspired oxygen, and poor peripheral perfusion or artifact. Promptly assess airway, respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, mental status, skin color, breath sounds, perfusion, oxygen source and connections, ordered target, probe position, pulse agreement, waveform or signal quality, and the trend rather than treating the display alone. Warm or reposition a poorly perfused site and repeat the measurement according to device and facility instructions; do not delay care for a symptomatic patient while troubleshooting. Cause-directed evaluation can include repeat pulse oximetry at a reliable site, an ABG for PaO2 and PaCO2, or co-oximetry when precise oxygenation or a dyshemoglobin is a concern, plus clinician-selected chest imaging or cardiopulmonary testing. Escalate immediately for respiratory distress, cyanosis, altered consciousness, shock, rapidly increasing oxygen need, or rapid deterioration, regardless of whether one displayed value looks reassuring. One SpO2 value does not diagnose the cause or measure PaCO2, ventilation, hemoglobin concentration, or tissue oxygen delivery; supplemental oxygen can delay desaturation during hypoventilation. FDA notes that poor circulation, skin pigmentation, skin thickness or temperature, tobacco use, and nail polish can reduce accuracy, so reconcile the number with symptoms and another measurement method when they disagree.

High Causes

  • Supplemental oxygen or recovery from hypoxemia; compare with the ordered target rather than pursuing the highest possible saturation

  • A falsely reassuring reading from device or probe limitations, including selected dyshemoglobins that standard pulse oximetry does not distinguish

  • Adequate arterial saturation despite a separate ventilation, hemoglobin-content, perfusion, or tissue-utilization problem that SpO2 cannot measure

Low Causes

  • True hypoxemia from lung disease, airway compromise, low inspired oxygen, hypoventilation, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, diffusion impairment, or shunt

  • Poor peripheral perfusion, motion, a misplaced probe, cold skin, nail products, or an unreliable signal

  • A target intentionally individualized below common adult examples, which still requires confirmation against the order and clinical context

Critical Concerns

  • Treat respiratory distress, cyanosis, altered consciousness, shock, or rapid deterioration as urgent even when a pulse oximeter value appears acceptable.

  • A falling trend with increasing oxygen need or work of breathing can signal worsening respiratory failure before a single universal cutoff is reached.

  • Do not titrate oxygen to a generic target when a diagnosis-specific or prescribed target applies.

Nursing Considerations

  • Verify probe placement, pulse agreement, waveform or signal quality, skin temperature and perfusion, oxygen source and connections, and the ordered target before acting on an unexpected number.

  • Assess the patient and respiratory trend first; repeat at a reliable site or obtain ordered ABG or co-oximetry when the number and clinical picture disagree.

  • Document saturation, oxygen device and flow or FiO2, activity or sleep state, signal quality, symptoms, intervention, and response.

Nclex Traps

  • SpO2 screens oxygen saturation; it does not measure ventilation or PaCO2, so a sedated patient can hypoventilate before desaturation appears.

  • A COPD oxygen target in one adult acute-care guideline is not a universal normal range for every patient with COPD.

  • Do not rely on the pulse oximeter alone when symptoms, perfusion, waveform quality, or a possible dyshemoglobin makes the reading questionable.
