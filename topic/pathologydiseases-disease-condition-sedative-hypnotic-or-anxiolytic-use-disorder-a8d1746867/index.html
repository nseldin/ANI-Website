Plain Language

Sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic use disorder is a treatable illness in which medicines or drugs that slow the nervous system—such as benzodiazepines, some sleep medicines, or barbiturates—are used in a hard-to-control pattern that causes harm. Needing a slow taper after taking a prescribed benzodiazepine does not by itself mean the person has this disorder.

Why It Matters

These substances can cause falls, coma, and fatal breathing suppression, especially with opioids or alcohol, and abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures or delirium. Nurses must recognize overdose and dangerous withdrawal while avoiding stigma and unsafe sudden discontinuation of a prescribed medicine.

Definition

Sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic use disorder is a problematic pattern of use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR diagnosis requires at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months and specifies severity. Tolerance and withdrawal that occur only during appropriate medical treatment do not count by themselves, and physical dependence must not be mislabeled as a use disorder.

Pathology

No single toxicology result or prescribed dose establishes this disorder. Repeated central nervous system depressant exposure can produce tolerance and physical dependence; impairment and organ injury arise through sedation, reduced coordination, respiratory depression, aspiration, falls, interactions, and withdrawal-related autonomic and neurologic overactivity.

Pathophysiology

  • Benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics, barbiturates, and related agents enhance inhibitory signaling by different mechanisms and with different half-lives. Combined exposure with opioids, alcohol, or other sedatives can suppress consciousness and breathing more than one drug alone.

  • Intoxication commonly causes slurred speech, poor coordination, unsteady gait, impaired memory and judgment, sleepiness, and sometimes disinhibition; severe poisoning can cause coma, aspiration, low blood pressure, and respiratory failure.

  • Withdrawal after physical dependence can cause anxiety, insomnia, tremor, sweating, nausea, perceptual disturbance, tachycardia, hypertension, confusion, seizures, or delirium. Timing and severity depend on the agent, half-life, dose, duration, rate of reduction, co-use, and patient vulnerability.

Etiology

Risk can arise from nonmedical use, escalating prescribed use, multiple prescribers, severe anxiety or insomnia, trauma, other substance use, rapid-onset formulations, long exposure, and abrupt care transitions. A patient may be physically dependent without impaired control or harm, or may have both dependence and a use disorder; each question must be assessed separately.

Risk Factors

  • Long-term or high-dose exposure, rapid dose escalation, short-acting agents, prior severe withdrawal, seizure history, or abrupt interruption

  • Concurrent opioids, alcohol, gabapentinoids, muscle relaxants, antihistamines, or other sedating medicines; lung or sleep-related breathing disease

  • Older age, falls, cognitive impairment, liver or renal disease, pregnancy, polypharmacy, and limited medication continuity

  • Anxiety, insomnia, trauma, depression, suicide risk, prior substance use disorder, and unstable housing or fragmented prescribing

Signs Symptoms

  • Impaired control, craving, unsuccessful efforts to reduce, substantial time obtaining or recovering, role failure, risky use, and continued use despite physical, cognitive, relationship, or occupational harm

  • Intoxication with slurred speech, unsteady gait, poor coordination, nystagmus, impaired attention or memory, disinhibition, sleepiness, or slowed breathing

  • Withdrawal with anxiety, insomnia, irritability, tremor, sweating, nausea, sensory sensitivity, perceptual change, tachycardia, high blood pressure, confusion, seizure, or delirium

  • Repeated falls, motor-vehicle events, early refills, dose escalation, use outside the prescribed plan, multiple sources, or combining with alcohol or opioids

Diagnostics

  • Reconcile every sedative, hypnotic, anxiolytic, alcohol, opioid, and other depressant: generic name, formulation, dose, schedule, duration, prescriber, last dose, adherence, nonmedical sources, co-use, prior tapers, seizures, delirium, overdose, and functional harm.

  • Apply the DSM-5-TR use-disorder criteria only after separating impaired control and harm from expected tolerance or withdrawal during appropriate medical treatment. Document the qualifying features and 12-month time frame.

  • Assess current intoxication, overdose, and withdrawal separately. A withdrawal score can help trend a patient under an appropriate protocol but does not diagnose the use disorder or replace agent-specific history.

  • Interpret toxicology by the exact assay: routine screens may miss some benzodiazepines or hypnotics, detect metabolites rather than the parent drug, and remain positive after impairment has resolved.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses the disorder. In suspected overdose, prioritize respiratory and neurologic assessment, glucose, ECG, electrolytes, renal and liver measures, blood gas, acetaminophen and salicylate testing, and other co-exposure evaluation as indicated.

  • In withdrawal, assess glucose, electrolytes, renal and liver function, pregnancy, ECG risk, hydration, infection, trauma, and other withdrawal syndromes according to severity and setting.

  • Prescription records and toxicology can support medication reconciliation but cannot distinguish prescribed use, physical dependence, diversion, and a use disorder without clinical context.

Assessment

First determine whether the patient has inadequate ventilation, aspiration, shock, trauma, hypoglycemia, mixed overdose, seizure, delirium, or suicidal ingestion. Then establish the exact agent and last dose, prescribed versus nonmedical pattern, co-depressants, degree of physical dependence, withdrawal history, diagnostic features, pregnancy, fall risk, and whether the current setting can monitor a safe taper.

Differential

Differentiate intoxication from opioid or alcohol toxicity, stroke, head injury, hypoglycemia, infection, hepatic encephalopathy, and other causes of depressed consciousness. Differentiate withdrawal from alcohol withdrawal, stimulant intoxication, serotonin toxicity, thyroid disease, panic, mania, delirium, and epilepsy. Distinguish physical dependence from use disorder while allowing both to coexist.

Treatments

  • For benzodiazepine physical dependence, use a gradual, individualized, clinician-supervised taper when ongoing risks outweigh benefits. The pace may need pauses or smaller reductions; selected patients may transition to a longer-acting benzodiazepine such as diazepam, but this is not appropriate for everyone.

  • Use a monitored inpatient or medically managed setting for severe or complicated withdrawal, prior withdrawal seizure or delirium, unstable medical or psychiatric illness, or inability to taper safely as an outpatient. Treat seizures and delirium immediately under protocol.

  • There is no FDA-approved medication that by itself treats this use disorder. Pair the taper or acute stabilization plan with behavioral treatment, insomnia and anxiety care, treatment of co-occurring substance use, recovery support, and coordinated prescribing.

  • For overdose, provide airway and ventilation support, monitoring, and co-ingestant treatment. Give naloxone when opioid exposure is possible; it reverses opioids, not benzodiazepines. Any use of flumazenil is toxicology- or emergency-clinician-directed because it can precipitate seizures or withdrawal in high-risk or mixed exposures.

Contraindications

  • Do not abruptly stop a benzodiazepine in a physically dependent patient unless an immediate life-threatening reason and monitored specialist plan require it; rapid reduction can cause seizures and delirium.

  • Do not combine sedatives with alcohol, opioids, or other central nervous system depressants without an explicit prescriber review; the combination can cause fatal respiratory depression.

  • Do not use flumazenil routinely for an unknown or mixed overdose, chronic benzodiazepine exposure, seizure risk, or suspected proconvulsant co-ingestion; obtain toxicology or emergency guidance.

  • Do not diagnose addiction solely because a patient needs a slow taper, and do not continue an unsafe prescription solely to avoid discussing dependence and alternatives.

Safety Considerations

  • Monitor airway, breathing, oxygenation and ventilation, consciousness, blood pressure, aspiration, and recurrent sedation. Pulse oximetry can appear acceptable while ventilation is failing, especially with supplemental oxygen.

  • Use fall, driving, medication-security, and delirium precautions; verify doses across prescribers and pharmacies and avoid duplicate administration during transitions.

  • Withdrawal risk can rise after missed doses or formulary interruption. Communicate the taper and last administered dose clearly at admission, transfer, and discharge.

  • Assess suicide risk and intent in every unexplained overdose or escalating-use pattern; access to large sedative supplies can increase lethality, especially with opioids or alcohol.

Nursing Priorities

  • In overdose, support airway and ventilation, place the patient on appropriate monitoring, check glucose, prevent aspiration and falls, identify co-exposures, and escalate recurrent or worsening sedation.

  • In withdrawal, trend vital signs, orientation, tremor, perceptual symptoms, sleep, agitation, seizure activity, fluids, and medication timing; reduce stimulation and maintain seizure and delirium precautions when indicated.

  • Reconcile the exact product and last dose with the patient, prescription source, caregiver when appropriate, and pharmacy; document physical dependence separately from qualifying use-disorder features.

  • Teach and document the taper plan, missed-dose instructions, interaction risks, secure storage, emergency symptoms, follow-up prescriber, and who will manage changes after discharge.

Red Flags

  • Slow or shallow breathing, inability to awaken, cyanosis, recurrent apnea, aspiration, shock, or suspected opioid, alcohol, or other mixed overdose

  • New seizure, delirium, hallucinations, severe autonomic instability, escalating agitation, or rapidly worsening confusion after dose reduction or missed doses

  • Head injury, focal neurologic deficit, severe hypoglycemia, fever, or another medical cause that could be hidden by sedation

  • Pregnancy with severe withdrawal, suicidal ingestion, violent intent, unsafe access to medications, or inability to follow a safe taper setting

Complications

  • Respiratory failure, aspiration pneumonia, coma, hypoxic injury, falls, fractures, motor-vehicle injury, and death, especially with opioids or alcohol

  • Withdrawal seizures, delirium, trauma, dehydration, autonomic instability, and return to uncontrolled use after an overly rapid taper

  • Cognitive impairment, memory problems, daytime sedation, functional decline, relationship or occupational harm, and medication diversion

  • Fragmented care, duplicate prescribing, undertreated anxiety or insomnia, suicide, and stigma from confusing dependence with addiction

Prognosis

Improvement is possible with coordinated, gradual care, but a taper may take months or longer and symptoms can fluctuate. Success is safer function and reduced harm, not meeting an arbitrary deadline. Recurrent impaired use needs ongoing treatment, while persistent cognitive, sleep, or anxiety symptoms require reassessment for comorbid illness and medication effects.

Prevention

  • Use a clear indication, lowest effective exposure, regular benefit-risk review, one coordinated prescribing plan, and non-drug insomnia or anxiety treatments when appropriate.

  • Teach before prescribing that physical dependence can develop and that the medicine should not be stopped suddenly or combined with alcohol or opioids.

  • Maintain accurate medication reconciliation and taper continuity during hospitalization, surgery, incarceration, rehabilitation, and changes in prescriber.

Patient Education

  • Physical dependence means your body has adapted; it does not automatically mean addiction, but stopping suddenly can still be dangerous.

  • Do not change the dose or stop on your own. A safe taper is individualized and may need to slow or pause.

  • Call emergency services for slow breathing, inability to wake, a seizure, severe confusion, or hallucinations. Naloxone helps only if an opioid is involved.

  • Avoid alcohol and unreviewed sedating medicines, do not drive while impaired, and keep medicines locked away from children and others.

Special Populations

  • Older adults have greater fall, delirium, cognitive, and accumulation risk; review ongoing need and taper slowly with attention to organ function and polypharmacy.

  • Pregnancy and lactation require coordinated obstetric, pediatric, psychiatric, and prescribing review; both ongoing exposure and abrupt withdrawal can cause harm, so do not make sudden changes without a plan.

  • Patients using opioids need naloxone and coordinated risk reduction. Benzodiazepine use raises respiratory risk but should not automatically exclude evidence-based opioid use disorder medication.

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate confidentiality, caregiver involvement when safe, assessment of diversion and accidental ingestion, and treatment of anxiety, sleep, trauma, and other substance use.

Nclex Traps

  • Physical dependence from prescribed benzodiazepine use is not automatically a sedative use disorder.

  • Sedative withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium; abrupt discontinuation is unsafe in a dependent patient.

  • Naloxone reverses an opioid co-exposure, not benzodiazepine poisoning itself.

  • Flumazenil is not a routine antidote for an unknown or mixed overdose because it can precipitate seizures or withdrawal.
