Contraindications

  • Renal or hepatic impairment combined with a P-gp or strong CYP3A4 inhibitor; product hypersensitivity.

  • Clarify eGFR, hepatic disease, age/frailty, CBC abnormalities, neuromuscular disease, recent interacting medicine within the label-specified window, statins/fibrates, and exact time of flare onset.

Nursing Essentials

  • For acute gout, use the current low-dose regimen early; repeating obsolete high-dose schedules adds GI and systemic toxicity without better relief.

  • Before administration, reconcile CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors and kidney/liver function. Treat unexplained diarrhea, weakness, numbness, bruising, or fever as possible toxicity, not routine gout.

Interactions

  • Strong or moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors and P-gp inhibitors can require avoidance, interruption, or major dose reduction; the combination is contraindicated with renal or hepatic impairment.

  • Statins, fibrates, cyclosporine, and other myotoxic drugs increase neuromyopathy/rhabdomyolysis risk; digoxin and other P-gp-related combinations warrant added vigilance.

Key Labs

  • CBC, creatinine/eGFR, AST/ALT and bilirubin; CK and urinalysis when weakness, pain, or dark urine occurs

  • Medication-interaction review, GI toxicity, neuromuscular examination, infection signs, flare timing, and response

Nclex Traps

  • Colchicine treats crystal-triggered inflammation but does not lower urate; allopurinol, febuxostat, or a uricosuric addresses the long-term urate pool.

  • Diarrhea is common but can also be the earliest dose-limiting toxicity signal; escalating through it is unsafe.

Used To Treat

Low-dose treatment of an acute gout flare, prevention of flares when urate-lowering therapy is started or adjusted, and familial Mediterranean fever; product-specific indications vary. It is not a general analgesic and does not lower serum urate or dissolve monosodium urate crystals.

Mechanism

Colchicine binds soluble beta-tubulin and forms a complex that prevents addition of tubulin units to microtubules; at higher intracellular concentrations it can promote microtubule depolymerization. Neutrophils then lose polarized movement, endothelial adhesion, degranulation, and efficient trafficking toward monosodium urate crystals. Microtubule disruption also interferes with assembly and signaling of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which normally activates caspase-1 to cleave pro-IL-1 beta into inflammatory IL-1 beta. The result is less neutrophil recruitment and less amplification of the flare. Colchicine is a CYP3A4 and P-gp substrate with enterohepatic and renal handling. CYP3A4 inhibition reduces metabolism, P-gp inhibition reduces intestinal and biliary efflux, and renal/hepatic impairment reduces reserve; combining these factors can abruptly raise intracellular toxicity. Hemodialysis does not meaningfully remove colchicine.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Fatal accidental and intentional overdoses and fatal drug interactions have occurred. Colchicine with a P-gp or strong CYP3A4 inhibitor is contraindicated in patients with renal or hepatic impairment. Review clarithromycin, azoles, protease inhibitors, cyclosporine, verapamil/diltiazem, grapefruit, and other inhibitors before every course. Stop and evaluate severe or persistent diarrhea/vomiting, muscle pain or weakness, neuropathy, bruising, infection, or cytopenia. Keep away from children; there is no simple home antidote and dialysis does not remove it.

Description

Colchicine is a narrow-therapeutic-index microtubule inhibitor that binds beta-tubulin, blocks neutrophil trafficking and NLRP3-IL-1 beta amplification during gout flares, and reduces inflammation without lowering serum urate. Disrupted microtubule assembly impairs neutrophil migration, adhesion, and granule release. That is why a low dose given early in a gout flare can reduce pain and swelling, but the drug does not remove the urate burden that caused the attack. Rapidly dividing intestinal and marrow cells also depend on microtubules, so diarrhea can be the first toxicity signal and overdose can progress to pancytopenia, shock, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, and multiorgan collapse. P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 normally limit exposure; blocking both can make a therapeutic dose fatal, especially when kidney or liver clearance is impaired.

Adverse Effects

  • Dose-limiting diarrhea, abdominal cramping, nausea, vomiting, and pharyngolaryngeal discomfort

  • Myelosuppression/pancytopenia, neuromyopathy, rhabdomyolysis, peripheral neuropathy, hepatotoxicity, arrhythmia, shock, alopecia, multiorgan failure, and death
