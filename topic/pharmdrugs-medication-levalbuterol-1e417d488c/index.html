Used To Treat

Treatment or prevention of bronchospasm in patients with reversible obstructive airway disease using product- and age-specific inhaled formulations. Like racemic albuterol, it is a rescue bronchodilator and not anti-inflammatory asthma control.

Mechanism

Levalbuterol binds beta2 receptors, activates Gs and adenylyl cyclase, raises intracellular cyclic AMP, and activates protein kinase A. PKA reduces myosin light-chain kinase activity and calcium-dependent smooth-muscle contraction, opening the bronchial lumen. Because beta2 selectivity is relative rather than absolute and the human heart also contains beta2 receptors, systemic exposure can increase heart rate and provoke arrhythmia. Skeletal-muscle beta2 signaling stimulates Na+/K+-ATPase and shifts potassium into cells. The R-enantiomer has far greater beta2 activity than S-albuterol, but a clinically delivered dose still activates the same receptor pathway as albuterol; stereochemical purity does not eliminate pathway-based toxicity.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Life-threatening paradoxical bronchospasm can occur, especially with a new canister or formulation. Cardiovascular effects, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, or hypersensitivity require reassessment. More frequent use or declining response can indicate deteriorating asthma; do not increase dose or frequency without evaluating the anti-inflammatory control plan and other causes of dyspnea.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity to levalbuterol, racemic albuterol, or product components

  • Use caution in cardiovascular disease, tachyarrhythmia, hyperthyroidism, seizure disorder, diabetes, and susceptibility to hypokalemia

Nursing Essentials

  • Do not convert albuterol and levalbuterol doses by puff count alone; verify concentration, device, age, and prescribed dose.

  • Reassess objective airflow and work of breathing after use, and track rescue frequency exactly as with albuterol.

Interactions

  • Nonselective beta blockers oppose bronchodilation; other sympathomimetics add cardiovascular and metabolic toxicity.

  • MAO inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, non-potassium-sparing diuretics, and arrhythmogenic drugs require risk review.

Key Labs

  • Symptoms, peak flow or spirometry, heart rate/rhythm, oxygenation, and inhaler or nebulizer technique

  • Potassium, glucose, lactate, and ECG during repeated high doses or suspected toxicity

Nclex Traps

  • Levalbuterol is R-albuterol, not a different receptor class.

  • Purified enantiomer does not guarantee less tachycardia for a given patient or allow LABA-like maintenance use.

Description

Levalbuterol is the purified R-enantiomer of racemic albuterol and a rapid-onset beta 2 agonist that activates airway beta2 receptors to relax smooth muscle through cyclic AMP signaling. This stereoisomer produces most of albuterol's bronchodilation, but removing the S-enantiomer does not make the drug free of tremor, tachycardia, hypokalemia, or paradoxical bronchospasm, and clinical studies do not support assuming that every patient will have meaningfully fewer cardiac effects than with appropriately dosed albuterol. The practical distinction is formulation and dose equivalence, not a blanket claim that Xopenex is safer. Increasing rescue need still means the underlying airway disease requires reassessment.

Adverse Effects

  • Tremor, nervousness, headache, dizziness, palpitations, tachycardia, throat irritation, and muscle cramps

  • Paradoxical bronchospasm, hypersensitivity, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, arrhythmia, ischemia, or lactic acidosis during intensive exposure
