Risk Factors

  • Cardioembolism and vertebrobasilar atherosclerosis can obstruct the PCA or its origin.

  • Fetal PCA anatomy can connect carotid disease to a posterior-territory infarct.

  • Top-of-the-basilar embolism can affect both PCAs, thalami, and midbrain, producing a much broader syndrome.

Signs Symptoms

  • Sudden homonymous visual-field loss, bumping into objects, inability to read, or visual confusion may be the main deficit.

  • Memory change, thalamic sensory loss, severe central pain, visual hallucinations, or impaired awareness can accompany deeper branch involvement.

  • Bilateral injury can cause cortical blindness, confusion, or reduced consciousness even when pupils and ocular structures are intact.

Diagnostics

  • Test each visual field in both eyes because patients may describe field loss as blur or may not notice it.

  • NCCT rules out hemorrhage but can miss early or small posterior infarction, so CTA and MRI DWI are important when the examination remains concerning.

  • Assess reading, writing, naming, memory, sensation, pupils, and eye movements because these functions separate occipital, temporal, thalamic, and midbrain involvement.

Labs

  • Glucose is immediate because visual change and confusion can be metabolic mimics.

  • Acute reperfusion labs and medication history determine bleeding safety, while ECG and telemetry evaluate embolic source.

  • Inflammatory, infectious, or toxic testing is guided by the differential because migraine aura, seizure, and metabolic encephalopathy can mimic visual stroke.

Treatments

  • Activate acute ischemic-stroke evaluation for a disabling visual deficit because preserved strength does not make the deficit minor.

  • Use vascular and tissue imaging to determine IV thrombolysis or endovascular options because proximal PCA and basilar disease may be treatable.

  • Begin visual rehabilitation, compensatory scanning, fall precautions, and driving counseling because field loss changes safety even when gait strength appears normal.

Nursing Priorities

  • Approach from the intact field initially and teach systematic scanning because the patient may not detect hazards on the affected side.

  • Place objects consistently and assess reading, medication management, mobility, and toileting because vision loss can masquerade as confusion or clumsiness.

  • Escalate new eye-movement, pupil, consciousness, or bilateral visual findings because they may indicate more proximal posterior-circulation involvement.

Complications

  • Falls, collisions, medication errors, and unsafe driving can result from an unrecognized field cut.

  • Persistent alexia, memory impairment, cortical blindness, or thalamic pain can greatly limit independence.

  • Bilateral edema or proximal basilar involvement can threaten consciousness and brainstem function.

Contraindications

  • Do not test one eye at a time and call the deficit monocular without mapping both visual fields because homonymous loss is post-chiasmal.

  • Do not dismiss sudden painless vision loss because PCA stroke may have no weakness.

  • Do not assume a negative early CT excludes posterior infarction when focal findings persist.

Red Flags

  • Sudden homonymous field loss or cortical blindness

  • Visual deficit plus new memory, thalamic sensory, pupil, or eye-movement abnormality

  • Bilateral visual loss or reduced consciousness suggesting top-of-basilar involvement

Patient Education

  • Explain that the eyes may be healthy while the brain's visual map is injured.

  • Teach scanning, home-hazard reduction, mobility assistance, and formal driving guidance because insight into field loss may be incomplete.

  • Call emergency services for any recurrent sudden visual loss, imbalance, weakness, speech change, or severe headache.

Nclex Traps

  • PCA stroke can be disabling with little or no weakness.

  • Homonymous hemianopia affects the same visual field in both eyes, not one whole eye.

  • A patient who denies obvious blindness may have impaired awareness rather than normal vision.

Name

Posterior cerebral artery stroke

Category

Neurovascular localization

Definition

Posterior cerebral artery stroke is ischemia or infarction in occipital, inferomedial temporal, thalamic, or midbrain structures supplied by the PCA and its branches. A sudden contralateral homonymous visual-field deficit is the classic cortical clue, but memory loss, alexia, sensory change, pain, altered consciousness, or eye-movement findings can occur when temporal, thalamic, callosal, or midbrain branches are involved. Strength may remain nearly normal, which makes PCA stroke easy to miss if screening focuses only on face and arm weakness.

Etiology

PCA occlusion commonly results from embolism, intracranial atherosclerosis, vertebrobasilar disease, dissection, or less common vasculopathy. Some PCAs arise predominantly from the internal carotid through a fetal-type posterior communicating artery, so the embolic source and collateral pattern do not always follow the textbook posterior-circulation route.

Pathology

Occipital cortex injury interrupts visual information after the optic chiasm, causing loss of the same side of visual space in both eyes. Dominant occipital and splenial injury can disconnect visual input from language networks, causing alexia without agraphia. Thalamic infarction can disrupt sensation, arousal, memory, or pain processing, while midbrain branch injury can affect pupils, eye movement, and motor pathways.

Pathophysiology

A unilateral occipital infarct causes contralateral homonymous hemianopia because post-chiasmal fibers represent the opposite visual field from both eyes. Macular vision may be spared when collateral supply preserves the occipital pole, but this is not guaranteed. Bilateral PCA infarction can produce cortical blindness; patients may be unaware of the loss because visual association networks are also injured. Dominant temporal involvement can impair memory, while thalamic injury can later produce central post-stroke pain because sensory processing pathways are damaged.
