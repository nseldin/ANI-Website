Summary

The cardiac cycle is the repeating sequence of ventricular filling, pressure generation, ejection, and relaxation that occurs during one heartbeat. Valves respond passively to changing pressure gradients, so their opening and closure mark the boundaries between filling, isovolumetric contraction, ejection, and isovolumetric relaxation. Linking those mechanical phases to the ECG and heart sounds explains why tachycardia impairs filling, why stiffness raises filling pressure, and why a valve lesion changes both sound and flow.

Quick Answer

During diastole the relaxed ventricle fills through an open atrioventricular valve; atrial contraction adds a final contribution. Ventricular depolarization initiates contraction, the atrioventricular valves close to create S1, and pressure rises with all valves closed. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic or pulmonary pressure, the semilunar valve opens and ejection occurs. As the ventricle relaxes, semilunar closure creates S2; pressure falls with all valves closed until the atrioventricular valve opens and filling begins again. Systole is contraction and ejection, while diastole is relaxation and filling, but both contain important subphases.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and phase map

    Text

    Begin at ventricular diastole: early rapid filling follows atrioventricular valve opening, then slower filling occurs, and atrial systole completes the cycle's filling phase. End-diastolic volume is the ventricular volume just before systole. Isovolumetric contraction begins when rising ventricular pressure closes the mitral or tricuspid valve; volume cannot change because all valves are closed. Ejection begins when ventricular pressure opens the aortic or pulmonary valve. End-systolic volume remains after ejection. Isovolumetric relaxation follows semilunar closure, again with all valves closed, until ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure and the atrioventricular valve reopens.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: why pressure changes create flow

    Text

    Myocyte activation generates wall tension and raises chamber pressure. No volume leaves during isovolumetric contraction because the inlet valve has closed and the outlet valve has not yet opened. Once chamber pressure exceeds arterial pressure, blood accelerates forward; as contraction wanes and arterial pressure becomes greater, the outlet valve closes. Active calcium reuptake permits myocardial relaxation, lowering ventricular pressure enough for filling. Therefore impaired relaxation can raise filling pressure even when ejection fraction looks preserved, while impaired contraction leaves more end-systolic volume. The cycle is a pressure-driven sequence, not a set of valves opening on a timer.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Heart rate determines how much time is available for the cycle. Diastole shortens disproportionately as rate rises, which can reduce ventricular filling and coronary perfusion. Rhythm determines whether atrial contraction is coordinated; loss of the atrial contribution matters more in a stiff ventricle. Contractility, venous return, arterial load, valve function, and compliance reshape the pressure-volume relationship. These ideas connect to but do not replace the dedicated Preload, Afterload, Stroke Volume, Ejection Fraction, and Cardiac Output entries. Those cards provide quantitative definitions; this card explains when pressures and valves change during the beat.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Tachyarrhythmia can reduce filling time so much that output falls despite many beats. Bradycardia allows more filling time but may provide too few ejections per minute. A stiff ventricle requires higher pressure to accept volume, transmitting pressure backward to the atrium and lungs or systemic veins. Weak contraction increases residual end-systolic volume and may reduce forward stroke volume. Stenotic valves require a larger pressure gradient to move blood; regurgitant valves allow backward volume during the phase when they should be closed. Pericardial constraint limits filling. Dyssynchronous activation wastes work because regions contract at different times rather than producing one coordinated ejection.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Beta blockers and selected calcium-channel blockers slow rate and may lengthen filling, but excessive slowing reduces output or worsens block. Diuretics and vasodilators change loading conditions; inotropes change contraction; none should be described as directly opening a normal valve. Atrial fibrillation removes organized atrial systole. Aortic stenosis prolongs or impedes ejection, mitral stenosis obstructs diastolic filling, and regurgitation creates abnormal flow in the opposite phase. Hypertrophy and ischemia impair relaxation, while systolic heart failure impairs ejection. Cardiac resynchronization can improve mechanical timing in selected patients with electrical dyssynchrony.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    ECG timing can be aligned with mechanics: the P wave precedes atrial contraction, QRS precedes ventricular systole, and the T wave reflects repolarization before relaxation. Echocardiography shows valve motion, filling patterns, wall motion, chamber volume, and Doppler flow. Heart sounds and murmurs are timed to systole or diastole; a diastolic murmur is never explained simply by the word diastole but by abnormal flow through a specific valve. Pressure tracings and pressure-volume loops provide invasive or conceptual detail. Biomarkers may show injury or wall stress but do not display cycle phases. Blood pressure reflects arterial consequences, not all intracardiac pressures.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Relate pulse and blood pressure to rhythm, perfusion, breathing, activity tolerance, and volume findings. A rapid rhythm with dizziness, cool skin, low pressure, or chest pain may be shortening filling and impairing coronary supply. A slow rhythm with normal mentation and pressure is different from the same rate with shock. Auscultate systematically and note timing, location, radiation, and change rather than diagnosing from sound alone. Review fluid balance and rate-changing medicines, maintain ordered monitoring, and reassess after interventions. When teaching, use the simple sequence fill, close, build pressure, eject, close, relax; then connect symptoms to the disrupted phase.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Escalate for hypotension with altered mentation or cool mottled skin, ischemic chest pain, acute pulmonary edema, syncope during exertion, new sustained tachyarrhythmia or severe bradycardia with poor perfusion, or a new murmur with hemodynamic deterioration. Mechanical complications after myocardial infarction can abruptly disrupt valve competence or septal integrity. Tamponade impairs diastolic filling and can progress to obstructive shock. A patient whose rate looks acceptable may still be failing mechanically, so falling urine output, increasing lactate, worsening oxygenation, or narrow pulse pressure should not be dismissed.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Diastole is not merely the heart doing nothing; relaxation and filling require energy, compliance, open inflow valves, and adequate time. Systole does not begin when the aortic valve opens; isovolumetric contraction comes first. All valves are closed during both isovolumetric phases. S1 and S2 signal valve closure, not opening. Ejection fraction is a fraction of end-diastolic volume, not the percentage of all blood in the body pumped per beat, and a preserved ejection fraction does not prove normal filling. Treat the Wiggers diagram as connected pressure relationships rather than disconnected curves: ask which chamber or vessel pressure has just become greater than which other pressure.
