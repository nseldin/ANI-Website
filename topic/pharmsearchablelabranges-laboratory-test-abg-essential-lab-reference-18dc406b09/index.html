Name

ABG

Range

ABG uses arterial whole blood to report pH, PaCO2, and PaO2; bicarbonate (HCO3) is usually calculated. Common adult teaching examples are pH 7.35-7.45, PaCO2 35-45 mm Hg, HCO3 22-26 mEq/L, and PaO2 about 75-100 mm Hg. These are not universal critical or treatment thresholds: use the laboratory's printed interval and policy, and account for age, altitude, inspired oxygen (FiO2) or delivery device, ventilator settings, temperature reporting, and collection method. Record the oxygen and ventilator context before interpreting a trend.

Why

An ABG result signals whether arterial acid-base balance, ventilation through PaCO2, or oxygenation pressure through PaO2 is deranged, and the pattern can change oxygen, ventilatory support, and cause-directed treatment. High PaCO2 with acidemia raises concern for respiratory acidosis from inadequate alveolar ventilation; low PaCO2 with alkalemia suggests respiratory alkalosis. The pH, PaCO2, and HCO3 directions plus expected compensation distinguish respiratory from metabolic disorders and can reveal a mixed disorder even when pH is near the printed interval. Low PaO2 indicates hypoxemia, but interpret it with FiO2 and the clinical setting. Promptly assess airway patency, respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing or fatigue, mental status, SpO2 and waveform, oxygen device and flow or FiO2, ventilator settings, breath sounds, perfusion, and hemodynamics. Verify patient and specimen identity, arterial source, collection time, oxygen context, absence of air, and prompt transport when a result conflicts with the bedside picture. Cause-directed evaluation can include a repeat ABG plus electrolytes and anion gap, glucose and ketones, lactate, renal testing, toxicology testing, or chest imaging as ordered for the pattern. Escalate immediately for respiratory distress, increasing oxygen or ventilatory support, worsening fatigue, altered consciousness, shock, or rapid deterioration, and follow the laboratory's critical-value policy rather than inventing one trigger. One ABG does not diagnose the cause of an acid-base or gas-exchange disorder. PaO2 alone does not establish tissue oxygen delivery or exclude ventilatory failure during supplemental oxygen, and peripheral venous PO2 cannot substitute for PaO2. Air bubbles, excess anticoagulant, line contamination, delay, or improper temperature handling can distort the specimen; an unrecorded oxygen or ventilator change can mislead interpretation.

Category

Core labs

High Causes

  • High PaCO2: inadequate alveolar ventilation from reduced drive, respiratory muscle fatigue, airway obstruction, or insufficient mechanical ventilation

  • High HCO3: a primary metabolic alkalosis or renal compensation for sustained respiratory acidosis, interpreted with pH and PaCO2

  • High PaO2: supplemental oxygen or a high inspired-oxygen setting; interpret benefit and oxygen-exposure risk in the ordered clinical context

Low Causes

  • Low PaCO2: hyperventilation from pain, anxiety, hypoxemia, sepsis, or compensation for metabolic acidosis

  • Low HCO3: a primary metabolic acidosis or renal compensation for respiratory alkalosis, interpreted with pH and PaCO2

  • Low PaO2: hypoxemia from low inspired oxygen, hypoventilation, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, diffusion impairment, or shunt

Critical Concerns

  • Respiratory distress, exhaustion, decreasing consciousness, shock, or a rapidly worsening gas pattern can signal impending respiratory or circulatory failure even when one component remains near its printed interval.

  • A near-normal pH can hide compensation or two opposing disorders; inspect PaCO2, HCO3, PaO2, FiO2, symptoms, and the trend before calling the ABG normal.

  • Use local critical-value notification and escalation policy because analyzers, reference intervals, populations, and clinical triggers differ.

Nursing Considerations

  • Document the oxygen device, flow or FiO2, ventilator settings, respiratory assessment, temperature context when required, draw site, and collection time.

  • Check specimen identity, arterial source, air removal, anticoagulant technique, and transport timing; request repeat sampling through the ordered pathway when the result is physiologically implausible.

  • Document any suspected preanalytic problem and follow the laboratory's handling, rejection, or recollection protocol rather than interpreting a compromised specimen as a physiologic change.

Nclex Traps

  • PaCO2 is the ventilation clue and PaO2 is the oxygenation-pressure clue; SpO2 alone cannot replace PaCO2.

  • Bicarbonate on an ABG is commonly calculated, so interpret it with pH, PaCO2, electrolytes, and the clinical question rather than as an isolated kidney value.

  • Do not label an ABG compensated from pH alone or use a venous PO2 as if it were arterial PaO2.
