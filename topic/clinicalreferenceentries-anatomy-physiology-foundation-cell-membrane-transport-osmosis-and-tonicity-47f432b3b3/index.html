Summary

Cell membranes are selectively permeable barriers that let some substances cross directly and require channels, carriers, pumps, or vesicles for others. Diffusion follows an electrochemical gradient, active transport spends energy to build gradients, osmosis moves water toward the side with more effective nonpenetrating solute, and tonicity predicts whether a solution makes cells swell, shrink, or remain the same size.

Quick Answer

Isotonic fluid produces no sustained net change in cell volume, hypotonic fluid drives water into cells, and hypertonic fluid pulls water out. Tonicity depends on solutes that cannot freely cross the membrane, not simply on the total number of dissolved particles. This is why glucose or urea can raise measured osmolality yet have different lasting effects on cell volume.

Sections

    • Membrane structure and selectivity

    • The phospholipid bilayer has water-facing heads and a hydrophobic core. Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross relatively easily, while ions and most polar molecules need proteins. Channels form selective pores; carriers bind a solute and change shape; pumps move substances against gradients; receptors convert external signals into intracellular responses; and vesicles move larger cargo by endocytosis or exocytosis. Cholesterol and cytoskeletal attachments influence membrane stability and fluidity. Selective permeability allows intracellular chemistry to differ from plasma while cells still exchange nutrients, waste, water, electrical charge, and signals.

    • Diffusion and electrochemical gradients

    • Simple diffusion is net movement from higher to lower concentration caused by random molecular motion. Facilitated diffusion also moves down a gradient but uses a channel or carrier, so it is selective and can saturate. Ions respond to both concentration and electrical forces; together these form an electrochemical gradient. Sodium is concentrated outside many cells and potassium inside. Opening a sodium channel therefore lets sodium enter rapidly, while opening selected potassium channels often lets potassium leave. Diffusion does not require the cell to spend ATP directly, but the gradients it uses were often created by energy-dependent pumps.

    • Active and coupled transport

    • Primary active transport uses ATP directly. The sodium-potassium ATPase moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in per cycle, preserving cell volume, sodium and potassium gradients, and electrical excitability. Secondary active transport uses a stored gradient instead of splitting ATP at the transporter. Sodium-glucose cotransport brings glucose into intestinal or proximal-tubule cells by coupling it to inward sodium movement; another carrier then lets glucose leave toward blood. Exchangers can move solutes in opposite directions. When ATP generation fails, gradients dissipate, sodium and water accumulate inside cells, calcium control fails, and swelling can progress to membrane injury.

    • Osmosis, osmolality, and tonicity

    • Osmosis is water movement across a water-permeable membrane in response to an effective solute gradient. Osmolality counts dissolved particles per kilogram of water, whereas tonicity describes the sustained effect of extracellular fluid on cell volume. A penetrating solute can contribute to measured osmolality but enter cells, reducing its lasting water pull. Effective osmoles such as extracellular sodium salts largely determine plasma tonicity. In isotonic fluid, cell volume remains stable; hypotonic extracellular fluid promotes cell swelling; hypertonic fluid promotes shrinkage. Aquaporin water channels change how quickly water moves but do not reverse the direction established by effective osmotic forces.

    • Regulation and relationships

    • The kidneys, thirst, antidiuretic hormone, aldosterone, natriuretic signals, and cellular transporters coordinate water and solute balance. Insulin stimulates cellular potassium uptake partly through sodium-potassium ATPase activity. Acid-base disorders, catecholamines, cell destruction, and medication effects redistribute potassium without necessarily changing total-body potassium in the same direction. Sodium concentration mainly reflects water balance relative to body sodium, not simply dietary salt. Capillary hydrostatic and oncotic forces govern movement between plasma and interstitium, while tonicity governs water distribution across cell membranes; edema and cellular swelling are related but not identical processes.

    • Clinical connections

    • IV-fluid choice is applied membrane physiology. Isotonic crystalloids expand extracellular fluid but distribute beyond plasma; they do not directly refill intracellular water. Hypertonic saline can draw water from swollen brain cells but can cause dangerous osmotic shifts if sodium changes too quickly. Excess free water can worsen cerebral edema in severe hyponatremia. Mannitol acts as an extracellular osmotic agent when retained outside cells and filtered by functioning kidneys. Dialysis uses diffusion and fluid movement across a semipermeable membrane. SGLT2 inhibitors, loop and thiazide diuretics, insulin, local anesthetics, channel blockers, and cardiac glycosides all connect clinical effects to transport proteins or ion gradients.

    • Assessment, labs, and diagnostics

    • Assess neurologic status, thirst, mucous membranes, edema, weight change, intake and output, urine concentration, muscle strength, reflexes, bowel function, and cardiac rhythm. Interpret serum sodium with measured or calculated osmolality, glucose, renal function, urine osmolality, and urine sodium when ordered. Potassium abnormalities require an ECG and cause assessment because serum concentration reflects intake, loss, kidney excretion, transcellular shifts, and cell injury. A hemolyzed specimen can falsely elevate potassium. Trend the rate of sodium change because neurologic risk depends on magnitude, duration, symptoms, and correction speed rather than one number alone.

    • Nursing actions and why

    • Verify the exact fluid, concentration, route, pump setting, compatibility, access patency, and prescribed correction goal. Use an infusion pump for hypertonic or high-risk therapy and perform ordered neurologic, pulmonary, electrolyte, glucose, and urine-output checks. Reassess after insulin, potassium replacement, diuresis, dialysis, or osmotic therapy because the serum value and cell distribution can change rapidly. Protect against free-water excess or loss according to the cause, not a memorized fluid label. Stop and clarify an order that could change sodium too quickly, give potassium despite unsafe renal output, or deliver a concentrated solution through unsuitable access.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Seizure, coma, severe confusion, or rapidly worsening headache with sodium disturbance suggests dangerous brain swelling or osmotic stress. Peaked T waves, QRS widening, severe weakness, paralysis, or bradyarrhythmia with hyperkalemia requires immediate action. Ventricular dysrhythmia can also occur with severe hypokalemia. Abrupt oliguria during potassium or osmotic therapy increases accumulation risk. New pulmonary crackles, hypoxemia, or edema during fluid administration suggests the extracellular expansion is exceeding cardiac or renal tolerance. Pain, blanching, swelling, or loss of blood return around a concentrated infusion may signal extravasation.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Osmosis is water movement, not solute diffusion. Hyperosmolar and hypertonic are not automatically interchangeable because penetrating solutes matter. Normal saline is approximately isotonic but can still worsen edema or hyperchloremic acidosis in sufficient amounts. A low serum sodium does not always mean total-body sodium is low; it may reflect excess water. A high serum potassium can coexist with total-body depletion in diabetic ketoacidosis. Sodium-potassium ATPase maintains gradients; it does not create an action potential by rapidly reversing its direction. Choose fluids and replacement from the patient's mechanism, trend, organ function, and symptoms.
