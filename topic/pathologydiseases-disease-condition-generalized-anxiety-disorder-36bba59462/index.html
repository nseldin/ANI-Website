Plain Language

Generalized anxiety disorder causes excessive, hard-to-control worry about several parts of everyday life, such as health, family, money, school, or work. The worry happens much of the time and comes with problems such as tension, restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, or disturbed sleep.

Why It Matters

GAD can steadily impair sleep, concentration, relationships, work, school, physical health care, and medication or substance safety. Nurses should identify the worry pattern, rule out urgent medical or substance causes, assess depression and suicide risk, and connect the patient with treatment before avoidance and reassurance-seeking become entrenched.

Definition

Generalized anxiety disorder is an anxiety disorder with excessive anxiety and apprehensive worry about multiple events or activities that is difficult to control, persists across the required diagnostic period, causes distress or impaired functioning, and is not better explained by another mental disorder, a substance or medication, or a medical condition. DSM-5-TR generally requires the pattern on more days than not for at least six months; ICD-11 uses a several-month pattern and does not turn one duration phrase or screening score into a diagnosis.

Pathology

GAD is a clinical syndrome rather than a structural lesion or laboratory diagnosis. Threat appraisal remains broadly activated across ordinary life domains, while worry, checking, avoidance, muscle tension, and repeated reassurance provide short relief but can maintain uncertainty and arousal. Genetic, developmental, learning, medical, and social factors interact; no single mechanism is universal.

Pathophysiology

  • Repeated threat prediction activates sympathetic and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal stress responses, contributing to palpitations, sweating, gastrointestinal discomfort, muscle tension, headache, fatigue, and sleep disturbance.

  • Worry can function as an attempted control strategy. Avoidance and reassurance reduce distress briefly but prevent learning that uncertainty can be tolerated and feared outcomes are not inevitable.

Etiology

No single event causes GAD. Familial vulnerability, behavioral inhibition, chronic stress, adversity, learned worry, intolerance of uncertainty, medical illness, pain, sleep disruption, and social instability may contribute. Symptoms can begin at any age and commonly coexist with depression or another anxiety disorder.

Risk Factors

  • Family history of anxiety or depression and a temperament marked by threat sensitivity

  • Childhood adversity, chronic stress, caregiving burden, discrimination, financial insecurity, illness, pain, or sleep loss

  • Another anxiety disorder, major depression, trauma-related symptoms, or alcohol and other substance use

  • Stimulants, high caffeine intake, corticosteroids, thyroid hormone excess, withdrawal states, or other exposures that can cause or amplify anxiety

Signs Symptoms

  • Worry across several life areas that feels excessive and difficult to stop or redirect

  • Restlessness, feeling keyed up, fatigue, concentration difficulty, irritability, muscle tension, or sleep disturbance

  • Repeated checking, reassurance-seeking, procrastination, overpreparation, avoidance, or frequent health visits for anxiety-related body symptoms

  • Headache, jaw tension, palpitations, sweating, nausea, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, dizziness, or shortness of breath

  • Functional decline in work, school, caregiving, relationships, finances, self-care, or medical follow-up

Diagnostics

  • Diagnosis is clinical: establish the breadth, controllability, duration, physical symptoms, distress, impairment, developmental and cultural context, substances, medicines, and course of worry.

  • GAD-2 or GAD-7 can support case finding and response tracking, but no score independently diagnoses GAD or rules out a medical condition.

  • Assess depression, bipolar-spectrum history, trauma, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, panic attacks, health anxiety, psychosis, ADHD, sleep disorder, and substance use.

  • Use examination and targeted testing when onset, vital signs, history, or symptoms suggest thyroid disease, arrhythmia, anemia, hypoglycemia, pulmonary disease, medication effect, intoxication, withdrawal, or another medical explanation.

Labs

  • No laboratory, imaging, genetic, or physiologic test confirms GAD.

  • Target studies to the presentation; examples may include glucose, CBC, thyroid testing, ECG, pregnancy testing, toxicology, or medication levels when clinically indicated rather than as a universal panel.

  • Normal tests do not prove anxiety is the cause, and an abnormal result does not exclude a coexisting anxiety disorder.

Assessment

Begin with airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, glucose when indicated, mental status, intoxication or withdrawal, and acute medical symptoms. Then assess the exact worry domains, onset, duration, sleep, caffeine and stimulants, alcohol and drugs, medications, impairment, coping, supports, depression, bipolar symptoms, self-harm, suicide, and barriers to care. Ask what the patient fears will happen and what they do to prevent it.

Differential

  • Expected short-term worry that is proportionate to a stressor and does not cause persistent impairment

  • Panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, illness anxiety disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, PTSD, adjustment disorder, or separation anxiety disorder

  • Major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism, psychosis, insomnia, or somatic symptom disorder

  • Hyperthyroidism, arrhythmia, anemia, hypoglycemia, asthma or other cardiopulmonary illness, pain, menopause, seizure, or another medical condition

  • Caffeine or stimulant excess, cannabis or other substances, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, corticosteroids, thyroid medicine, decongestants, or other medication effects

Treatments

  • Provide education, active monitoring, sleep and substance assessment, and shared decision-making. Low-intensity CBT-based guided self-help or psychoeducation may suit milder presentations.

  • For marked impairment or inadequate response, offer high-intensity CBT or applied relaxation delivered by a trained clinician. CBT targets worry beliefs, avoidance, reassurance, and intolerance of uncertainty.

  • When an adult chooses medication, an SSRI is a first-line class. Escitalopram is a representative U.S.-labeled generic for adult GAD; sertraline is a guideline-supported example in some jurisdictions but may be off-label for GAD depending on location.

  • Review diagnosis, adherence, adverse effects, comorbidity, treatment quality, and patient goals before switching, combining, or escalating care; complex refractory illness warrants specialist input.

Contraindications

  • Do not use benzodiazepines as routine long-term GAD treatment; dependence, tolerance, withdrawal, falls, cognitive effects, and dangerous interaction with opioids or alcohol can outweigh short relief.

  • Do not use an antipsychotic as routine primary-care treatment for GAD without a separate supported indication and specialist plan.

  • Do not combine serotonergic drugs casually, use an SSRI with an MAOI, or stop an SSRI or SNRI abruptly; follow the current product label and individualized taper plan.

  • Do not attribute first-time chest pain, syncope, hypoxia, focal neurologic change, severe agitation, or delirium to anxiety without appropriate medical evaluation.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess suicide and self-harm risk when depression, hopelessness, severe impairment, substance use, or treatment activation is present.

  • Before an antidepressant, review bipolar-spectrum history, current mania or hypomania, pregnancy, interactions, bleeding risk, sodium risk, and prior medication response.

  • Monitor early agitation, insomnia, suicidal thinking in younger patients, serotonin toxicity, hyponatremia risk, bleeding, sexual adverse effects, and discontinuation symptoms according to the drug and patient.

  • Escalate new severe chest pain, syncope, persistent tachyarrhythmia, hypoxia, seizure, delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, or inability to maintain safety.

Nursing Priorities

  • Rule out acute medical and substance emergencies before using anxiety as the explanation.

  • Ask directly about suicide, self-harm, alcohol, sedatives, stimulants, sleep, nutrition, adherence, and functional loss.

  • Use calm, concrete communication; identify one worry cycle and one measurable functional goal rather than offering endless reassurance.

  • Monitor treatment response by functioning, sleep, avoidance, worry control, adverse effects, and safety—not by a screening score alone.

Red Flags

  • Suicidal intent, recent attempt, inability to maintain safety, severe self-neglect, or dangerous substance use

  • Chest pain with concerning features, syncope, hypoxia, focal neurologic deficit, seizure, delirium, fever, or rapidly changing mental status

  • New mania, psychosis, severe agitation, markedly reduced need for sleep, or escalating suicidality after an antidepressant change

  • Severe alcohol, benzodiazepine, or other sedative withdrawal; opioid-sedative co-use; or inability to eat, sleep, work, care for dependents, or obtain essential care

Complications

  • Major depression, another anxiety disorder, substance misuse, insomnia, chronic pain amplification, and increased suicide risk

  • Occupational, academic, financial, relationship, caregiving, and health-care impairment

  • Medication dependence or withdrawal when short-term sedatives become a coping strategy

  • Repeated low-value testing or delayed needed care when physical symptoms are either overattributed to disease or dismissed as anxiety

Prognosis

GAD may fluctuate or become chronic, but evidence-based psychotherapy and appropriately monitored medication can meaningfully improve worry, physical symptoms, and function. Recovery is measured by restored sleep, concentration, participation, and ability to tolerate uncertainty—not by never feeling anxious.

Prevention

  • Address sleep disruption, excessive caffeine or stimulants, chronic stress, bullying, pain, and substance use early.

  • Teach coping and uncertainty tolerance without reinforcing repeated reassurance or avoidance.

  • Screen for depression and suicide risk when functioning narrows, and maintain relapse planning after improvement.

Patient Education

  • GAD is more than ordinary worry: the worry is broad, difficult to control, persistent, and disruptive.

  • Body symptoms are real. They still require appropriate medical assessment when new or concerning, and normal testing does not make the distress fake.

  • CBT builds skills through practice; antidepressants usually work gradually and can briefly increase anxiety or sleep problems early.

  • Do not stop an antidepressant suddenly. Seek prompt help for suicidal thoughts, severe agitation, unusually high energy with little sleep, confusion, seizure, or serious physical symptoms.

Special Populations

  • Children may show irritability, reassurance-seeking, stomachaches, school difficulty, or sleep dependence; use developmental assessment, family and school information, CBT, and specialist medication review.

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding require individualized risk-benefit discussion; untreated illness also carries risk, and medication decisions should use current obstetric and product guidance.

  • Older adults need careful review for delirium, dementia, thyroid or cardiac illness, falls, polypharmacy, hyponatremia, sedative exposure, and grief or isolation.

  • Autistic people and those with intellectual, communication, sensory, or language needs require accessible assessment and adapted therapy rather than assuming all repetitive worry is GAD.

Nclex Traps

  • GAD is not diagnosed by GAD-7 alone; pattern, duration, differential, distress, and impairment matter.

  • First-time severe cardiopulmonary or neurologic symptoms are not automatically anxiety.

  • SSRIs are not PRN calming medicines; therapeutic benefit is gradual and early activation requires monitoring.

  • Benzodiazepines are not routine long-term treatment and become especially dangerous with opioids, alcohol, older age, or respiratory disease.
