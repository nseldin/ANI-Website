Used To Treat

This is a physiology and medication-pathway card. It explains why ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, and direct renin inhibitors lower pressure or albuminuria, and why those same drugs can cause hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine change, acute kidney injury in vulnerable patients, and fetal toxicity.

Description

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is the body's pressure-and-volume rescue circuit. Juxtaglomerular cells release renin when renal perfusion pressure falls, sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa falls, or beta-1 sympathetic signaling rises. Renin cleaves liver-derived angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; angiotensin-converting enzyme, especially on vascular endothelium, converts it to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II activates AT1 receptors, constricting arterioles, preferentially supporting efferent glomerular tone, stimulating aldosterone and vasopressin, increasing proximal sodium reabsorption, and reinforcing thirst and sympathetic activity. That response helps preserve pressure and filtration during true volume loss. Chronic overactivity, however, sustains hypertension, albuminuria, ventricular remodeling, fibrosis, and congestion. RAAS drugs are useful because they interrupt that maladaptive signaling, but a patient who currently depends on it for perfusion can deteriorate when it is blocked.

Mechanism

The pathway begins when renin's aspartyl-protease activity cleaves angiotensinogen. ACE removes two amino acids from angiotensin I to create angiotensin II and also degrades bradykinin. AT1-receptor activation couples through Gq proteins, phospholipase C, inositol trisphosphate, and intracellular calcium to contract vascular smooth muscle. In the adrenal zona glomerulosa it increases aldosterone, which raises epithelial sodium-channel and sodium-potassium-ATPase activity in the distal nephron; sodium retention expands extracellular volume, while potassium and hydrogen secretion rise. Angiotensin II also constricts the efferent arteriole, helping maintain glomerular capillary pressure when renal inflow is low. ACE inhibitors reduce angiotensin II and preserve bradykinin. ARBs leave angiotensin II concentration intact or increased but block AT1 signaling. Aliskiren blocks renin upstream. Sacubitril preserves natriuretic peptides while valsartan blocks AT1. These different targets explain both shared risks and class-specific effects.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. This pathway warning applies to ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aliskiren, and sacubitril/valsartan. It is a mechanism-based class danger, not a harmless labeling formality.

Adverse Effects

  • Excess pathway suppression can cause hypotension, dizziness, syncope, hyperkalemia, and a hemodynamic fall in GFR; ACE inhibition additionally causes bradykinin cough and can cause angioedema.

  • Severe consequences include airway angioedema, acute kidney injury, dangerous hyperkalemia, shock in a profoundly volume-depleted patient, and fetal or neonatal renal injury.

Contraindications

  • Pregnancy is incompatible with direct RAAS blockade. Avoid duplicate ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, or aliskiren therapy unless a highly specific specialist plan overrides routine practice.

  • Active angioedema, prior ACE-inhibitor angioedema for ACE inhibitors/ARNI, severe hyperkalemia, symptomatic hypotension, evolving acute kidney injury, or suspected bilateral renal-artery stenosis requires immediate clarification.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before initiation or dose escalation, assess pressure, orthostasis, volume status, creatinine/eGFR, potassium, pregnancy potential, NSAID/diuretic exposure, potassium products, and whether renal perfusion may be RAAS dependent.

  • After a start or dose change, trend creatinine and potassium rather than treating one value in isolation. A modest early eGFR dip can reflect the intended efferent-arteriole effect; a large or progressive decline should trigger review for volume depletion, NSAIDs, renal-artery stenosis, sepsis, obstruction, or excessive pressure reduction.

Interactions

  • NSAID plus diuretic plus ACE inhibitor/ARB creates the classic triple-whammy physiology: reduced afferent dilation, reduced circulating volume, and reduced efferent tone can collapse glomerular pressure.

  • Potassium supplements, salt substitutes, trimethoprim, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, and CKD amplify hyperkalemia. Lithium exposure can rise when sodium and renal handling change.

Key Labs

  • Blood pressure and orthostasis, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, urine output, hydration/perfusion, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio when kidney protection is the goal.

  • KDIGO advises checking creatinine and potassium within about 2 to 4 weeks after starting or changing an ACE inhibitor or ARB; acute illness or high-risk physiology can justify earlier checks.

Nclex Traps

  • RAAS blockade can protect kidneys chronically while lowering GFR acutely. That is not a contradiction: lower intraglomerular pressure reduces long-term barotrauma but can expose dependence on efferent constriction during poor renal inflow.

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs share fetal, potassium, pressure, and renal risks. Only ACE inhibitors directly inhibit bradykinin breakdown, so dry cough is much more characteristic of ACE inhibition.
