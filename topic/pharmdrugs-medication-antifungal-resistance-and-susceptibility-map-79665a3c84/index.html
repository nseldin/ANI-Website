Used To Treat

A mechanism card for interpreting intrinsic resistance, acquired resistance, cross-resistance, MIC results, and breakthrough fungal infection.

Description

Fungi resist therapy by changing the drug target, changing how much drug reaches it, replacing the blocked pathway, or living in a protected community. Azole resistance commonly involves ERG11/CYP51 mutation or overexpression plus efflux pumps such as CDR or MDR transporters; one mechanism can create cross-resistance across several azoles. Echinocandin resistance usually involves FKS hot-spot mutations that reduce glucan-synthase binding, while increased chitin can partially compensate for lost glucan. Flucytosine resistance develops rapidly when cytosine permease, deaminase, or downstream activation is lost, which is why monotherapy is avoided in cryptococcosis. Amphotericin resistance is less common but can follow reduced or altered ergosterol. Biofilm on catheters and devices changes metabolism, matrix penetration, and stress responses, making source removal as important as the MIC.

Mechanism

Intrinsic resistance is built into a species before exposure, such as Candida krusei resistance to fluconazole. Acquired resistance emerges under selective pressure as a susceptible population accumulates or selects mutations. An MIC is the concentration that inhibits growth under standardized laboratory conditions; it is interpreted using species- and drug-specific breakpoints or epidemiologic cutoff values when available. A susceptible result increases confidence but does not guarantee success because host immunity, drug exposure, site penetration, and source control remain outside the test tube. A resistant result can explain failure even when the serum level is high. The clinical question is therefore not 'does the drug kill fungus?' but 'does this exposure reach this organism at this site in this host while the source is controlled?'

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Candida auris and other multidrug-resistant fungi require infection-control notification, species confirmation, susceptibility testing, and public-health or specialist coordination. Do not rely on routine species assumptions.

Adverse Effects

  • Inadequate or interrupted exposure can select resistance while allowing progression. Unnecessary prolonged prophylaxis also changes colonizing flora and breakthrough species.

  • Escalating multiple toxic drugs without confirming resistance can cause organ injury while leaving a mechanical source untouched.

Contraindications

  • Do not equate a higher MIC across different species or drugs without the correct breakpoint context.

  • Do not call persistent culture 'resistance' before checking dose delivery, formulation, interactions, source control, and infected compartment.

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain adequate cultures before switching when feasible, preserve isolate/specimen information, document prior antifungal exposure, and implement organism-specific infection control.

  • Support adherence and exact administration because subtherapeutic exposure is both a patient-level failure risk and a population-level resistance pressure.

Interactions

  • Enzyme inducers can create functional resistance by lowering exposure even when the organism is genetically susceptible.

  • Biofilm-associated hardware acts like a pharmacologic interaction by reducing effective drug access and continuously reseeding infection.

Key Labs

  • Species identification, MIC and susceptibility interpretation, serial cultures, drug level when relevant, organ function, and molecular resistance testing when available for high-consequence organisms.

  • Trend clinical and microbiologic response. A laboratory category should change management only in the context of drug, site, dose, and host.

Nclex Traps

  • Resistance is not always caused by nonadherence, and nonresponse is not always resistance.

  • FKS mutations affect echinocandins; ERG11/CYP51 and efflux commonly affect azoles.

Source Note

Current U.S. fluconazole, caspofungin, flucytosine, and amphotericin labels plus CDC/IDSA resistance principles.

    Note
