Definition

Wartenberg finger sign is an observed clinical sign. It is a clue to ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction. A positive finding means the little finger remains abducted or drifts away from the ring finger.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Wartenberg finger sign means the little finger remains abducted or drifts away from the ring finger.

Why It Matters

The observed Wartenberg finger sign finding can signal ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction. Weak interosseous adduction allows extensor forces to pull the little finger outward. Wartenberg finger sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction

Assessment Method

Ask the patient to hold the fingers extended and adducted, then observe the little finger.

Positive Finding

The little finger remains abducted or drifts away from the ring finger.

Mechanism

Weak interosseous adduction allows extensor forces to pull the little finger outward.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Wartenberg finger sign finding raises concern for ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction because weak interosseous adduction allows extensor forces to pull the little finger outward. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Wartenberg finger sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Wartenberg finger sign to focus evaluation of ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: ulnar nerve injury, habitual finger posture, arthritis. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask the patient to hold the fingers extended and adducted, then observe the little finger. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Wartenberg finger sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

It does not localize the exact lesion and can be obscured by joint contracture. Therefore, Wartenberg finger sign cannot confirm or exclude ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • ulnar nerve injury

  • habitual finger posture

  • arthritis

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as ulnar nerve injury, habitual finger posture, arthritis.

False Negative Situations

Wartenberg finger sign may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction.

Modern Status

Current localization adjunct. When performed as described, Wartenberg finger sign can adjust concern for ulnar motor weakness or pyramidal dysfunction, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Wartenberg finger sign: It does not localize the exact lesion and can be obscured by joint contracture.

Safety Precautions

Observe Wartenberg finger sign with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Ask the patient to hold the fingers extended and adducted, then observe the little finger. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not confuse this with Wartenberg syndrome, a sensory radial-neuropathy condition.
