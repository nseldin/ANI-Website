Definition

Shingles/postherpetic neuralgia develops when latent varicella-zoster virus reactivates in a sensory ganglion, inflames the corresponding nerve, and may leave persistent neuropathic firing after the rash heals. The characteristic pattern is unilateral burning pain followed by grouped vesicles in one or adjacent dermatomes that usually stop at the midline; allodynia persisting after healing indicates postherpetic neuralgia. Confirm or stage it with clinical inspection of rash distribution and ocular, otic, motor, or disseminated involvement; distinguish it from herpes simplex, contact dermatitis, cellulitis, and radiculopathy by the dermatomal vesicles and neuropathic pain pattern. The priority is to start a prescribed antiviral promptly and adjust it for kidney function because herpes zoster ophthalmicus with vision loss can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Altered level of consciousness, focal weakness, speech change, pupil change, seizure activity, sensory loss, severe headache, gait change, or new swallowing trouble.

  • Sudden change from baseline is a red flag even when vital signs are not dramatic.

Diagnostics

  • clinical inspection of rash distribution and ocular, otic, motor, or disseminated involvement

  • PCR of vesicle material when morphology is atypical or confirmation changes isolation or treatment

  • focused eye examination for forehead, nasal-tip, or visual symptoms

  • Distinguish Shingles/postherpetic neuralgia from herpes simplex, contact dermatitis, cellulitis, and radiculopathy by the dermatomal vesicles and neuropathic pain pattern.

Labs

  • glucose must be checked with altered mental status

  • sodium abnormalities can worsen seizures/confusion

  • infection markers may rise with meningitis/encephalitis

Treatments

  • start a prescribed antiviral promptly and adjust it for kidney function

  • protect lesions and use transmission precautions appropriate to localization and immune status

  • treat acute and persistent neuropathic pain with an individualized nonopioid-centered plan

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate the same day for forehead or nose lesions, eye pain or vision change, facial weakness or ear vesicles, confusion, weakness, urinary retention, disseminated lesions, dyspnea, or sepsis because ophthalmic zoster, Ramsay Hunt syndrome, myelitis or encephalitis, pneumonia, and dissemination need urgent specialty treatment.

  • Map the rash by dermatome and midline, inspect vesicles and crusting, and assess pain, allodynia, immune status, fever, neurologic findings, eye and ear symptoms, and dissemination because location and host immunity predict ocular, otic, central nervous system, pulmonary, and transmission risk.

  • Start prescribed antiviral therapy promptly and review renal function, hydration, dose, and treatment window because early suppression of varicella-zoster replication shortens acute disease and is especially important for ocular, neurologic, disseminated, or immunocompromised cases.

  • For localized shingles in an immunocompetent patient whose lesions can be contained and covered, use Standard Precautions until lesions are dry and crusted; for disseminated disease in any patient or localized disease in an immunocompromised patient until dissemination is excluded, use Airborne + Contact + Standard Precautions because varicella-zoster virus can spread from vesicle fluid and airborne particles.

  • Use a scheduled multimodal pain plan, protect numb or allodynic skin, monitor sleep, mood, function, and medication sedation, and reassess pain after rash healing because persistent peripheral and central sensitization causes postherpetic neuralgia and secondary injury.

Complications

  • herpes zoster ophthalmicus with vision loss

  • Ramsay Hunt syndrome or central nervous system involvement

  • dissemination can complicate Shingles/postherpetic neuralgia.

  • secondary infection can complicate Shingles/postherpetic neuralgia.

  • or disabling postherpetic neuralgia

Red Flags

  • Forehead or nasal-tip rash, red painful eye, photophobia, or visual change

  • Ear vesicles, facial paralysis, severe vertigo, or hearing loss

  • Confusion, severe headache, weakness, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or seizure

  • Widespread lesions, dyspnea, hypoxemia, fever, or systemic illness in an immunocompromised person

Patient Education

  • Keep lesions covered, wash hands after touching them, and avoid all susceptible nonimmune contacts until every lesion is dry and crusted, especially pregnant or immunocompromised people and newborns, because shingles can transmit varicella and cause severe disease in vulnerable people.

  • Seek urgent care for any eye or ear involvement, facial weakness, confusion, or spreading rash, and receive recombinant zoster vaccine when eligible because prior shingles does not reliably prevent recurrence.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not misroute shingles as a renal disorder; it is reactivation of a neurotropic herpesvirus

  • A rash on the forehead or nose plus eye symptoms needs same-day ophthalmic assessment
