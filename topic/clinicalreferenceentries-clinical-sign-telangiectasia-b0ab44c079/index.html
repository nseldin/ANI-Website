Definition

Telangiectasia means one or more small blood vessels near the surface of the skin or a mucous membrane are permanently widened and visible. They often appear as fine red, pink, or purple lines or spots that pale with pressure and then refill.

Plain Language

Telangiectasia means tiny surface blood vessels stay widened enough to see.

Why It Matters

An isolated lesion is often harmless, but the number, pattern, location, bleeding, and associated symptoms matter. Multiple lesions on the lips, mouth, fingers, or nose with recurrent nosebleeds or anemia can indicate an inherited bleeding disorder, while telangiectasias with other connective-tissue findings can support systemic disease.

Associated Conditions

  • persistent dilation of small blood vessels near the skin or mucosal surface that may be idiopathic or related to aging, sun exposure, pregnancy, rosacea, venous pressure, prolonged topical corticosteroid use, systemic sclerosis, or an inherited vascular disorder

Assessment Method

Inspect skin, lips, oral cavity, conjunctivae, nail beds, and visible mucosa in neutral light; document number, size, color, linear or punctate pattern, distribution, symmetry, blanching and refill with gentle pressure, bleeding, new medications or topical corticosteroids, sun exposure, recurrent nosebleeds, anemia symptoms, family history, and systemic findings.

Positive Finding

Fine flat red, pink, or purple vessels or spots remain visibly widened near the surface, blanch with gentle pressure, and refill when pressure is released.

Mechanism

Capillaries, venules, or arterioles near the surface stay abnormally dilated, making blood within them visible through the skin or mucosa.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Telangiectasia finding raises concern for persistent dilation of small blood vessels near the skin or mucosal surface that may be idiopathic or related to aging, sun exposure, pregnancy, rosacea, venous pressure, prolonged topical corticosteroid use, systemic sclerosis, or an inherited vascular disorder because capillaries, venules, or arterioles near the surface stay abnormally dilated, making blood within them visible through the skin or mucosa. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Telangiectasia does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Evidence Limitations

Skin tone, lighting, vessel depth, pressure technique, and lesion site affect visibility; morphology alone cannot establish the cause, and treatment may temporarily reduce the finding. Therefore, Telangiectasia cannot confirm or exclude persistent dilation of small blood vessels near the skin or mucosal surface that may be idiopathic or related to aging, sun exposure, pregnancy, rosacea, venous pressure, prolonged topical corticosteroid use, systemic sclerosis, or an inherited vascular disorder by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as petechiae, spider angioma, cherry angioma, small varicose veins, erythema.

False Negative Situations

Telangiectasia may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of persistent dilation of small blood vessels near the skin or mucosal surface that may be idiopathic or related to aging, sun exposure, pregnancy, rosacea, venous pressure, prolonged topical corticosteroid use, systemic sclerosis, or an inherited vascular disorder.
