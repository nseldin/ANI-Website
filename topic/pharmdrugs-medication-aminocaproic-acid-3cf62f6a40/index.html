Used To Treat

Bleeding caused by excessive fibrinolysis when the diagnosis is established, including selected surgical, hematologic, urinary, oral, or mucosal contexts. It stabilizes clot already being formed; it does not replace platelets or deficient coagulation factors and should not be used indiscriminately for bleeding of unknown cause.

Mechanism

Plasminogen contains kringle domains with lysine-binding pockets that recognize exposed lysine residues on partially degraded fibrin. Docking brings plasminogen near tissue plasminogen activator and accelerates conversion to plasmin; plasmin then cleaves fibrin and exposes more lysine, creating positive feedback. Epsilon-aminocaproic acid competitively occupies those lysine-binding pockets, preventing plasminogen attachment and activation on fibrin. At larger concentrations it inhibits plasmin activity directly. The drug is largely renally eliminated, so kidney dysfunction prolongs exposure. It preserves a hemostatic plug but does not correct low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia, a factor deficiency, or the anatomic source of bleeding.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Use only when hyperfibrinolysis is established or strongly expected. In DIC, inhibiting fibrinolysis without controlling ongoing thrombin generation can trap pathologic microvascular clot; specialist-directed heparin may be required in rare selected DIC use. Upper urinary-tract bleeding can form obstructing clots. Rapid IV administration can cause hypotension, bradycardia, or dysrhythmia.

Contraindications

  • Active intravascular clotting or DIC unless used under a specific specialist plan that addresses coagulation activation

  • Upper urinary-tract bleeding when obstruction risk outweighs benefit

  • Serious hypersensitivity and dose/rate caution in renal impairment

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm evidence of fibrinolysis and the bleeding source. Monitor for flank pain, oliguria, chest pain, unilateral swelling, neurologic change, or worsening organ function.

  • Administer IV loading and maintenance at the prescribed rate; rapid delivery creates cardiovascular toxicity that is not related to the intended antifibrinolytic effect.

Interactions

  • Factor IX complex concentrates and anti-inhibitor coagulant complexes can substantially increase thrombosis when combined.

  • Other procoagulant agents and estrogen add thrombosis risk; renal impairment increases exposure.

Key Labs

  • CBC/hemoglobin, fibrinogen, D-dimer or fibrinolysis studies selected by context, PT/aPTT, renal function, CK with muscle symptoms, and urinalysis

  • Clinical hemostasis, urine output/flank pain, thrombotic signs, and infusion hemodynamics

Nclex Traps

  • Aminocaproic acid protects fibrin; it does not create fibrin or replace a missing factor.

  • Hematuria is not automatically an indication because upper-tract clots can obstruct the kidney.

Description

Aminocaproic acid is a synthetic analog of the amino acid lysine that protects fibrin from premature breakdown. Plasminogen uses lysine-binding sites to attach to fibrin, where activators convert it to plasmin. Aminocaproic acid occupies those sites, so plasminogen cannot concentrate on the clot and fibrinolysis slows. At higher concentrations it also directly inhibits plasmin. This explains why it works best when excessive clot dissolution is the problem and why it can be dangerous when widespread intravascular clotting or an obstructed upper urinary tract is present.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, diarrhea, abdominal pain, headache, dizziness, or hypotension with rapid infusion

  • Thrombosis, myopathy/rhabdomyolysis, renal impairment, or seizures

  • Upper urinary-tract obstruction from retained clots and intrarenal obstruction
