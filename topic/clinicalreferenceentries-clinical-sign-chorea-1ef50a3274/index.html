Definition

Chorea is a pattern of brief, irregular, unpredictable involuntary movements that appear to flow randomly from one body part to another.

Plain Language

Chorea looks like restless, dance-like movements that the person cannot fully control or keep still.

Why It Matters

Chorea can result from Huntington disease, rheumatic fever, medication effects, autoimmune disease, stroke, or metabolic disturbance. Sudden one-sided movement or chorea with weakness, confusion, severe glucose abnormality, or pregnancy-related illness requires urgent evaluation.

Assessment Method

Observe the face, tongue, trunk, limbs, hands, gait, posture, and goal-directed activity; assess whether movements are irregular, flowing, suppressible, or incorporated into gestures, test sustained tongue protrusion and hand grip when safe, and document onset, distribution, progression, medications, glucose, systemic illness, cognition, behavior, swallowing, falls, and family history.

Positive Finding

Brief irregular unpredictable nonrhythmic movements appear to flow randomly from one body region to another and interfere with maintaining a steady posture or action.

Mechanism

Impaired basal-ganglia selection and suppression of motor programs allows unwanted movements to interrupt and blend into voluntary activity.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Chorea response raises concern for basal-ganglia and motor-network dysfunction from hereditary, autoimmune, infectious, vascular, metabolic, endocrine, medication, or toxic causes because impaired basal-ganglia selection and suppression of motor programs allows unwanted movements to interrupt and blend into voluntary activity. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Chorea does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Evidence Limitations

Mild chorea can resemble fidgeting or restlessness, and severe chorea can overlap with athetosis or ballism; medication effects, tics, akathisia, and functional movement require distinction. Therefore, Chorea cannot confirm or exclude basal-ganglia and motor-network dysfunction from hereditary, autoimmune, infectious, vascular, metabolic, endocrine, medication, or toxic causes by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from athetosis, myoclonus, tics, akathisia, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Chorea may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.
