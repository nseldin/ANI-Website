Definition

Bacillus anthracis is a clinically important bacterium associated with cutaneous anthrax, inhalation anthrax, gastrointestinal anthrax, and injection anthrax. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Bacillus anthracis is a Gram-positive bacillus with a facultative anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, spore-forming, nonmotile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Occupational vaccination where indicated, animal-health control, exposure management, and avoidance of suspect animal products reduce risk; routine person spread is uncommon. The reviewed route description is the reviewed zoonotic transmission and environmental exposure transmission route categories. Onset varies substantially by cutaneous, inhalational, gastrointestinal, or injection route, and inhalational spores can remain dormant. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Environmentally persistent spores can germinate after route-specific exposure; capsule and edema and lethal toxins support local injury, edema, and systemic shock. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Bacillus anthracis is associated with cutaneous anthrax, inhalation anthrax, gastrointestinal anthrax, and injection anthrax. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Suspected anthrax requires immediate clinical, laboratory, and public-health coordination with culture and molecular confirmation from an appropriate specimen. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
