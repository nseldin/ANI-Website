Definition

Heat intolerance means a person feels excessively hot, sweaty, or unwell in ordinary warmth that they previously tolerated. It is a reported symptom and does not automatically mean the core temperature is high.

Plain Language

The person becomes uncomfortably hot in temperatures that do not usually bother other people.

Why It Matters

Persistent heat intolerance can support concern for hyperthyroidism, medication or stimulant effects, vasomotor symptoms, or autonomic dysfunction. Confusion, collapse, seizure, severe dehydration, unstable vital signs, or a very high measured temperature after heat exposure is heat illness, not simple intolerance, and requires emergency care.

Associated Conditions

  • hyperthyroidism, menopause or vasomotor symptoms, medication or stimulant effects, autonomic dysfunction, anxiety, fever, deconditioning, or environmental heat exposure

Assessment Method

Ask what temperatures and activities trigger symptoms, whether others are comfortable, and about sweating, flushing, palpitations, tremor, sleep, weight change, medications, substance exposure, menstrual context, and duration; measure temperature, heart rate, hydration, and mental status when symptomatic.

Positive Finding

The patient feels excessively hot, uncomfortable, sweaty, or unable to function in ordinary warmth that was previously tolerated, without necessarily having an elevated core temperature.

Mechanism

Increased metabolic heat production, heightened adrenergic signaling, vasomotor instability, altered sweating or blood-flow control, medication effects, or changed thermal perception can reduce heat tolerance.

Clinical Interpretation

Evidence Limitations

Thermal comfort is subjective and depends on environment, clothing, activity, age, fitness, hydration, hormones, medication, and baseline; absence does not exclude thyroid disease. Therefore, Heat intolerance cannot confirm or exclude hyperthyroidism, menopause or vasomotor symptoms, medication or stimulant effects, autonomic dysfunction, anxiety, fever, deconditioning, or environmental heat exposure by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive response can reflect another painful or sensory process such as fever, hot flash, heat illness, medication effect, or a trigger that was not applied as defined.

False Negative Situations

Heat intolerance may not reproduce when the trigger is incomplete, symptoms are intermittent, treatment has reduced the response, or communication is limited.
