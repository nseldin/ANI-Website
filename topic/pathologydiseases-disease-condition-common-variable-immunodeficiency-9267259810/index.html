Definition

Common variable immunodeficiency is a heterogeneous primary antibody-failure syndrome characterized by persistently low IgG, low IgA or IgM, impaired antibody function, and exclusion of secondary or more specific immune disorders. Recurrent infection is common, but autoimmunity, inflammatory organ disease, lymphoproliferation, or malignancy may be the first clue.

Pathology

B cells are often present but fail to mature effectively into class-switched memory cells and antibody-secreting plasma cells. Tissue may show bronchiectasis, granulomatous-lymphocytic interstitial lung disease, nodular lymphoid hyperplasia, enteropathy, splenomegaly, granulomas, or lymphoma; these are complications, not required findings in every patient.

Pathophysiology

  • Reduced antibody quantity and poor antigen-specific response impair opsonization and mucosal defense, especially against encapsulated respiratory bacteria.

  • Broader B-cell and T-cell dysregulation also breaks immune tolerance and control of lymphoid growth. Immunoglobulin replacement restores circulating IgG protection against many infections but does not fully correct mucosal IgA loss, autoimmunity, granulomatous inflammation, or cancer risk.

Etiology

Most CVID is genetically complex or unexplained; a minority has a defined monogenic immune defect. The phenotype can emerge in childhood or adulthood and may evolve over time. CVID is a diagnosis of exclusion because medication effects, protein loss, hematologic malignancy, HIV, nephrotic disease, and other primary immune deficiencies can produce a similar low-immunoglobulin pattern.

Risk Factors

  • Family history of antibody deficiency or immune dysregulation

  • Persistently low immunoglobulins with poor vaccine responses

  • Diagnostic delay after recurrent sinopulmonary infection

  • Existing bronchiectasis, autoimmunity, granulomatous disease, enteropathy, splenomegaly, or lymphadenopathy

Signs Symptoms

  • Recurrent or unusually severe sinusitis, otitis, bronchitis, and pneumonia, often with encapsulated bacteria

  • Chronic cough, crackles, clubbing, or bronchiectasis from repeated airway injury

  • Persistent diarrhea, giardiasis, malabsorption, weight loss, or inflammatory enteropathy

  • Autoimmune cytopenias, inflammatory arthritis, thyroid disease, vitiligo, granulomas, lymphadenopathy, or splenomegaly

  • Poor vaccine response, prolonged infection recovery, lymphoma, or gastric malignancy

Diagnostics

  • Repeat quantitative IgG, IgA, and IgM using age-appropriate reference ranges and confirm persistence before assigning a lifelong diagnosis when the patient is stable.

  • Assess specific antibody responses to prior or supervised test immunization before immunoglobulin replacement when safe and interpretable; replacement antibodies make later vaccine-response testing difficult.

  • Use flow cytometry for B-, T-, and NK-cell subsets and switched-memory B cells, and exclude profound T-cell deficiency or another defined immunodeficiency.

  • Exclude secondary hypogammaglobulinemia from protein-losing gut or kidney disease, medications, lymphoid malignancy, HIV, and other systemic causes.

  • Evaluate lungs, gastrointestinal tract, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, blood counts, and selected genetics according to phenotype. A new mass or node is not presumed benign CVID lymphoid hyperplasia.

Labs

  • IgG is persistently below the age-adjusted reference range with low IgA or IgM; isolated mild IgG reduction is insufficient.

  • Specific antibody responses to protein and polysaccharide antigens are impaired in many diagnostic frameworks.

  • CBC may show immune thrombocytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, neutropenia, or lymphocyte abnormalities.

  • Pulmonary testing and high-resolution imaging may reveal bronchiectasis or interstitial disease, while stool, liver, endoscopic, and biopsy studies are selected for organ symptoms.

Assessment

Assess the lifetime pattern of sinus, ear, lung, and gastrointestinal infections, organisms, antibiotic use, admissions, vaccine response, chronic cough, stool and weight change, autoimmune cytopenia, nodes, spleen symptoms, family history, and immunosuppressive medication exposure. Examine for bronchiectasis, clubbing, lymphoproliferation, granulomatous disease, liver disease, malnutrition, and active infection. Interpret immunoglobulin and vaccine-response data before replacement whenever safe and exclude secondary loss or malignancy.

Differential

Differentiate CVID from X-linked agammaglobulinemia, hyper-IgM and combined immunodeficiency syndromes, transient hypogammaglobulinemia of childhood, IgG subclass or specific-antibody deficiency, medication-induced hypogammaglobulinemia, protein-losing enteropathy, nephrotic syndrome, severe burns, malnutrition, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, lymphoma, thymoma-associated immunodeficiency, myeloma, and HIV.

Treatments

  • Lifelong intravenous or subcutaneous immunoglobulin replacement is the foundation for clinically significant CVID. Dose and interval are individualized to infection control, organ disease, adverse effects, and trough or steady-state levels rather than one universal number.

  • Treat suspected bacterial infection promptly because deficient antibody defense permits infection to progress quickly, and use culture-directed antibiotics when possible. Selected patients with persistent breakthrough infection or bronchiectasis may need prophylactic antibiotics and airway-clearance therapy.

  • Manage autoimmune cytopenia, granulomatous-lymphocytic interstitial lung disease, enteropathy, liver disease, and malignancy with immunology and organ specialists because immunoglobulin alone does not control these complications.

  • For established bronchiectasis or chronic pulmonary impairment, use airway clearance and pulmonary rehabilitation to preserve function. Add individualized vaccination planning, nutrition support for enteropathy, and cancer surveillance because CVID increases lymphoid and gastric malignancy risk.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose CVID from one low IgG result or before excluding secondary causes and more specific immune defects.

  • Do not use routine live vaccines without immunology review; safety and likely benefit depend on residual T-cell function and the specific vaccine.

  • Do not stop immunoglobulin because one trough level is normal; the measured IgG may reflect the replacement that is preventing infection.

  • Immunosuppression for autoimmune or inflammatory complications requires heightened infection prevention and should not be copied from immunocompetent protocols without specialist oversight.

Safety Considerations

  • Immunoglobulin products can cause infusion reactions, headache, aseptic meningitis, hemolysis, thrombosis, or kidney injury; product, rate, hydration, renal function, and thrombotic risk matter.

  • For severe active infection, follow the treating protocol. UKPIN consensus advises postponing immunoglobulin replacement until at least 48 hours of appropriate antimicrobial therapy and clinical improvement unless the immunology team directs otherwise.

  • Fever during an infusion may be a reaction or infection; stop or slow according to protocol and assess rather than masking severe symptoms automatically.

  • Patients may have serious infection without a strong antibody response or dramatic fever.

  • Chronic lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, cytopenia, weight loss, or lung change requires evaluation for inflammatory complications and malignancy.

Nursing Priorities

  • Before each infusion, assess current infection, prior reaction, hydration, renal function, thrombosis risk, product, lot, route, rate plan, and premedication orders. Escalate severe active infection before starting rather than proceeding automatically; verify the antimicrobial course, clinical improvement, and prescriber decision.

  • During and after infusion, monitor vital signs and symptoms such as headache, chills, chest or back pain, dyspnea, rash, hypotension, dark urine, and neurologic change; follow the product-specific reaction protocol.

  • Track infections by site, organism, antibiotics, hospitalization, missed work or school, and relation to the infusion cycle because clinical breakthrough guides dose adjustment.

  • Assess chronic cough, exercise tolerance, stool and weight change, nodes, spleen symptoms, bruising, fatigue, and autoimmune signs because CVID complications can progress even when replacement IgG is adequate.

  • Teach aseptic subcutaneous technique, storage, lot documentation, sharps disposal, and the exact protocol for postponing or escalating a home infusion during infection.

Red Flags

  • Sepsis symptoms, meningismus, respiratory distress, hypoxemia, or rapidly worsening pneumonia

  • Acute infusion-associated chest pain, severe dyspnea, hypotension, focal neurologic change, hemoglobinuria, or oliguria

  • New persistent lymph node, drenching sweats, unexplained weight loss, splenic pain, or progressive cytopenia

  • Hemoptysis, marked decline in lung function, or severe persistent diarrhea with dehydration

Complications

  • Bronchiectasis, chronic sinus and lung disease, respiratory failure, and hearing impairment

  • Autoimmune cytopenias and other autoimmune disease

  • Granulomatous-lymphocytic interstitial lung disease, enteropathy, malabsorption, nodular regenerative liver disease, and portal hypertension

  • Lymphoma and gastric or other malignancy

  • Infusion-related thrombosis, hemolysis, kidney injury, or aseptic meningitis

Prognosis

Immunoglobulin replacement and prompt infection treatment have greatly improved survival. Long-term outcome is increasingly determined by noninfectious complications such as chronic lung disease, immune dysregulation, liver disease, and malignancy. Regular multidisciplinary surveillance is therefore necessary even when infections become infrequent.

Prevention

CVID itself cannot usually be prevented. Early diagnosis and consistent immunoglobulin replacement prevent many bacterial infections and reduce cumulative airway damage. Use individualized vaccination and household-infection prevention, hand hygiene, dental care, smoking avoidance, and prompt evaluation of fever or respiratory decline. Monitor lungs and other involved organs so irreversible complications are recognized before symptoms become advanced.

Patient Education

  • Replacement immunoglobulin supplies antibodies; it controls risk but does not cure the underlying immune dysregulation.

  • Keep an infection and infusion record and bring it to immunology visits.

  • Contact the team before live vaccines, travel vaccines, prolonged antibiotics, or stopping infusions.

  • Seek urgent care for breathing difficulty, meningitis symptoms, sepsis signs, severe infusion reaction, dark urine, reduced urine, or a new focal neurologic symptom.

Special Populations

  • Young children require repeated age-adjusted testing because transient hypogammaglobulinemia and monogenic immune disorders can mimic CVID.

  • Pregnancy usually continues immunoglobulin replacement with maternal-fetal and immunology coordination; maintaining maternal protection also supports passive neonatal antibody transfer.

  • Older adults need especially careful exclusion of lymphoid malignancy and medication-related secondary deficiency.

  • Patients with bronchiectasis, enteropathy, or protein loss may need a higher individualized immunoglobulin requirement and organ-specific therapy.

Nclex Traps

  • CVID is not simply low IgG; impaired function, persistence, and exclusion of secondary causes are essential.

  • A patient can have CVID with autoimmune disease or lymphadenopathy rather than obvious recurrent infection.

  • A normal post-infusion IgG does not mean replacement can be stopped.

  • Immunoglobulin lowers bacterial infection burden but does not eliminate surveillance for bronchiectasis, inflammatory disease, or lymphoma.
