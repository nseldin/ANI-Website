Summary

The respiratory tract conducts, conditions, and protects inspired air before it reaches alveoli, where oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across a thin alveolar-capillary membrane. Ventilation moves gas between atmosphere and alveoli; perfusion brings blood to the exchange surface; diffusion moves each gas down its partial-pressure gradient. Effective oxygenation therefore requires open airways, working respiratory muscles, ventilated alveoli, intact membrane, adequate pulmonary blood flow, and enough hemoglobin—not merely air entering the mouth. Because these steps operate in series, failure at any link can reduce tissue oxygen delivery.

Quick Answer

Air passes through nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and respiratory bronchioles to alveolar ducts and alveoli. Conducting airways warm, humidify, filter, and move mucus; the respiratory zone performs exchange. Type I alveolar cells create a thin diffusion surface, type II cells produce surfactant and help restore epithelium, and alveolar macrophages remove particles. Oxygen crosses from alveolus to blood and binds hemoglobin; carbon dioxide crosses in the opposite direction and is exhaled. The diaphragm creates most quiet inspiratory pressure. The brain adjusts ventilation mainly in response to carbon dioxide-related acidity, with oxygen-sensitive input becoming especially important when oxygen is low.

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The upper tract includes nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, and pharynx; the larynx protects the lower airway and produces voice. The lower tract includes trachea, main bronchi, branching bronchi and bronchioles, and the respiratory zone. Cartilage supports larger airways, while bronchiolar smooth muscle regulates small-airway caliber. The right lung has three lobes and the left usually two, leaving space for the heart. Visceral and parietal pleura with a thin lubricated space couple lungs to the moving chest wall. Alveoli cluster around capillary networks. The diaphragm separates chest and abdomen; intercostal and accessory muscles change thoracic volume. Pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood to alveoli and pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: ventilation and gas exchange

    Text

    Diaphragm contraction enlarges the thorax, makes pleural pressure more negative, expands alveoli, and lowers alveolar pressure so air flows inward. Elastic recoil supports quiet exhalation. Surfactant lowers surface tension, reduces opening work, and helps prevent small alveoli from collapsing. At the exchange membrane, oxygen diffuses from higher alveolar partial pressure into lower-pressure venous blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood to alveolus. Hemoglobin binding maintains the gradient for oxygen uptake. Carbon dioxide is carried dissolved, bound to proteins, and mostly as bicarbonate. Ventilation must refresh alveolar gas and perfusion must remove or deliver gas; failure of either limits exchange despite an intact membrane.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Brainstem networks generate rhythm, and chemoreceptors respond strongly to carbon dioxide-driven hydrogen-ion changes; peripheral receptors also sense low arterial oxygen. Stretch, irritant, and protective reflexes modify breathing. Autonomic tone changes bronchiolar smooth muscle, while epithelium and immune cells influence mucus and inflammation. Gravity and regional mechanics create normal ventilation and perfusion differences. This card teaches the normal foundation and links to, rather than reproduces, the advanced pulmonary cards on shunt, dead space, detailed V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, respiratory failure, mechanical ventilation, and acid-base compensation. Renal bicarbonate handling belongs in the dedicated renal and acid-base entries.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Upper-airway obstruction prevents gas movement before alveoli are reached. Bronchospasm, edema, secretions, or dynamic collapse increase airway resistance. Hypoventilation raises carbon dioxide and lowers alveolar oxygen. Alveolar filling or collapse creates poorly ventilated units; vascular obstruction creates ventilated but poorly perfused units. Interstitial thickening or loss of surface area slows diffusion. Pneumothorax separates lung from chest-wall mechanics and permits recoil collapse. Neuromuscular weakness reduces the pressure available to ventilate. Anemia can severely reduce oxygen content despite acceptable PaO2 and saturation. Carbon monoxide impairs hemoglobin carriage while a standard pulse oximeter can mislead. The failed link predicts whether airway support, oxygen, ventilation, perfusion treatment, or another therapy is needed.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Beta2 agonists relax bronchial smooth muscle; antimuscarinics reduce cholinergic bronchoconstriction; corticosteroids suppress airway inflammation but are not instant bronchodilators. Surfactant therapy supports selected premature neonates. Opioids and sedatives can depress respiratory drive, while neuromuscular blockers stop muscle movement without providing sedation or ventilation. Asthma primarily narrows inflamed airways; pneumonia fills or inflames alveoli; pulmonary edema floods interstitium and alveoli; emphysema destroys exchange surface and recoil; fibrosis thickens tissue; pulmonary embolism blocks perfusion. Oxygen treats hypoxemia but does not by itself open an obstructed airway or ventilate an apneic patient.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    Pulse oximetry estimates hemoglobin saturation and must be interpreted with waveform, perfusion, motion, skin factors, dyshemoglobins, and clinical appearance. Arterial blood gas measures pH, PaCO2, and PaO2 and supports ventilation and oxygenation reasoning; venous gas can answer some but not all questions. Capnography tracks exhaled carbon dioxide and is valuable for ventilation and airway monitoring. Chest radiography and CT show structure; spirometry assesses airflow; lung volumes and diffusing capacity address other mechanics. PaO2 describes dissolved oxygen pressure, not total oxygen content. A normal SpO2 cannot exclude hypercapnia, increased work, impending fatigue, or carbon-monoxide poisoning.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess airway sounds and patency, respiratory rate and pattern, depth, effort, speech, mental status, chest symmetry, breath sounds, cough and secretions, skin color, saturation trend, and hemodynamics. Count respirations rather than accepting an automatically copied value. Position to optimize mechanics when appropriate, mobilize secretions, encourage ordered lung-expansion strategies, administer oxygen and inhaled therapy to a defined target, and reassess response. Escalate before exhaustion: a previously agitated patient becoming quiet with shallow breathing may be worsening. With artificial airways or ventilation, verify tube and circuit safety, humidification, secretion management, skin protection, alarm response, and aspiration prevention per protocol.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Stridor, inability to speak or handle secretions, silent chest in severe asthma, apnea, gasping, cyanosis, rapidly falling saturation, altered mental status, severe accessory-muscle use, paradoxical breathing, unilateral absent breath sounds with instability, or exhaustion requires immediate escalation. Sudden dyspnea with chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, or shock may be pulmonary embolism. Pink frothy sputum suggests pulmonary edema. Smoke or caustic inhalation can produce delayed airway edema. A normal saturation must not reassure when ventilation is failing, and oxygen should never delay opening the airway, assisting ventilation, treating anaphylaxis, decompressing a tension pneumothorax, or activating emergency response.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    The lungs do not actively inflate themselves; respiratory muscles create the pressure change. Breathing, ventilation, oxygenation, and tissue oxygen delivery are related but not interchangeable. Pulmonary arteries are oxygen-poor and pulmonary veins oxygen-rich because vessel names describe direction. Carbon dioxide crosses readily, but its level depends heavily on alveolar ventilation. Pulse oximetry does not measure carbon dioxide. Oxygen can raise saturation without correcting fatigue or apnea. Crackles do not identify one disease by themselves. This normal-anatomy card is not a substitute for disease-specific V/Q, shunt, dead-space, ventilator, ARDS, COPD, or acid-base reasoning.
