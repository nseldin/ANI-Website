Definition

Homan sign is a historical bedside examination maneuver. It is a clue to suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb. A positive finding means calf discomfort occurs during forced ankle dorsiflexion.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Homan sign means calf discomfort occurs during forced ankle dorsiflexion.

Why It Matters

A positive Homan sign response can signal suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb. Dorsiflexion stretches calf muscles and inflamed or tender tissues, but that nonspecific mechanical tension is not unique to venous thrombosis. Homan sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb

Assessment Method

Historically, the examiner dorsiflexed the ankle while the knee was extended and asked about calf pain; modern care should not deliberately rely on or repeatedly provoke this response.

Positive Finding

Calf discomfort occurs during forced ankle dorsiflexion.

Mechanism

Dorsiflexion stretches calf muscles and inflamed or tender tissues, but that nonspecific mechanical tension is not unique to venous thrombosis.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Homan sign response raises concern for suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb because dorsiflexion stretches calf muscles and inflamed or tender tissues, but that nonspecific mechanical tension is not unique to venous thrombosis. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Homan sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Homan sign to focus evaluation of suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb and the reviewed alternatives: calf strain, Achilles or muscle injury, cellulitis, Baker cyst. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Historically, the examiner dorsiflexed the ankle while the knee was extended and asked about calf pain; modern care should not deliberately rely on or repeatedly provoke this response. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Homan sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

The sign has poor and inconsistent sensitivity and specificity, and a negative response cannot reassure against DVT. Therefore, Homan sign cannot confirm or exclude suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • calf strain

  • Achilles or muscle injury

  • cellulitis

  • Baker cyst

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from calf strain, Achilles or muscle injury, cellulitis, Baker cyst, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Homan sign may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Outdated and diagnostically unreliable; it should not be used to confirm or exclude DVT. Homan sign remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude suspected deep-vein thrombosis of the lower limb. Apply the reviewed limitation for Homan sign: The sign has poor and inconsistent sensitivity and specificity, and a negative response cannot reassure against DVT.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Homan sign, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Historically, the examiner dorsiflexed the ankle while the knee was extended and asked about calf pain; modern care should not deliberately rely on or repeatedly provoke this response. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not use forceful calf squeezing or dorsiflexion when DVT, trauma, compartment syndrome, or severe pain is suspected; use validated clinical pathways and ultrasound instead.

Summary

Homan sign is a historical examination finding once associated with deep vein thrombosis: calf discomfort or resistance during forced ankle dorsiflexion, usually described with the knee extended. It is unreliable and outdated for deciding whether DVT is present or absent. Many nonthrombotic problems can make dorsiflexion painful, and many proven DVTs produce no Homan response. It should not be intentionally elicited to diagnose or rule out DVT, and patients should not be taught to perform it on themselves. Modern evaluation begins with symptoms, risk factors, and a validated pretest-probability pathway, then uses a highly sensitive D-dimer where appropriate and compression or duplex ultrasonography according to risk and protocol. Suspected pulmonary embolism or limb-threatening venous disease requires urgent escalation independent of this historical sign.

Quick Answer

Do not use Homan sign as a bedside clot test. If a patient already reports calf pain when moving the ankle, document that symptom without repeatedly forcing dorsiflexion and without calling the response diagnostic. Assess unilateral swelling, pain, tenderness, warmth, erythema, venous distention, risk factors, onset, cardiopulmonary symptoms, and alternative causes. Apply the institution's validated DVT pretest-probability process. In an appropriate low- or unlikely-probability setting, a highly sensitive negative D-dimer may help exclude DVT; a positive D-dimer does not prove a clot. Patients with likely probability generally require compression or duplex ultrasonography, and persistent concern after an initial negative study may require serial imaging according to the pathway. Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, hypoxemia, hypotension, or a severely swollen cyanotic limb is an emergency.

  • Label

    Official and alternative name

    Text

    The sign is most often called Homan sign or Homans sign; possessive forms such as Homan's or Homans' sign also appear. 'Dorsiflexion sign' describes the historical maneuver but can be confused with other causes of pain during ankle movement. It is linked to John Homans and belongs in the history of venous examination, not in a current rule-out strategy. The modern clinical question is suspected deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremity, which should be named directly. Using precise wording matters: 'patient reports calf pain with active ankle movement' is an observation, whereas 'positive Homan sign means DVT' is an unsupported inference. This entry retains the eponym so users can understand old notes and exam questions while clearly labeling its current status.

  • Label

    Historical assessment method—not recommended

    Text

    Historically, the patient lay supine while an examiner extended the knee and forcibly dorsiflexed the ankle; calf pain, discomfort, or resistance was interpreted as positive. Descriptions varied in knee position, degree of force, and what counted as a response, which already weakened reproducibility. Current evidence does not support performing this maneuver. Do not intentionally elicit it to diagnose or rule out DVT, do not repeatedly dorsiflex a painful limb for documentation, and do not teach a patient to test at home. Ordinary observation of spontaneous pain or discomfort during necessary movement can be recorded without turning it into a provocative test. Current assessment uses history, a complete but gentle limb examination, validated pretest probability, and objective laboratory or imaging pathways.

  • Label

    Positive finding in historical usage

    Text

    In historical usage, calf pain or unusual resistance produced by forced ankle dorsiflexion was called a positive Homan sign. That positive label does not establish venous thrombosis. Pain may come from gastrocnemius or soleus strain, Achilles or ankle pathology, cellulitis, edema, ruptured Baker cyst, nerve irritation, trauma, postoperative inflammation, compartment pathology, or nonspecific tenderness. The maneuver also fails to stress or localize every thrombosed venous segment in a consistent way. If an old record states 'Homan positive,' treat it as a report that discomfort occurred during a variable maneuver and seek objective information. If an old record states 'Homan negative,' do not use it to lower current concern without a valid diagnostic pathway.

  • Label

    Associated condition and the actual DVT mechanism

    Text

    The sign was associated with deep vein thrombosis, a clot forming in a deep vein, usually through interacting venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. DVT may cause unilateral swelling, pain, tenderness, warmth, or color change because venous outflow and local inflammation are altered, but it can also be clinically subtle. Proximal clots carry important embolic risk because material can travel through the right heart to the pulmonary arteries. Risk rises with prior VTE, active cancer, surgery or trauma, immobility, hospitalization, pregnancy or postpartum state, estrogen exposure, thrombophilia, and selected chronic illness, but no risk factor or symptom is individually diagnostic. Homan sign neither measures the clot nor explains which of these mechanisms is present.

  • Label

    Why the maneuver lacks a sound diagnostic mechanism

    Text

    The original intuition was that stretching calf structures or veins during dorsiflexion would provoke pain when a thrombosed vein was inflamed. In practice, ankle movement changes tension across muscles, tendons, fascia, nerves, joints, edematous tissue, and perhaps veins at the same time. The resulting discomfort is not specific to a clot, and a clot may be located where the maneuver produces no symptom. Pain perception, knee position, examiner force, postoperative state, and baseline mobility further change the response. A test whose stimulus is poorly standardized and whose response can arise from many tissues cannot reliably separate DVT from its mimics. This explains the false positives and false negatives rather than merely memorizing that the sign is 'bad.'

  • Label

    Interpretation, diagnostic value, and modern reliability status

    Text

    Homan sign is considered unreliable and outdated for DVT diagnosis. Clinical assessment alone cannot establish or exclude venous thromboembolism, and modern guidelines do not build diagnostic pathways around this maneuver. This entry intentionally provides no memorized sensitivity or specificity because estimates vary with historical technique, patient selection, clot location, and reference standard and would imply precision the sign does not have. Its remaining value is historical literacy: recognizing the term in older documentation, understanding why medicine moved to validated probability rules and objective testing, and correcting unsafe self-testing advice. A current answer that relies on a positive or negative Homan sign is weaker than one that integrates pretest probability, D-dimer where appropriate, and ultrasonography.

  • Label

    False positives and false negatives

    Text

    False positives occur when calf or ankle movement hurts for reasons other than DVT, including muscle injury, cellulitis, superficial thrombophlebitis, ruptured popliteal cyst, arthritis, tendon injury, edema, hematoma, postoperative pain, trauma, or compartment disease. False negatives occur when a true clot is asymptomatic, distal or proximal without provoked pain, early, partially occlusive, or simply not mechanically linked to the maneuver. Variation in technique creates both errors: a forceful examiner may provoke nonspecific pain, while a gentle or differently positioned test may provoke none. Analgesia, neuropathy, altered consciousness, paralysis, and communication barriers further distort response. The danger is asymmetric: a false negative can delay anticoagulation and allow embolization, while a false positive can expose a patient to unnecessary imaging, anticoagulation, bleeding, and anxiety.

  • Label

    Current assessment for suspected DVT

    Text

    Begin with onset, unilateral symptoms, prior VTE, cancer, recent surgery or immobilization, pregnancy or postpartum state, estrogen exposure, trauma, infection, central lines, and competing diagnoses. Examine both limbs for circumference difference using a consistent landmark when ordered, edema, tenderness along the deep venous distribution, warmth, erythema, collateral veins, pulses, skin condition, and neurovascular status. Do not massage the limb. Apply a validated clinical prediction rule such as the pathway specified by local policy; the score estimates pretest probability and is not itself a diagnosis. Check immediately for pulmonary embolism symptoms and for phlegmasia or limb threat. The assessment decides which objective test comes next; it does not replace that test.

  • Label

    D-dimer in the probability pathway

    Text

    D-dimer is a fibrin-degradation marker. A highly sensitive negative result can help exclude DVT when pretest probability is low or unlikely and the assay and pathway are appropriate. A positive result is nonspecific because surgery, pregnancy, infection, inflammation, cancer, increasing age, hospitalization, and many other states raise D-dimer without an acute DVT. Therefore, a positive D-dimer does not diagnose a clot and usually leads to imaging in the relevant pathway. In likely or high-probability presentations, beginning with imaging is commonly appropriate because a negative D-dimer strategy may not lower risk enough. Age-adjusted or population-specific approaches require the exact validated protocol. Do not compare this structured use with Homan sign; one is a validated probabilistic tool, the other an unreliable maneuver.

  • Label

    Compression and duplex ultrasonography

    Text

    Compression ultrasonography evaluates whether a vein collapses under probe pressure; an acute thrombus can make a venous segment noncompressible. Duplex studies may add grayscale imaging, color flow, and spectral information. It is the standard imaging approach for lower-extremity DVT, but the exact protocol may examine proximal veins or the whole leg. A negative initial proximal scan does not end every high-suspicion case; serial ultrasonography may be required when the pathway predicts residual risk or symptoms persist. Prior DVT can leave chronic noncompressibility, so comparison with earlier studies helps distinguish new from old disease. Technical limitations, body habitus, edema, dressings, or inaccessible segments can produce an indeterminate study. Objective imaging has limits, but those limits are managed systematically rather than replaced with Homan sign.

  • Label

    Safety precautions

    Text

    Do not intentionally elicit Homan sign, massage a suspected DVT, or encourage vigorous calf manipulation. Routine gentle movement needed for safe assessment is different from repeated provocative testing. Follow mobility, anticoagulation, and imaging orders rather than imposing unprescribed bed rest; current management depends on the patient's stability and treatment plan. Before anticoagulants, communicate bleeding risks, recent surgery, neuraxial procedures, kidney or liver impairment, platelet problems, and concurrent medicines. If pulmonary embolism is suspected, prioritize airway, breathing, circulation, oxygen and monitoring as ordered, rapid medical response, and safe transport. The concern about a maneuver should never distract from the larger risks of embolism, bleeding, or limb-threatening venous obstruction.

  • Label

    Nursing relevance and actions

    Text

    Recognize DVT risk during admission and transitions, ensure prescribed prevention is implemented, and reassess when mobility or condition changes. For suspected DVT, stop treating the symptom as routine muscle pain, compare limbs, obtain vital signs and oxygen saturation, ask about chest symptoms, notify the responsible clinician, and prepare for the probability-based diagnostic plan. Do not chart Homan sign as a routine negative check. Administer anticoagulation only as ordered and monitor for bleeding, platelet changes, kidney-dependent dosing concerns, and new embolic symptoms. Teach patients to report unilateral swelling or pain and emergency cardiopulmonary symptoms; explicitly discourage home dorsiflexion testing because a painless response cannot reassure them and pain cannot diagnose a clot.

  • Label

    Documentation

    Text

    Document symptom onset, location and character of pain, limb circumference with the exact landmark if measured, edema, warmth, color, tenderness location, skin condition, pulses, sensation, movement, risk factors, cardiopulmonary screen, vital signs, pretest-probability tool and score only if within role and policy, tests ordered, results, notifications, and treatment. If pain occurred incidentally with movement, write that observation rather than 'Homan positive.' If an older note uses the eponym, clarify that current management relies on objective testing. Record ultrasound type and whether it was complete, limited, negative, positive, or indeterminate; record the follow-up plan after a negative or limited study when concern persists. Clear documentation prevents a historical sign from displacing an unfinished workup.

  • Label

    Escalation and red flags

    Text

    Activate urgent evaluation for sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, new hypoxemia, tachycardia with distress, hypotension, cyanosis, altered mental status, or cardiac arrest because these can indicate pulmonary embolism. Escalate a leg that becomes massively swollen, cyanotic, extremely painful, numb, weak, blistered, or poorly perfused because phlegmasia and venous gangrene can threaten the limb and circulation. Also escalate progressive unilateral swelling or pain in a high-risk patient even when Homan sign was recorded as negative or an initial test was inconclusive. The emergency decision follows physiology and objective risk, never the response to forced dorsiflexion.

  • Label

    Related diagnostics, diseases, and misconceptions

    Text

    Connect to deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, venous thromboembolism prevention, Wells or other validated pretest-probability rules, D-dimer, compression and duplex ultrasonography, serial ultrasound, superficial thrombophlebitis, cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, muscle strain, hematoma, compartment syndrome, post-thrombotic syndrome, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, anticoagulants, and bleeding assessment. The main trap is treating an eponym as a modern test. Do not infer DVT from calf pain during dorsiflexion, do not rule it out when no pain occurs, do not quote a universal accuracy number, and do not teach self-testing. The durable replacement is probability-guided objective diagnosis.
