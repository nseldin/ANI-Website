Definition

Hemodialysis circulates blood through an extracorporeal dialyzer where a semipermeable membrane permits controlled solute and water exchange with dialysate. Diffusion, convection, and ultrafiltration are distinct processes, and the delivered treatment depends on time, flows, membrane, access, interruptions, and the patient's distribution volume and catabolic state.

Etiology

HD is used for kidney failure, severe AKI, selected poisonings, and refractory electrolyte, acid-base, volume, or uremic complications. It requires vascular access and enough circulatory tolerance for the planned rate of solute and fluid removal.

Pathology

Blood flows through hollow fibers while dialysate flows on the opposite side, usually countercurrent to blood so a concentration gradient is preserved along the dialyzer. Small solutes cross down concentration gradients; water crosses under transmembrane pressure; solutes dissolved in that moving water undergo convective transport.

Pathophysiology

Potassium, urea, and other small solutes diffuse efficiently when the blood-dialysate gradient, membrane area, and flow are high. Bicarbonate-rich dialysate corrects acidosis by diffusion, while dialysate electrolyte composition shapes the direction and speed of change. Ultrafiltration reduces plasma water, but interstitial fluid must refill the vascular space; when removal exceeds refill and cardiovascular compensation, hypotension and organ ischemia follow. Solute rebound occurs after treatment as material redistributes from tissues, explaining why a post-treatment value does not equal total-body removal.

Risk Factors

  • Catheter access, poor fistula or graft flow, recirculation, or repeated interruptions

  • Large interdialytic gains or aggressive ultrafiltration needs

  • Autonomic dysfunction, cardiac disease, low albumin, sepsis, or frailty

  • Very high urea, first treatment, neurologic disease, or severe dysnatremia increasing osmotic-shift risk

Signs Symptoms

  • Expected fatigue can occur, but chest pain, severe dyspnea, neurologic change, fever, or persistent hypotension is not routine

  • Cramps, nausea, dizziness, headache, or yawning can precede intradialytic hypotension

  • Chills or fever can indicate access or circuit infection

  • Prolonged access bleeding, altered thrill, or poor machine flows suggest access dysfunction

Diagnostics

  • Verify pre/post weight, target weight, pressure, volume exam, access findings, prescription, and recent labs

  • Assess delivered adequacy, access recirculation, and treatment interruptions when clearance is poor

  • Review medications for dialysis removal and timing

  • Investigate symptoms rather than labeling every event a routine dialysis reaction

Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, BUN, sodium, calcium, phosphorus, glucose, and hemoglobin guide treatment context

  • Kt/V or urea reduction measures estimate small-solute delivery but do not measure every toxin or symptom

  • Rapid post-dialysis shifts can rebound

  • Residual kidney clearance adds to total weekly clearance

Treatments

  • Individualize treatment time, frequency, dialyzer, flows, dialysate, anticoagulation, and ultrafiltration goal

  • Reduce or stop ultrafiltration and treat the cause when hypotension develops

  • Correct access dysfunction, infection, underdialysis, and medication-timing errors

  • Use gentler initial or modified prescriptions when disequilibrium or hemodynamic risk is high

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify dialyzer, bath composition, blood and dialysate flows, treatment time, ultrafiltration goal, anticoagulation, and ordered weight because each prescription element changes solute or fluid removal.

  • Trend pre-, intra-, and post-treatment blood pressure, weight, symptoms, access pressures, circuit status, potassium, bicarbonate, urea metrics, and volume findings because adequate clearance can coexist with unsafe fluid removal.

  • Distinguish diffusion of small solutes, convection with solvent drag, and pressure-driven ultrafiltration because troubleshooting depends on whether the problem is concentration, membrane flow, or water removal.

  • Administer medications and meals at dialysis-appropriate times while checking dialyzability because the circuit can remove therapeutic drugs and intradialytic intake can worsen hypotension or treatment tolerance.

  • Stop or escalate per protocol for chest pain, severe hypotension, air or blood-leak alarm, hemolysis signs, seizure, or access hemorrhage because dialysis complications can progress within minutes.

Complications

  • Intradialytic hypotension, cramps, myocardial or cerebral stunning, arrhythmia

  • Access infection, thrombosis, stenosis, bleeding, or catheter bloodstream infection

  • Disequilibrium, electrolyte shift, hemolysis, air embolism, circuit clotting, or blood loss

  • Underdialysis and protein-energy wasting

Contraindications

  • No absolute universal contraindication applies when HD is lifesaving, but modality and prescription must change for severe hemodynamic or intracranial risk

  • Do not pursue aggressive ultrafiltration merely to reach a scale number when perfusion is failing

Red Flags

  • Air detector or blood-leak alarm with suspected patient exposure

  • Chest pain, malignant dysrhythmia, severe hypotension, or loss of consciousness

  • Sudden back pain, dyspnea, dark blood, hyperkalemia, or suspected hemolysis

  • Access disconnection, uncontrolled bleeding, or rapidly expanding hematoma

Patient Education

  • Explain that dialysis removes small wastes mainly by diffusion and excess water by ultrafiltration, while convection carries solutes with moving water.

  • Teach patients to complete the prescribed treatment time because shortening sessions reduces clearance and often forces faster, less tolerable fluid removal later.

Nclex Traps

  • Diffusion removes solute; ultrafiltration removes water; convection couples solute to water movement

  • Dialysis hypotension can injure organs and is not harmless

  • A completed session may still deliver inadequate clearance if access or interruptions limit it

  • Do not take BP or routine blood draws from a fistula or graft arm

Causal Links

  • Concentration gradient across dialyzer -> diffusion -> small-solute removal

  • Transmembrane pressure -> ultrafiltration -> plasma-water removal

  • Ultrafiltration faster than plasma refill -> reduced circulating volume -> hypotension and ischemia

  • Access dysfunction or interruption -> lower delivered blood processing -> underdialysis
