Definition

AV fistula thrombosis is clot occlusion of a hemodialysis fistula that reduces or stops access flow. An absent or abruptly changed thrill is an urgent finding because early declotting and correction of the underlying lesion can determine whether the access is saved.

Pathology

Neointimal hyperplasia narrows the access circuit, creating turbulence, pressure change, and low-flow zones. A clot then propagates through the fistula, eliminating the continuous flow responsible for its thrill and bruit.

Pathophysiology

Stenosis progressively raises venous pressures, prolongs post-cannulation bleeding, worsens recirculation, and reduces delivered dialysis before complete thrombosis occurs. Once flow stops, potassium, water, and uremic clearance may be interrupted and a temporary catheter may be required. Prolonged thrombosis becomes harder to salvage as clot organizes, so waiting until the next scheduled session can lose the access.

Etiology

Most thrombosed fistulas have an underlying inflow or outflow stenosis. Hypotension, dehydration, excessive compression after needle removal, infection, trauma, hypercoagulability, aneurysmal change, or poor cardiac output can further reduce flow and trigger clotting.

Risk Factors

  • Access stenosis or prior thrombosis

  • Intradialytic hypotension, dehydration, low cardiac output, or excessive compression

  • Infection, trauma, hypercoagulability, or cannulation injury

  • Missed surveillance clues such as prolonged bleeding or declining flows

Signs Symptoms

  • Absent, weak, discontinuous, or changed thrill and bruit

  • Difficult cannulation, poor blood flow, pressure alarms, recirculation, or inadequate dialysis

  • Swelling, pain, or venous collateral development

  • Hyperkalemia or overload if dialysis is delayed

Diagnostics

  • Immediately inspect, palpate, and auscultate the full access

  • Review prior pressure, flow, adequacy, and bleeding trends

  • Use duplex ultrasound or fistulography to locate thrombus and stenosis

  • Assess distal hand perfusion and urgent dialysis need

Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, BUN, creatinine, and volume status guide urgency

  • CBC and coagulation studies are selected for procedure and bleeding context

  • Adequacy decline may precede complete thrombosis

  • Cultures are needed when infection may have triggered thrombosis

Treatments

  • Notify the dialysis and vascular-access team urgently

  • Perform endovascular or surgical thrombectomy and correct underlying stenosis when salvageable

  • Use temporary access only when dialysis cannot safely wait

  • Treat infection, hypotension, compression, or hypercoagulable contributors

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess the entire fistula for thrill, bruit, pulse, aneurysmal change, cannulation difficulty, prolonged bleeding, arm swelling, and distal circulation because stenosis often precedes complete thrombosis.

  • Compare findings with the documented baseline before every dialysis session and trend access pressures and delivered clearance because declining flow may be detectable before the thrill disappears.

  • Stop cannulation and notify the dialysis and access team immediately when thrill or bruit is absent because rapid thrombectomy or revision offers the best chance to preserve the access.

  • Protect the access arm from blood pressure cuffs, venipuncture, constriction, dehydration, and prolonged direct compression because reduced inflow or outflow encourages clot formation.

  • Activate emergency evaluation for a cold painful hand, motor or sensory loss, rapidly expanding swelling, severe bleeding, chest symptoms, or sepsis because limb ischemia, hemorrhage, central obstruction, or infection may coexist.

Complications

  • Permanent access loss and catheter dependence

  • Missed dialysis, hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, acidosis, and uremia

  • Procedure bleeding, embolic or vascular injury

  • Recurrent thrombosis if stenosis is not corrected

Contraindications

  • Do not inherit or use the unsafe alias 'atrioventricular' for this dialysis access

  • Do not wait for the next dialysis session after loss of thrill

  • Do not apply prolonged heavy pressure to force hemostasis

Red Flags

  • Absent thrill or bruit in a previously functioning fistula

  • Cold painful hand with pallor, numbness, weakness, or reduced pulse

  • Rapidly expanding arm swelling, hematoma, or uncontrolled bleeding

  • Fever, rigors, drainage, or spreading erythema near the access

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to feel for the fistula thrill every day and call the access team immediately if it disappears rather than waiting for dialysis.

  • Explain that blood pressure cuffs, blood draws, tight clothing, and sleeping directly on the access can reduce flow and threaten the fistula.

Nclex Traps

  • Absent thrill is urgent

  • Stenosis usually precedes thrombosis

  • Excess pressure after needle removal can stop flow

  • Dialysis consequences become part of the access emergency
