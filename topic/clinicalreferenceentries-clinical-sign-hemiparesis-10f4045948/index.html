Definition

Hemiparesis means partial weakness on one side of the body. Voluntary movement is still present, which distinguishes it from hemiplegia, or paralysis of one side.

Plain Language

The face, arm, leg, or the whole side is weaker than the other side but can still move to some degree.

Why It Matters

Sudden hemiparesis is a classic stroke warning sign and needs immediate assessment, last-known-well documentation, glucose testing, and stroke activation. The weak side also needs protection from falls, shoulder injury, pressure injury, and unsafe swallowing or transfers.

Associated Conditions

  • partial weakness of the face, arm, and or leg on one side from a contralateral cerebral or selected brainstem corticospinal lesion, commonly stroke, or less often spinal, demyelinating, traumatic, neoplastic, or functional disease

Assessment Method

Compare facial movement, arm and leg strength, grip, pronator drift, sustained antigravity posture, gait, tone, reflexes, sensation, speech, and neglect on both sides; document exact muscles and strength rather than only one-sided weakness.

Positive Finding

Strength is reduced on one side of the body but voluntary movement remains, with the distribution ranging from face or one limb to the entire side.

Mechanism

Damage to descending motor pathways before or after their crossing reduces motor drive to muscles on one side; the pattern and associated cranial, sensory, and cortical findings help localize the lesion.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Hemiparesis finding raises concern for partial weakness of the face, arm, and or leg on one side from a contralateral cerebral or selected brainstem corticospinal lesion, commonly stroke, or less often spinal, demyelinating, traumatic, neoplastic, or functional disease because damage to descending motor pathways before or after their crossing reduces motor drive to muscles on one side; the pattern and associated cranial, sensory, and cortical findings help localize the lesion. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Hemiparesis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Hemiparesis to focus evaluation of partial weakness of the face, arm, and or leg on one side from a contralateral cerebral or selected brainstem corticospinal lesion, commonly stroke, or less often spinal, demyelinating, traumatic, neoplastic, or functional disease and the reviewed alternatives: peripheral nerve injury, radiculopathy, pain-limited movement, functional weakness. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compare facial movement, arm and leg strength, grip, pronator drift, sustained antigravity posture, gait, tone, reflexes, sensation, speech, and neglect on both sides; document exact muscles and strength rather than only one-sided weakness. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Hemiparesis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Pain, orthopedic restriction, poor effort, neglect, fatigue, hypoglycemia, and peripheral nerve or root lesions can produce asymmetric performance; a brief screen cannot establish the level or cause. Therefore, Hemiparesis cannot confirm or exclude partial weakness of the face, arm, and or leg on one side from a contralateral cerebral or selected brainstem corticospinal lesion, commonly stroke, or less often spinal, demyelinating, traumatic, neoplastic, or functional disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • peripheral nerve injury

  • radiculopathy

  • pain-limited movement

  • functional weakness

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as peripheral nerve injury, radiculopathy, pain-limited movement, functional weakness.

False Negative Situations

Hemiparesis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of partial weakness of the face, arm, and or leg on one side from a contralateral cerebral or selected brainstem corticospinal lesion, commonly stroke, or less often spinal, demyelinating, traumatic, neoplastic, or functional disease.

Modern Status

Current high-priority localization sign; hemiparesis means partial weakness and should not be used as a synonym for complete hemiplegia. Interpret Hemiparesis in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Pain, orthopedic restriction, poor effort, neglect, fatigue, hypoglycemia, and peripheral nerve or root lesions can produce asymmetric performance; a brief screen cannot establish the level or cause.

Safety Precautions

Observe Hemiparesis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Compare facial movement, arm and leg strength, grip, pronator drift, sustained antigravity posture, gait, tone, reflexes, sensation, speech, and neglect on both sides; document exact muscles and strength rather than only one-sided weakness. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Treat sudden hemiparesis as stroke until urgently evaluated, record last known well, check glucose, protect the weak limb, and assess swallowing, falls, and pressure-injury risk.
