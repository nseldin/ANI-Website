Definition

Dystonia is a movement disorder in which sustained or intermittent patterned movements repeatedly twist or pull a body part into an abnormal posture. The movement may also be tremulous or jerky.

Plain Language

Muscles repeatedly pull a body part into the same twisting movement or unusual posture.

Why It Matters

Dystonia can impair vision, speech, swallowing, breathing, walking, or hand use. New jaw, tongue, neck, eye, or throat dystonia after an antipsychotic or anti-nausea medicine can be an acute medication reaction, and airway involvement is an emergency.

Associated Conditions

  • abnormal central motor-control activity that may be isolated, genetic, medication-induced, degenerative, structural, metabolic, or part of another neurologic disorder

Assessment Method

Observe at rest, during posture, walking, speech, writing, and the task that triggers symptoms; document body distribution, patterned direction, sustained versus intermittent course, twisting, tremulous or jerky quality, overflow, pain, sensory tricks, sleep effect, recent dopamine-blocking or antiemetic medication, airway and swallowing symptoms, and functional impairment.

Positive Finding

Sustained or intermittent patterned movements or postures repeatedly twist or pull a body region in a characteristic direction and may worsen with voluntary action.

Mechanism

Abnormal sensorimotor-network inhibition and integration recruit muscles in an excessive patterned manner, producing repeated movement, posture, or overflow into nearby muscles.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Dystonia response raises concern for abnormal central motor-control activity that may be isolated, genetic, medication-induced, degenerative, structural, metabolic, or part of another neurologic disorder because abnormal sensorimotor-network inhibition and integration recruit muscles in an excessive patterned manner, producing repeated movement, posture, or overflow into nearby muscles. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Dystonia does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Dystonia to focus evaluation of abnormal central motor-control activity that may be isolated, genetic, medication-induced, degenerative, structural, metabolic, or part of another neurologic disorder and the reviewed alternatives: myoclonus, spasticity, tetany, muscle spasm. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Observe at rest, during posture, walking, speech, writing, and the task that triggers symptoms; document body distribution, patterned direction, sustained versus intermittent course, twisting, tremulous or jerky quality, overflow, pain, sensory tricks, sleep effect, recent dopamine-blocking or antiemetic medication, airway and swallowing symptoms, and functional impairment. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Dystonia as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Muscle spasm, tetany, seizure, rigidity, orthopedic deformity, functional movement, and pain guarding can resemble dystonia; a sensory trick can support but does not prove the diagnosis. Therefore, Dystonia cannot confirm or exclude abnormal central motor-control activity that may be isolated, genetic, medication-induced, degenerative, structural, metabolic, or part of another neurologic disorder by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • myoclonus

  • spasticity

  • tetany

  • muscle spasm

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from myoclonus, spasticity, tetany, muscle spasm, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Dystonia may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current movement-disorder phenotype with many causes; distribution, onset, time course, medication exposure, and associated neurologic findings guide classification. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Dystonia, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Observe at rest, during posture, walking, speech, writing, and the task that triggers symptoms; document body distribution, patterned direction, sustained versus intermittent course, twisting, tremulous or jerky quality, overflow, pain, sensory tricks, sleep effect, recent dopamine-blocking or antiemetic medication, airway and swallowing symptoms, and functional impairment. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Acute jaw, tongue, neck, eye, or laryngeal dystonia after a dopamine-blocking medication requires immediate medication and airway assessment; stridor, drooling, cyanosis, inability to swallow, generalized painful contractions, fever, autonomic instability, or dark urine requires emergency care.

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."
