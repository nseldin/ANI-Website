Definition

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is an aggressive mature B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by sheets of large abnormal lymphoid cells that grow diffusely and can enlarge quickly. The general ANI card focuses on DLBCL not otherwise specified; primary mediastinal, primary CNS, EBV-positive, transformed, and other defined entities require their own classification.

Pathology

The tumor effaces normal lymph-node or extranodal architecture with large atypical B cells. Different molecular programs alter proliferation, B-cell-receptor and NF-kappa-B signaling, apoptosis, and immune escape, explaining why two tumors with the same broad name can behave differently and respond to different risk-adapted strategies.

Pathophysiology

  • Clonal B cells divide rapidly and accumulate in lymph nodes or almost any extranodal organ, which produces a growing mass, organ dysfunction, and release of lactate dehydrogenase from high cellular turnover.

  • A large chemosensitive tumor burden can break down abruptly after treatment, releasing potassium, phosphate, and nucleic-acid metabolites. This is why tumor-lysis prevention and monitoring begin before the first cycle rather than after kidney injury or arrhythmia appears.

Etiology

Most DLBCL has no single identifiable cause. Risk is increased by age, HIV or another immunodeficiency, immunosuppressive therapy, selected autoimmune or chronic inflammatory states, defined virus-associated entities, and transformation from an indolent lymphoma. These contexts guide classification but do not make every enlarged node lymphoma.

Risk Factors

  • Older age

  • HIV, post-transplant immunosuppression, congenital immune deficiency, or another major immune-compromising state

  • Transformation from follicular or another indolent lymphoma

  • Selected autoimmune or chronic inflammatory disorders and defined virus-associated lymphoma settings

Signs Symptoms

  • Rapidly enlarging, usually painless lymph node or extranodal mass

  • Fever, drenching night sweats, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or pruritus

  • Abdominal pain, early satiety, obstruction, bleeding, cough, dyspnea, bone pain, skin lesion, or another organ-specific manifestation

  • Cytopenia, recurrent infection, bruising, or bone pain when marrow is involved

  • Neurologic symptoms, testicular mass, kidney injury, or other high-risk extranodal presentation requiring urgent staging

Diagnostics

  • Obtain adequate tissue for expert hematopathology. Excisional biopsy is preferred when feasible; a well-planned core biopsy may suffice, while fine-needle aspiration alone often cannot establish architecture or complete classification.

  • Use morphology, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry when appropriate, and cytogenetic or molecular testing including MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 evaluation to distinguish DLBCL from other high-grade lymphomas.

  • Stage primarily with FDG PET-CT and assess marrow or CNS only when the clinical and biologic risk profile warrants it.

  • Calculate prognostic risk from age, stage, LDH, performance status, extranodal burden, biology, and response rather than assuming one prognosis from the title alone. Suspected relapse generally needs repeat biopsy.

Labs

  • CBC with differential identifies marrow suppression or baseline cytopenia; chemistry, renal and liver tests determine organ function for therapy.

  • LDH reflects turnover and contributes to prognostic assessment but is not diagnostic by itself.

  • Uric acid, potassium, phosphate, calcium, creatinine, and urine output establish tumor-lysis risk and response to prevention.

  • Screen for hepatitis B before anti-CD20 therapy and assess hepatitis C, HIV, pregnancy, and fertility according to the treatment plan.

  • Baseline cardiac evaluation is required when anthracycline therapy is planned and should be repeated when symptoms or cumulative-risk guidance indicates.

Assessment

Map every node or mass, B symptom, performance change, infection sign, neurologic symptom, organ-compression finding, and baseline comorbidity. Before treatment, document cardiac, renal, hepatic, neurologic, reproductive, viral, and tumor-lysis risks. During therapy, trend temperature, CBC, metabolic markers, intake and output, mucosa, neuropathy, cardiac symptoms, infusion response, and functional recovery.

Differential

Differentiate DLBCL from Burkitt lymphoma, defined high-grade B-cell lymphomas, primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma, transformed follicular lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, B-lymphoblastic lymphoma, plasmablastic lymphoma, reactive or infectious lymphadenopathy, and metastatic malignancy. Classification matters because treatment intensity, CNS strategy, and prognosis differ.

Treatments

  • Use curative-intent immunochemotherapy when appropriate. R-CHOP remains a major standard, while polatuzumab-R-CHP is used for selected previously untreated patients under current oncology guidance.

  • Localized disease may receive risk-adapted abbreviated or full systemic therapy with selected radiation; advanced disease generally requires full systemic treatment.

  • Relapsed or refractory disease may require salvage therapy, autologous stem-cell transplantation, CAR-T therapy, bispecific antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, or a clinical trial according to timing, fitness, biology, and prior therapy.

  • Provide tumor-lysis prevention, antiemetic support, infection prevention, growth-factor or transfusion support when indicated, fertility counseling, and symptom-directed palliative care alongside disease treatment.

Contraindications

  • Do not start therapy from a fine-needle sample or imaging impression when adequate tissue can safely establish the lymphoma subtype.

  • Rituximab or another anti-CD20 antibody requires hepatitis B screening; prior infection may require antiviral prophylaxis and monitoring rather than automatic denial of curative treatment.

  • Anthracycline use requires individualized cardiac assessment; do not assume a normal symptom review excludes reduced ventricular function.

  • Vincristine neurotoxicity and drug interactions require assessment; worsening neuropathy, ileus, or weakness must not be mistaken for cancer progression.

  • Live vaccines are generally avoided during substantial immunosuppression, and fertility- or pregnancy-related risks must be addressed before treatment when time permits.

Safety Considerations

  • Tumor lysis can begin spontaneously or after the first treatment and requires planned hydration, urate-lowering strategy, laboratory frequency, and escalation thresholds.

  • Rituximab can cause serious infusion reactions, severe mucocutaneous reactions, hepatitis B reactivation, and rare progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy.

  • Doxorubicin is a vesicant and can cause cardiomyopathy; verify line patency and stop immediately for suspected extravasation.

  • Neutropenic fever is an emergency even when the patient appears initially stable.

  • A new focal neurologic finding or testicular, kidney, adrenal, or selected other extranodal involvement may change CNS evaluation and prophylaxis planning.

Nursing Priorities

  • Before the first cycle, verify tissue diagnosis, regimen, consent, baseline CBC and organ tests, hepatitis B status, cardiac assessment, pregnancy or fertility plan, vascular access, tumor-lysis plan, and emergency contacts.

  • During antibody infusion, monitor closely for fever, rigors, rash, dyspnea, bronchospasm, hypoxia, hypotension, or angioedema and follow the protocol for stopping, treating, and restarting.

  • Trend temperature, CBC, potassium, phosphate, calcium, uric acid, creatinine, LDH, hydration, urine output, weight, mucositis, nutrition, bowel function, and neuropathy.

  • Use vesicant precautions for doxorubicin and escalate pain, burning, swelling, loss of blood return, or leakage immediately.

  • Teach infection precautions, neutropenic-fever action, oral care, medication-specific adverse effects, fertility considerations, and why follow-up imaging timing is standardized rather than performed after every symptom.

Red Flags

  • Fever or sepsis signs during neutropenia

  • Dyspnea, stridor, facial swelling, venous congestion, or hemodynamic compromise from airway or superior vena cava compression

  • Oliguria, arrhythmia, weakness, tetany, seizure, or abrupt metabolic change suggesting tumor lysis

  • Severe infusion reaction or suspected doxorubicin extravasation

  • New confusion, seizure, focal deficit, spinal-cord compression symptom, or rapidly worsening organ dysfunction

Complications

  • Tumor lysis syndrome, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, and seizure

  • Marrow failure, infection, bleeding, and treatment-related immunosuppression

  • Organ compression, CNS involvement, or tissue destruction at extranodal sites

  • Peripheral neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, infertility, secondary malignancy, and other treatment toxicity

  • Refractory disease or relapse

Prognosis

DLBCL is aggressive but often curable. Prognosis depends on clinical risk score, tumor biology, stage, involved sites, patient fitness, and depth and durability of response. Relapse timing and treatment eligibility guide the next strategy; a past remission does not make a new mass automatically recurrent lymphoma without reassessment.

Prevention

There is no general screening or prevention program. Reduce avoidable treatment harm through vaccination and infection planning before immunosuppression when feasible, hepatitis B management, fertility counseling, cardiac-risk control, and surveillance appropriate to the treatment received. HIV control and careful monitoring after transplantation reduce some lymphoma risk contexts.

Patient Education

  • The disease grows quickly, but rapid growth also makes many tumors responsive to curative-intent chemotherapy.

  • Call immediately for fever, shaking chills, breathing difficulty, reduced urine, new weakness or confusion, chest pain, or rapidly enlarging swelling.

  • Do not take new OTC medicines, supplements, or live vaccines without oncology review because interactions and immune suppression matter.

  • Keep the treatment schedule and laboratory appointments; tumor-lysis and blood-count monitoring are preventive care, not optional paperwork.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents generally follow pediatric mature B-cell lymphoma protocols rather than adult DLBCL regimens.

  • Very old or frail adults need geriatric and organ-function assessment to preserve curative intent when possible without unsafe dose assumptions.

  • Pregnancy requires multidisciplinary timing and regimen selection; fertility preservation should be discussed before treatment when clinically feasible.

  • People with HIV, hepatitis B exposure, cardiac disease, or kidney or liver impairment require coordinated disease and treatment-risk management.

Nclex Traps

  • An aggressive lymphoma can be curable; do not confuse rapid growth with treatment futility.

  • The nurse verifies hepatitis B screening before rituximab and cardiac assessment before anthracycline exposure.

  • Fever during neutropenia and metabolic signs of tumor lysis require urgent action, not routine next-visit follow-up.

  • DLBCL and Burkitt lymphoma are distinct; intensive Burkitt CNS-directed protocols are not automatically transferred to ordinary DLBCL.
