Definition

Heat exhaustion is a heat-illness syndrome caused by water and salt loss when prolonged heat exposure and sweating outstrip cardiovascular compensation, without the central nervous system dysfunction that defines heat stroke. The characteristic pattern is heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness, tachycardia, and normal or mildly elevated mental status without delirium or coma. Confirm or stage it with core temperature and repeated mental-status assessment; distinguish it from heat stroke, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, and cardiac ischemia by core temperature, cognition, glucose, and clinical context. The priority is to move to a cool setting, remove excess clothing, and use active cooling because progression to heat stroke can follow if the process advances.

Pathology

Heat exhaustion is a heat-illness syndrome caused by water and salt loss when prolonged heat exposure and sweating outstrip cardiovascular compensation, without the central nervous system dysfunction that defines heat stroke. The characteristic pattern is heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness, tachycardia, and normal or mildly elevated mental status without delirium or coma. Confirm or stage it with core temperature and repeated mental-status assessment; distinguish it from heat stroke, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, and cardiac ischemia by core temperature, cognition, glucose, and clinical context. The priority is to move to a cool setting, remove excess clothing, and use active cooling because progression to heat stroke can follow if the process advances. Mechanistically, heat exposure and sweating deplete water and salt while cardiovascular compensation becomes insufficient, but central nervous system dysfunction of heat stroke is absent.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • core temperature and repeated mental-status assessment

  • electrolytes, kidney function, CK, and liver tests when symptoms are severe or prolonged

  • exposure, exertion, medication, hydration, and comorbidity review

  • Distinguish Heat exhaustion from heat stroke, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, and cardiac ischemia by core temperature, cognition, glucose, and clinical context.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • move to a cool setting, remove excess clothing, and use active cooling

  • replace oral fluid and electrolytes when the patient can drink or intravenous isotonic fluid when needed

  • stop exertion and observe until vital signs, symptoms, and cognition normalize

Nursing Priorities

  • Activate emergency response for altered behavior, seizure, collapse, core temperature near or above 40 degrees Celsius, persistent hypotension, chest pain, dyspnea, repeated vomiting, worsening symptoms, or failure to improve within one hour because heat stroke and organ injury require immediate advanced care.

  • Move the patient out of heat, remove excess clothing and equipment, begin cool wet cloths, fans, or cold packs, and stop exertion because continued heat production can convert heat exhaustion into life-threatening heat stroke.

  • Assess airway, breathing, circulation, rectal core temperature when indicated, mental status, blood pressure, pulse, respirations, oxygen saturation, skin, and glucose because confusion or major central nervous system change indicates heat stroke rather than simple exhaustion.

  • Give cool oral water or electrolyte solution in small repeated amounts only when the patient is alert and not vomiting, or provide prescribed intravenous fluid because safe rehydration restores circulating volume without aspiration.

  • Trend temperature, orthostasis, urine output and color, sodium, potassium, creatinine, creatine kinase, and symptoms during cooling because hyponatremia, acute kidney injury, and exertional rhabdomyolysis may accompany or mimic heat illness.

Complications

  • progression to heat stroke

  • rhabdomyolysis can complicate Heat exhaustion.

  • kidney injury can complicate Heat exhaustion.

  • electrolyte disturbance can complicate Heat exhaustion.

  • syncope and traumatic injury

  • recurrent illness if return to heat is premature

Red Flags

  • Confusion, ataxia, seizure, collapse, or loss of consciousness

  • Core temperature near or above 40 degrees Celsius with systemic illness

  • Persistent hypotension, chest pain, severe dyspnea, or shock findings

  • Repeated vomiting, dark urine, oliguria, severe muscle pain, or worsening after cooling

Patient Education

  • Teach gradual heat acclimatization, scheduled water and rest breaks, breathable clothing, and a buddy system because thirst and self-awareness often lag behind physiologic heat strain.

  • Explain that returning to strenuous work or exercise the same day can trigger recurrence, so activity should resume only after recovery and clinical guidance.

Nclex Traps

  • Confusion, seizure, or coma means heat stroke until proved otherwise

  • Do not delay cooling while waiting for a temperature measurement
