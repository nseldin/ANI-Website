Definition

Silicosis develops when respirable crystalline silica is ingested by alveolar macrophages and drives nodular fibrosis that may accelerate after intense exposure. The characteristic pattern is progressive cough and dyspnea with upper-lung nodules or conglomerate fibrosis after mining, cutting, sandblasting, or engineered-stone exposure. Confirm or stage it with detailed task and control history with high-resolution CT; distinguish it from sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, fungal disease, coal-worker disease, and idiopathic fibrosis by exposure, imaging, microbiology, and tissue when needed. Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • detailed task and control history with high-resolution CT

  • spirometry, lung volumes, diffusion, and oxygen assessment

  • tuberculosis testing and autoimmune, infection, and cancer evaluation when indicated

  • Distinguish Silicosis from sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, fungal disease, coal-worker disease, and idiopathic fibrosis by exposure, imaging, microbiology, and tissue when needed.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • eliminate exposure and enforce effective respiratory controls

  • provide vaccination, smoking cessation, oxygen, rehabilitation, and prompt infection treatment

  • treat latent or active tuberculosis and refer advanced disease for transplant assessment

Nursing Priorities

  • Obtain detailed exposure to engineered stone, mining, sandblasting, construction, foundry, or abrasive dust, including intensity, controls, respirator fit, and latency because high-dose exposure can cause rapidly progressive disease in young workers.

  • Trend cough, dyspnea, chest pain, fatigue, oxygen saturation at rest and exertion, pulmonary function, chest imaging, weight, renal findings, and autoimmune symptoms because silica exposure can injure lung, kidney, and immune systems.

  • Arrange immediate removal from further respirable silica exposure, occupational-medicine care, public-health reporting where required, and workplace control review because silicosis is irreversible but entirely preventable and continued exposure accelerates fibrosis.

  • Screen for tuberculosis according to risk and follow symptoms, testing, and treatment carefully because silicosis markedly increases susceptibility to active tuberculosis and other mycobacterial or fungal infection.

  • Escalate immediately for rapidly worsening dyspnea, new hypoxemia, hemoptysis, fever or night sweats, acute weight loss, chest pain, syncope, or right-heart congestion because acute silicosis, infection, malignancy, embolism, or pulmonary hypertension may be present.

Complications

  • progressive massive fibrosis can complicate Silicosis.

  • respiratory failure can complicate Silicosis.

  • pulmonary hypertension can complicate Silicosis.

  • tuberculosis can complicate Silicosis.

  • autoimmune disease can complicate Silicosis.

  • kidney disease can complicate Silicosis.

  • lung cancer can complicate Silicosis.

Red Flags

  • Rapidly progressive dyspnea, new resting hypoxemia, or respiratory fatigue

  • Hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss, or known tuberculosis exposure

  • New chest pain, suspicious imaging mass, or unexplained systemic decline

  • Syncope, jugular venous distention, edema, cyanosis, or reduced urine output

Patient Education

  • Explain that no medicine removes silica scar, making complete exposure cessation and engineering controls the most important steps for preserving remaining lung function.

  • Teach the patient to share the exact job and material history with every clinician because silicosis and tuberculosis may otherwise be mistaken for more common lung disease.

Nclex Traps

  • Silica exposure markedly raises tuberculosis risk even after exposure ends

  • A disposable mask may not provide adequate protection for high-intensity silica work
