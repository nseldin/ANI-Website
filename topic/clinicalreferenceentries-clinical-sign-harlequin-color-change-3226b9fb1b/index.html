Definition

Harlequin color change is an observed neonatal vascular sign. It is a clue to immature autonomic vasomotor control. A positive finding means the dependent half becomes red while the upper half remains pale with a sharp midline border.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Harlequin color change means the dependent half becomes red while the upper half remains pale with a sharp midline border.

Why It Matters

The observed Harlequin color change finding can signal immature autonomic vasomotor control. Immature hypothalamic control permits gravity-dependent dilation on one side and constriction on the other. Harlequin color change cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • immature autonomic vasomotor control

Assessment Method

Observe a newborn lying on one side during a transient color episode.

Positive Finding

The dependent half becomes red while the upper half remains pale with a sharp midline border.

Mechanism

Immature hypothalamic control permits gravity-dependent dilation on one side and constriction on the other.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Harlequin color change finding raises concern for immature autonomic vasomotor control because immature hypothalamic control permits gravity-dependent dilation on one side and constriction on the other. Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Harlequin color change to focus evaluation of immature autonomic vasomotor control and the reviewed alternatives: port-wine stain, cyanosis, sepsis mottling. Evidence Limitations

Persistent, recurrent with illness, or non-midline changes need evaluation. Therefore, Harlequin color change cannot confirm or exclude immature autonomic vasomotor control by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • port-wine stain

  • cyanosis

  • sepsis mottling

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as port-wine stain, cyanosis, sepsis mottling.

False Negative Situations

Harlequin color change may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of immature autonomic vasomotor control.
