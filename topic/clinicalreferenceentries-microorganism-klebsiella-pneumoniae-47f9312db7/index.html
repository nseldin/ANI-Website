Klebsiella pneumoniae is a clinically important bacterium associated with pneumonia, urinary tract infection, bloodstream infection, and pyogenic liver abscess. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative bacillus with a facultative anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, nonmotile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

  • Content

    Hand hygiene, device care, environmental cleaning, and resistance-directed facility precautions reduce healthcare transmission. The reviewed route description is contact transmission. Endogenous colonization and healthcare acquisition can precede different infection syndromes by variable intervals. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

  • Content

    A prominent capsule, adherence factors, iron acquisition, and strain-dependent hypervirulence or resistance support mucosal colonization and invasive disease. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Klebsiella pneumoniae is associated with pneumonia, urinary tract infection, bloodstream infection, and pyogenic liver abscess. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Culture an appropriate clinical specimen and obtain susceptibility testing; laboratory phenotype is needed to identify carbapenem resistance or other clinically important resistance. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
