Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity to propofol or formulation components; clarify prior anaphylaxis to egg or soy products under the exact product label. Do not use without personnel and equipment able to rescue ventilation and circulation. Avoid or urgently reassess prolonged escalating exposure when acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia or cardiac instability develops.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before dosing, verify weight, indication, airway plan, baseline pressure/volume status, allergies, concurrent opioids or sedatives, pregnancy context and vascular access. Give slowly enough for the clinical context and be ready for immediate apnea and hypotension.

  • For continuous sedation, perform daily indication/depth review when appropriate, maintain analgesia separately, use aseptic tubing/vial time limits, and trend triglycerides, acid-base status, lactate, potassium, creatine kinase, renal function, ECG and hemodynamics when exposure is prolonged or rising.

Interactions

  • Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol and other sedatives compound apnea, hypotension and loss of airway tone. Negative chronotropes can worsen bradycardia. Catecholamines and glucocorticoids are recognized cofactors in many propofol-infusion-syndrome cases during critical illness, especially with high dose and prolonged exposure.

Key Labs

  • Continuous ECG, blood pressure, pulse oximetry and capnography/ventilation

  • Triglycerides during prolonged infusion

  • ABG/VBG, anion gap, lactate, potassium, creatine kinase, renal and hepatic function when infusion syndrome is possible

Nclex Traps

  • Propofol is not an analgesic. A still, amnestic patient can still require pain treatment.

  • White lipid emulsion is a contamination risk; aseptic time limits are a major safety feature.

  • New metabolic acidosis plus rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia or cardiac failure during an infusion is not ordinary sedation tolerance.

Class

Rapid-onset IV GABA-A positive-modulating sedative-hypnotic general anesthetic

Used To Treat

Induction and maintenance of general anesthesia, monitored-anesthesia-care sedation, and selected mechanically ventilated adult ICU sedation under continuous airway and hemodynamic monitoring; it provides hypnosis and amnesia but essentially no analgesia.

Mechanism

Propofol increases GABA-A receptor-mediated chloride current by positive allosteric modulation and, at higher concentrations, direct receptor activation. Neuronal hyperpolarization suppresses thalamocortical arousal and brainstem responses. Reduced sympathetic tone, venodilation, arterial vasodilation and some myocardial depression lower preload and pressure; impaired ventilatory drive and upper-airway tone explain apnea. In propofol infusion syndrome, impaired mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation and electron transport leave cardiac and skeletal muscle unable to meet energy demand.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Administer only by clinicians trained in general anesthesia or monitored sedation who are not simultaneously performing the procedure, with continuous oxygenation, ventilation and circulation monitoring and immediate airway-resuscitation equipment. Strict aseptic handling is essential because the lipid emulsion supports microbial growth. Propofol infusion syndrome can cause otherwise unexplained high-anion-gap/lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, lipemia, hepatomegaly, acute kidney injury, Brugada-like ECG changes, heart failure and cardiovascular collapse.

Description

Propofol is a rapid IV GABA-A positive-modulating anesthetic that increases inhibitory chloride current to suppress consciousness but provides essentially no analgesia. Its lipid emulsion enables injection, and a bolus wears off quickly largely through redistribution. Smooth awakening and antiemetic tendency are useful, but vasodilation, myocardial depression and ventilatory suppression can produce abrupt hypotension and apnea. Prolonged high exposure can cause propofol infusion syndrome, a mitochondrial energy-failure emergency.

Adverse Effects

  • Dose-dependent apnea or hypoventilation, upper-airway obstruction, hypotension, bradycardia or asystole, injection pain, hypertriglyceridemia, pancreatitis risk, involuntary movement, contamination-related infection, and propofol infusion syndrome.
