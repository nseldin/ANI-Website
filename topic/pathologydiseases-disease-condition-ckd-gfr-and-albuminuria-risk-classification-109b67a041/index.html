Definition

KDIGO classifies CKD by Cause, GFR category, and Albuminuria category, often abbreviated CGA. GFR and albuminuria form intersecting risk axes: lower GFR and higher albuminuria each increase the likelihood of progression, cardiovascular events, AKI, medication toxicity, and death.

Etiology

The classification describes severity and prognosis rather than cause. Two patients with the same eGFR can have different risk because their albuminuria, diagnosis, rate of change, age, comorbidities, and complications differ.

Pathology

GFR categories summarize remaining filtration, while albuminuria reflects glomerular barrier and vascular injury. The grid becomes progressively higher risk toward lower G categories and higher A categories, but clinical context still determines surveillance and treatment.

Pathophysiology

G1 is eGFR at least 90, G2 is 60-89, G3a is 45-59, G3b is 30-44, G4 is 15-29, and G5 is below 15 mL/min/1.73 m2. A1 is UACR below 30 mg/g, A2 is 30-300 mg/g, and A3 is above 300 mg/g. G1 and G2 require another marker of kidney damage to qualify as CKD. Albuminuria predicts risk because barrier leak accompanies endothelial and glomerular stress and can drive tubular inflammation even before filtration falls substantially.

Risk Factors

  • Diabetes, hypertension, glomerular disease, and smoking that increase albuminuria

  • Prior AKI and rapid eGFR decline

  • Cardiovascular disease, heart failure, obesity, and older age

  • Persistent high A category or low G category

Signs Symptoms

  • The categories themselves may be asymptomatic

  • Symptoms become more likely as filtration and reserve fall

  • Edema or foamy urine can accompany high albuminuria

  • Anemia, acidosis, potassium, mineral, and uremic findings become more common in advanced G categories

Diagnostics

  • Confirm eGFR chronicity and repeat abnormal UACR when transient exercise, fever, infection, marked hyperglycemia, menstruation, or other confounders are possible

  • Identify cause rather than stopping at a stage label

  • Use trend or validated kidney-failure risk tools when planning referral and replacement therapy

  • Interpret creatinine-based eGFR with muscle mass and consider cystatin C in selected uncertain decisions

Labs

  • G1 >=90, G2 60-89, G3a 45-59, G3b 30-44, G4 15-29, G5 <15 mL/min/1.73 m2

  • A1 <30, A2 30-300, A3 >300 mg/g UACR

  • A high A category can confer substantial risk despite preserved eGFR

  • Rapid change may represent AKI and should not be mislabeled as a stable CKD category

Treatments

  • Use the CGA risk profile to guide monitoring, kidney-protective therapy, referral, and complication screening

  • Treat cause, albuminuria, blood pressure, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk

  • Plan advanced-care options using predicted risk, symptoms, trajectory, and patient goals

  • Reclassify when sustained changes occur rather than reacting to ordinary analytic variation

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify chronicity, cause, eGFR category, urine albumin-creatinine category, blood pressure, diabetes status, and prior trends because CKD risk classification combines cause, G stage, and A stage rather than creatinine alone.

  • Trend eGFR and albuminuria at a frequency matched to risk while checking potassium, bicarbonate, hemoglobin, and medication tolerance because faster change or higher albuminuria predicts complications and kidney failure.

  • Use properly collected urine albumin-creatinine measurements and repeat unexpected abnormalities because exercise, fever, menstruation, infection, and acute illness can cause transient albuminuria.

  • Coordinate kidney-protective blood-pressure, diabetes, cardiovascular, and medication plans because albuminuria signals glomerular injury and systemic vascular risk even when eGFR remains preserved.

  • Escalate nephrology evaluation for abrupt eGFR decline, rapidly rising albuminuria, refractory hypertension, persistent potassium disturbance, or uremic symptoms because these findings exceed routine stable-CKD monitoring.

Complications

  • Underestimating cardiovascular and progression risk when albuminuria is ignored

  • Overdiagnosing CKD from isolated G1 or G2 eGFR

  • Delayed nephrology, transplant, or access planning

  • Medication dosing errors when changing filtration is missed

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose CKD from G1 or G2 eGFR alone

  • Do not use one UACR obtained during a transient confounder as permanent staging

  • Do not equate G5 with an automatic immediate dialysis order

Red Flags

  • Abrupt eGFR decline or creatinine rise inconsistent with prior trajectory

  • Rapidly increasing albuminuria, nephrotic-range protein loss, or active sediment

  • Refractory hypertension with headache, neurologic change, or pulmonary edema

  • Persistent hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pericarditic pain, or uremic confusion

Patient Education

  • Explain that GFR estimates filtration while albuminuria measures kidney-barrier injury, so two people with the same eGFR may have different risk.

  • Teach patients to keep scheduled blood and urine testing because CKD often progresses silently before swelling, nausea, itching, or fatigue becomes obvious.

Nclex Traps

  • Memorize 90, 60, 45, 30, and 15 for G categories

  • Albuminuria changes prognosis independently of eGFR

  • G5 means kidney-failure range, but dialysis initiation remains clinical

  • CKD needs at least 3 months of chronicity

Causal Links

  • Lower GFR category -> less clearance reserve -> more complication and drug-accumulation risk

  • Higher albuminuria category -> greater barrier injury -> higher kidney and cardiovascular risk

  • G and A intersection -> more accurate prognosis than either axis alone

  • Trend plus cause -> individualized monitoring and preparation intensity
