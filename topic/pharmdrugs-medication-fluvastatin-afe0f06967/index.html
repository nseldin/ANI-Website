Class

CYP2C9-metabolized HMG-CoA-reductase inhibitor; low- to moderate-intensity statin

Used To Treat

LDL-C reduction and coronary-risk modification in label-defined adults and selected pediatric HeFH patients. Immediate-release and extended-release regimens provide low- to moderate-intensity therapy.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Fluvastatin can cause myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, IMNM, and rare liver injury. Review CYP2C9 inhibitors, cyclosporine, fibrates, colchicine, renal disease, and formulation before dosing. Severe weakness, dark urine, reduced urine output, jaundice, or a new interacting medicine requires prompt assessment rather than waiting for routine follow-up.

Mechanism

Fluvastatin is administered as an active hydroxy acid and competitively inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, lowering hepatocyte cholesterol and increasing LDL-receptor transcription. CYP2C9 produces inactive hydroxylated metabolites, with smaller CYP2C8 and CYP3A contributions. Because immediate-release exposure falls sooner, evening or divided dosing overlaps the nocturnal synthesis period; the extended-release matrix sustains exposure across the day. The tablet must remain intact or the release profile is lost. Severe renal disease has limited study at high doses, and systemic illness or interacting exposure can still shift the muscle safety margin despite minimal CYP3A dependence.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record baseline lipid panel, exact product and dose, expected intensity, liver and thyroid context, kidney function when relevant, pregnancy plans, muscle history, alcohol, and all interacting medicines or supplements.

  • Teach urgent review for severe weakness, dark urine, oliguria, jaundice, or systemic illness. Mild symptoms still deserve assessment, but one episode does not prove permanent intolerance to every statin.

  • Immediate release is evening or twice daily; extended release is once daily at any consistent time and must not be crushed, chewed, or split.

  • Do not infer high intensity from the 80-mg tablet. Verify the actual percentage LDL response and escalate through a different pathway when needed.

Contraindications

  • Active liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis and serious hypersensitivity are major class barriers; use in stable chronic liver disease is individualized rather than automatically prohibited.

  • Most pregnant patients discontinue because therapy is preventive and fetal cholesterol biology matters. Current labeling permits rare individualized high-risk decisions; breastfeeding is generally not recommended during treatment.

Interactions

  • Fluconazole and other CYP2C9 inhibitors can raise fluvastatin. Cyclosporine and rifampin have important product-specific effects.

  • Gemfibrozil and other fibrates, lipid-dose niacin, colchicine, and other myotoxic drugs add muscle risk. Bile-acid sequestrants require spacing.

Key Labs

  • Repeat lipids after initiation or dose change to verify the expected percentage LDL reduction. Obtain baseline hepatic assessment; repeat liver tests for symptoms or clinical concern rather than routine asymptomatic monthly screening.

  • Use CK, creatinine, potassium, phosphate, urinalysis, and urine output for significant muscle symptoms or dark urine. Check TSH or other reversible contributors when the presentation suggests them.

Nclex Traps

  • Statins lower LDL mainly by increasing hepatic LDL-receptor clearance after intracellular cholesterol falls; they do not simply dissolve plaque or block intestinal fat.

  • Intensity is defined by expected percentage LDL reduction, not by comparing milligram numbers across drugs.

  • Fluvastatin is mainly CYP2C9, so an azole interaction can matter even when CYP3A is not the main pathway.

  • Lescol XL 80 mg is moderate intensity, not equivalent to atorvastatin 80 mg.

Description

Fluvastatin, branded Lescol or Lescol XL, is a low- to moderate-intensity HMG-CoA-reductase inhibitor that lowers hepatocyte cholesterol and upregulates LDL receptors, increasing plasma LDL clearance; unlike CYP3A4-dependent statins, it is metabolized mainly by CYP2C9. Immediate-release capsules are taken once in the evening or twice daily because their active exposure is shorter; the 80-mg extended-release tablet can be taken once daily at any time and must be swallowed whole. It does not provide high-intensity therapy. CYP2C9 inhibitors or reduced-function genetics can raise exposure, while cyclosporine and other transporter effects remain relevant. Fluvastatin's lower interaction burden with some CYP3A inhibitors can be useful, but renal disease, muscle symptoms, and a full medication review still matter. Choosing it should be intentional: if a patient needs at least 50-percent LDL reduction, use a high-intensity statin or complementary therapy rather than assuming 80 mg means high intensity.

Adverse Effects

  • Muscle symptoms can range from aches with normal CK to myopathy and rare rhabdomyolysis with hyperkalemia and myoglobin-mediated acute kidney injury. Risk rises with greater exposure, interacting drugs, CKD, hypothyroidism, frailty, and acute illness.

  • Mild aminotransferase and glucose or A1c increases can occur. Serious liver failure is rare, and the small diabetes signal usually does not outweigh prevention benefit when the indication is strong.
