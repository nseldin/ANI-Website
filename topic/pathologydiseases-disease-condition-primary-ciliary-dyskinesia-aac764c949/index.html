Definition

Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a usually inherited disorder of motile-cilia structure, function, or assembly. Ineffective mucociliary clearance causes chronic upper- and lower-airway infection and inflammation from infancy, while related cilia and flagella defects can alter organ laterality, hearing, and fertility.

Pathology

Airway epithelium retains mucus, bacteria, and inflammatory material because coordinated ciliary transport is ineffective. Repeated neutrophilic infection injures and remodels bronchi, eventually causing bronchiectasis; dysfunction of embryonic nodal cilia can randomize left-right organ development.

Pathophysiology

  • Motile cilia normally beat in a coordinated direction to move airway secretions toward the pharynx. Abnormal force, direction, coordination, or ciliary assembly leaves secretions stagnant, creating a cycle of infection, inflammation, wall damage, and further clearance failure.

  • Related flagellar and reproductive-tract cilia dysfunction contributes to male and female subfertility and ectopic-pregnancy risk. Laterality defects occur because embryonic nodal cilia help establish the left-right axis, but situs inversus is present in only a subset.

Etiology

Most PCD is autosomal recessive, with less common X-linked or autosomal-dominant forms involving genes needed for motile-cilia structure or function. A known affected sibling is a strong diagnostic clue. Kartagener syndrome describes PCD with situs inversus and chronic sinopulmonary disease; it is not synonymous with every PCD phenotype.

Risk Factors

  • Affected sibling or known familial pathogenic variant

  • Unexplained respiratory distress in a term neonate

  • Daily year-round wet cough and nasal congestion beginning in early childhood

  • Situs inversus, heterotaxy, congenital heart disease, or infertility together with chronic airway disease

Signs Symptoms

  • Unexplained respiratory distress, oxygen need, or lobar collapse in a term neonate

  • Daily year-round wet cough and nasal congestion beginning in infancy or early childhood

  • Recurrent otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia, chronic sputum, and later bronchiectasis or clubbing

  • Hearing difficulty and speech or learning effects from chronic middle-ear disease

  • Situs inversus or heterotaxy and male or female subfertility; these findings are supportive but not required

Diagnostics

  • Recognize a compatible lifelong phenotype and exclude cystic fibrosis before interpreting specialized PCD tests.

  • Use a specialist multimodal pathway that may combine standardized nasal nitric oxide in cooperative patients, multigene testing, transmission electron microscopy, immunofluorescence, and high-speed video analysis.

  • No single negative test excludes PCD. A meaningful minority of affected patients have normal electron microscopy or no currently identifiable pathogenic variants.

  • Use respiratory cultures, spirometry, imaging, audiology, ENT assessment, and laterality or congenital-organ evaluation to define complications, not to replace the etiologic diagnosis.

Labs

  • Nasal nitric oxide is usually very low in PCD but is not specific and must be measured with a validated protocol after excluding cystic fibrosis.

  • Airway cultures commonly identify Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and later Pseudomonas or other resistant organisms; nontuberculous mycobacteria can also occur.

  • Spirometry may show obstruction or declining function, while chest CT defines bronchiectasis and distribution.

  • Routine laboratory tests do not confirm PCD but help assess infection, inflammation, medication toxicity, and nutritional status.

Assessment

Establish onset in the newborn period, daily cough and congestion rather than intermittent symptoms, infection and culture history, hearing and speech effects, laterality, fertility history, growth, oxygenation, breath sounds, sputum, exercise tolerance, spirometry, and airway-clearance technique. A normal electron-microscopy report or absence of situs inversus does not close the evaluation.

Differential

Differentiate PCD from cystic fibrosis, immune deficiency, asthma, aspiration, chronic rhinosinusitis, recurrent viral infection, and other causes of non-CF bronchiectasis. Cough variability, allergy, or an asthma diagnosis can coexist but should not erase a lifelong daily wet-cough phenotype.

Treatments

  • Use individualized daily airway-clearance therapy and regular exercise to mobilize retained secretions; technique and adherence matter more than naming one universal device.

  • Treat pulmonary exacerbations promptly with culture-directed antibiotics when feasible to reduce bacterial burden and limit additional airway injury, and obtain surveillance cultures regularly through a specialty program.

  • Maintain current respiratory vaccination, nasal and sinus care, nutrition, hydration, and avoidance of tobacco smoke and airway irritants.

  • Coordinate ENT, audiology, speech, reproductive, congenital-heart, and pulmonary care. Selected patients with frequent exacerbations may receive long-term macrolide therapy after resistance and nontuberculous-mycobacteria review.

  • Advanced respiratory failure may require oxygen, noninvasive support, rehabilitation, or transplant evaluation.

Contraindications

  • Do not use cough suppressants routinely because cough is an essential clearance mechanism.

  • Inhaled corticosteroids are not routine PCD therapy unless a separate indication such as asthma or airway hyperreactivity exists.

  • Do not prescribe repeated empiric antibiotics without cultures and stewardship when a specimen can reasonably be obtained.

  • Do not exclude PCD because electron microscopy is normal, genetic testing is nondiagnostic, or situs inversus is absent.

  • Long-term macrolides require QT, interaction, resistance, hearing, and nontuberculous-mycobacteria risk assessment.

Safety Considerations

  • An acute increase in cough, sputum, breathlessness, fatigue, or exercise limitation can represent an exacerbation even before fever appears.

  • Airway-clearance devices can transmit organisms if cleaning and drying instructions are not followed.

  • Heterotaxy may include serious congenital heart or splenic abnormalities beyond organ position.

  • Female fertility impairment includes ectopic-pregnancy risk; early pregnancy symptoms require prompt evaluation.

  • Chronic ear disease affects communication and development and should not be dismissed as an isolated childhood nuisance.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess respiratory rate and effort, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, sputum amount and color, hemoptysis, hydration, temperature, exercise tolerance, and change from the patient's baseline.

  • Obtain sputum or another ordered respiratory culture before antibiotics when feasible without delaying urgent treatment.

  • Teach, observe, and correct airway-clearance and device-cleaning technique; schedule treatment around analgesia, bronchodilator use, meals, school, and rest as appropriate.

  • Track growth, weight, nutrition, spirometry, exacerbations, organisms, antibiotic courses, hearing, speech, and adherence across visits.

  • Reinforce vaccination, hand hygiene, smoke avoidance, early exacerbation reporting, and reproductive or laterality follow-up.

Red Flags

  • Respiratory distress, cyanosis, new or increasing oxygen requirement, or rapid decline from baseline

  • Significant hemoptysis, chest pain, or possible pneumothorax

  • High fever with increased work of breathing, lethargy, or sepsis signs

  • Dehydration, inability to clear secretions, or markedly reduced intake

  • Positive pregnancy test with unilateral pain, bleeding, dizziness, or syncope suggesting ectopic pregnancy

Complications

  • Bronchiectasis, chronic resistant infection, hemoptysis, and progressive respiratory insufficiency

  • Chronic sinus and middle-ear disease with hearing or speech impairment

  • Male and female subfertility and ectopic pregnancy

  • Congenital heart, splenic, or other organ abnormalities in heterotaxy

  • Treatment burden, reduced exercise tolerance, and eventual transplant need in severe disease

Prognosis

Course varies by genotype, infection burden, diagnosis timing, and access to consistent airway care. Early recognition, daily clearance, and prompt culture-guided infection treatment aim to preserve lung function, but some patients progress to advanced bronchiectasis or respiratory failure.

Prevention

The inherited defect cannot be prevented. Vaccination, regular cultures and follow-up, daily airway clearance, prompt treatment of exacerbations, smoke and irritant avoidance, device hygiene, and family testing reduce preventable lung and developmental harm.

Patient Education

  • PCD is not cystic fibrosis, although both impair airway clearance and can cause bronchiectasis.

  • Airway clearance is preventive treatment even on a day when breathing feels normal.

  • Learn the personal exacerbation pattern and report increased cough, sputum, breathlessness, fever, hemoptysis, or reduced exercise tolerance early.

  • Situs inversus is not required, and a normal single specialized test does not always exclude PCD.

Special Populations

  • Children need repeated audiology, speech, growth, school, and airway assessment.

  • People with laterality defects need appropriate cardiac, abdominal-organ, and splenic evaluation.

  • Pregnancy requires pulmonary optimization, infection control, medication review, and attention to ectopic-pregnancy risk.

  • Adults planning a family may need reproductive counseling and assisted-reproduction support.

Nclex Traps

  • Kartagener syndrome is a PCD subset; not every patient has situs inversus.

  • A normal electron-microscopy result does not exclude PCD.

  • Antibiotics treat infection, and airway clearance compensates for impaired transport; neither repairs the ciliary gene.

  • Routine cough suppression can worsen secretion retention.
