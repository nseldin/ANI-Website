Used To Treat

Suspected or confirmed methanol or ethylene-glycol poisoning and differentiation from isopropanol or other alcohol exposures. Fomepizole is the preferred specific alcohol-dehydrogenase inhibitor in many U.S. protocols; dialysis removes parent alcohol and toxic metabolites when EXTRIP criteria are met.

Description

Methanol and ethylene glycol are toxic alcohols whose metabolites block mitochondrial respiration or injure renal tubules, and fomepizole is an alcohol-dehydrogenase inhibitor that prevents those metabolites from forming, so treatment starts before confirmatory levels when the exposure pattern is dangerous. Methanol becomes formate, which blocks mitochondrial respiration and injures the retina and basal ganglia. Ethylene glycol becomes glycolate and oxalate, producing severe acidosis, kidney injury, hypocalcemia, and calcium-oxalate deposition. An early osmolar gap can fall as a late anion gap rises, so a normal gap at one moment never safely excludes exposure. Isopropanol instead produces acetone, ketosis without the same anion-gap acidosis, and usually does not benefit from alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade. Product identity, time, pH, end-organ findings, concentrations, and EXTRIP criteria must therefore drive a toxin-specific plan.

Mechanism

Fomepizole competitively inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing methanol and ethylene glycol from becoming the metabolites responsible for most organ injury. It buys time but does not remove metabolites already present. Bicarbonate corrects dangerous acidemia and reduces tissue penetration of some toxic acids; folate or folinic acid supports formate metabolism in methanol poisoning, while thiamine and pyridoxine are low-evidence adjuncts intended to favor less harmful ethylene-glycol metabolic routes. Intermittent hemodialysis efficiently removes small water-soluble parent alcohols and metabolites and corrects acidosis. Because dialysis also removes fomepizole, the antidote schedule must be adjusted during extracorporeal treatment.

Administration Timing

  • Call Poison Help and toxicology immediately, obtain blood gas, electrolytes, measured osmolality, ethanol and toxin concentrations when available, and start alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade when a credible exposure plus laboratory or clinical criteria support it; do not wait for a send-out concentration in a deteriorating patient.

  • Use the current fomepizole label and institutional toxic-alcohol protocol, including the required shorter dosing interval during hemodialysis. Protocol-directed ethanol is an alternative only when fomepizole is unavailable and requires intensive concentration, glucose, airway, and dosing control; beverage alcohol is not home treatment.

  • Contact nephrology and a dialysis-capable center early for severe acidosis, visual or neurologic findings, kidney injury, high toxin burden, or other current methanol- or ethylene-glycol-specific EXTRIP criteria. Continue blockade and toxin-specific cofactors until validated cessation endpoints are met.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact product or photograph the label because antifreeze, windshield fluid, solvents, hand products, and rubbing alcohol contain different alcohols and additives. Record co-ingested ethanol because it can delay metabolite formation and deceptively postpone acidosis.

  • Trend vision and neurologic status in methanol exposure; urine output, creatinine, calcium, and neuromuscular signs in ethylene-glycol exposure; and airway, glucose, and hemodynamics in every patient.

  • Coordinate antidote timing with dialysis start and stop. Document every fomepizole dose and extracorporeal interval because failure to adjust during dialysis can reopen metabolism.

Key Labs

  • Serial blood gas or pH, bicarbonate, anion gap, measured osmolality and calculated osmolar gap, glucose, lactate with awareness of assay interference, electrolytes, ionized calcium, creatinine, ethanol, methanol, ethylene glycol, urinalysis, and ECG.

  • Neither a normal osmolar gap nor absent urine crystals excludes poisoning. Late presentation may have little parent alcohol left while toxic metabolites and end-organ injury are greatest.

  • Formate and glycolate assays are not widely available; bedside decisions often must integrate history, acid-base trajectory, organ findings, and available parent concentrations.

Adverse Effects

  • Methanol can cause visual loss, coma, basal-ganglia injury, severe acidosis, shock, and death. Ethylene glycol can cause acidosis, hypocalcemia, dysrhythmia, pulmonary injury, and kidney failure.

  • Fomepizole can cause headache, nausea, dizziness, infusion-site reactions, and laboratory abnormalities; ethanol therapy adds intoxication, hypoglycemia, aspiration, and unstable concentrations. Dialysis adds access, hemodynamic, electrolyte, and anticoagulation hazards.

Contraindications

  • Do not give fomepizole reflexively for isolated isopropanol intoxication or ordinary ethanol exposure; blocking metabolism can prolong parent-alcohol intoxication without preventing the same toxic-acid syndrome.

  • Do not delay blockade or dialysis because a definitive concentration is unavailable when clinical and laboratory evidence indicates life-threatening methanol or ethylene-glycol poisoning.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Visual symptoms, seizure, coma, worsening acidosis, hypotension, dysrhythmia, hypocalcemia, oliguria, or kidney injury requires immediate ICU, toxicology, nephrology, and EXTRIP-guided escalation. Rebound after dialysis requires repeat gaps, pH, and concentrations because redistribution or incomplete removal can occur.

Interactions

  • Co-ingested ethanol competes for alcohol dehydrogenase and may delay toxicity, but its declining concentration can unmask rapid metabolite formation.

  • Dialysis removes fomepizole and mandates schedule adjustment. Drugs or diseases that cause lactic ketoacidosis, kidney failure, or altered osmolality can mimic or compound the laboratory pattern.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Small exploratory ingestions require product-specific poison-center assessment; severe hypoglycemia or rapid deterioration can occur, and all doses and dialysis volumes are weight dependent.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal acidemia and toxic metabolites threaten both patients; do not delay effective blockade and dialysis when indicated, with obstetric consultation in parallel.

  • Population

    Kidney or liver disease

    Note

    Baseline gaps and clearance can be abnormal, and kidney injury is itself an EXTRIP consideration in ethylene-glycol poisoning; interpret trends with specialists.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP recommendations differ between methanol and ethylene glycol and vary with antidote, concentration, acidosis, organ findings, and kidney function. Adjunctive folate, thiamine, and pyridoxine evidence is weaker than the evidence and mechanistic basis for prompt alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade.

Nclex Traps

  • The osmolar gap tends to be earlier and the anion gap later; either can be normal depending on timing.

  • Fomepizole prevents toxic metabolite formation but does not remove metabolites already formed.

  • Isopropanol typically causes ketosis without the same high-anion-gap acidosis and is not automatically treated like methanol or ethylene glycol.

Regulatory Status

Fomepizole is FDA-approved as an antidote for ethylene-glycol or suspected ethylene-glycol poisoning and, under its labeling, methanol poisoning; dialysis and cofactor use follow toxin-specific expert guidance. Ethanol protocols are institution-specific and not permission for unsupervised beverage use.

Boxed Warning

TOXIC-ALCOHOL WARNING: visual symptoms, severe acidosis, coma, seizure, or suspected antifreeze or methanol ingestion requires 911, Poison Help, immediate alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade assessment, and early dialysis planning. A normal osmolar gap does not rule out late poisoning, and home ethanol treatment can delay definitive care and cause aspiration or hypoglycemia.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
