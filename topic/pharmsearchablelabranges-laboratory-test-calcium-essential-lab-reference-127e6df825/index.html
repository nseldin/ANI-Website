Name

Calcium

Range

Total and ionized calcium are different measurements. Mayo Clinic Laboratories lists adult total-serum examples of 8.6-10.0 mg/dL at ages 18-59 and 8.8-10.2 mg/dL at age 60 or older, and an adult ionized-calcium example of 4.57-5.43 mg/dL at ages 24-97. These are one laboratory's method- and age-specific intervals, not universal ranges; use the result's own interval, specimen, age, albumin or protein context, and clinical setting. Direct ionized measurement may be needed when the active calcium fraction is uncertain because calculated correction from total calcium, albumin, and pH can disagree with direct measurement.

Why

An abnormal calcium result signals altered neuromuscular and cardiac excitability and raises concern for hypocalcemia or hypercalcemia. Low total or ionized calcium can reflect hypoparathyroidism, vitamin-D or magnesium deficiency, pancreatitis, or kidney disease; low albumin can lower total calcium without proving that active ionized calcium is low. High calcium commonly raises concern for hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, or excess vitamin D. Promptly assess tingling, cramps, spasms or tetany, weakness, constipation, thirst and urine output, kidney-stone symptoms, confusion, seizure activity, airway or laryngospasm signs, and rhythm or ECG changes. Cause-directed evaluation can include repeat total or direct ionized calcium, albumin or other protein context, magnesium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, kidney function, medication and supplement review, and malignancy evaluation as ordered. Escalate immediately for seizure, laryngospasm or respiratory compromise, acute neurologic change, hemodynamic instability, or a serious dysrhythmia. One calcium result does not diagnose the cause, quantify calcium in bone, or establish severity from one universal cutoff; interpret symptoms, calcium fraction, trend, laboratory critical limits, and the full clinical context.

High Causes

  • Primary hyperparathyroidism

  • Malignancy, including selected cancers involving bone

  • Excess vitamin D or calcium exposure

  • Medication effect such as a thiazide or lithium

  • Immobilization or another cause-directed bone-mineral disorder

Low Causes

  • Hypoparathyroidism, including after neck surgery

  • Vitamin-D or magnesium deficiency

  • Kidney disease

  • Pancreatitis

  • Low albumin lowering total calcium without necessarily lowering ionized calcium

  • Citrate exposure during large-volume transfusion or another critical-care context

Critical Concerns

  • Seizure, tetany, laryngospasm or respiratory compromise, acute neurologic change, serious dysrhythmia, or hemodynamic instability requires immediate escalation.

  • Use the reporting laboratory's critical limits and the patient's symptoms and ECG rather than one universal calcium emergency threshold.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess neuromuscular, airway, neurologic, hydration, kidney-stone, and cardiac findings and review neck surgery, kidney or parathyroid disease, medicines, supplements, and transfusion context.

  • Trend total or ionized calcium with albumin or protein context, magnesium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, kidney function, and ECG or other cause-directed testing as ordered.

Nclex Traps

  • A low total calcium in hypoalbuminemia does not automatically prove low active ionized calcium; direct measurement may be needed in a seriously ill or discordant presentation.

  • Blood calcium does not measure bone calcium, and one abnormal result does not diagnose a parathyroid, kidney, vitamin-D, pancreatic, or malignant cause.
