Definition

Urine sediment and fractional-excretion indices are contextual clues used to investigate AKI, not verdicts. Sediment can reveal the injured compartment, while FeNa estimates the fraction of filtered sodium excreted and FeUrea estimates the fraction of filtered urea excreted; both are altered by timing, treatment, CKD, and mixed disease.

Etiology

Sodium-avid prerenal physiology often lowers FeNa, whereas established tubular injury often raises it because damaged tubules cannot reclaim sodium. Diuretics increase sodium excretion, motivating use of FeUrea, but sepsis, CKD, pigment injury, glomerular disease, obstruction, osmotic diuresis, and early or recovering injury can violate the expected patterns.

Pathology

Urine microscopy samples material shed into the urinary space. Dysmorphic red cells and red-cell casts point toward glomerular capillary injury; white-cell casts suggest interstitial inflammation or pyelonephritis; renal tubular epithelial cells and muddy brown granular casts support tubular injury; crystals can identify selected drugs, stones, or toxins.

Pathophysiology

FeNa is calculated from urine and plasma sodium and creatinine, correcting urine sodium for the filtered sodium load. A value below roughly 1% can support sodium avidity and a value above roughly 2% can support tubular dysfunction in selected oliguric patients without CKD or diuretics, but these are teaching anchors rather than universal boundaries. FeUrea may be less directly altered by loop or thiazide diuretics, yet its diagnostic performance is also inconsistent. The 2022 meta-analysis found FeNa most useful in carefully selected oliguric patients without CKD or diuretic exposure and substantially less reliable outside that setting.

Risk Factors

  • Diuretic exposure, CKD, sepsis, or nonoliguric AKI that reduces index accuracy

  • Pigment nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, early obstruction, or contrast-associated patterns that may retain sodium

  • Osmotic diuresis or bicarbonaturia that raises urine sodium despite low perfusion

  • Delayed specimen collection after fluids or hemodynamic correction

Signs Symptoms

  • The indices themselves cause no symptoms

  • Oliguria makes classic FeNa evidence more applicable than nonoliguric states

  • Urine color, edema, rash, fever, flank pain, muscle injury, or systemic findings provide essential context

  • A bland sediment does not exclude serious hemodynamic or vascular disease

Diagnostics

  • Collect paired urine and blood samples close in time when calculating fractional excretion

  • Perform fresh urine microscopy when possible

  • Interpret indices with volume status, diuretics, CKD, sepsis, obstruction, and timing

  • Use ultrasound, serology, CK, hemolysis studies, cultures, or biopsy when the phenotype requires more than urine indices

Labs

  • FeNa = 100 x (urine sodium x plasma creatinine) / (plasma sodium x urine creatinine)

  • FeUrea uses urine and plasma urea with urine and plasma creatinine in the analogous relationship

  • BUN/creatinine ratio is affected by protein intake, catabolism, steroids, liver function, GI bleeding, and fluid status

  • Casts can be intermittent and observer-dependent; absence is not exclusion

Treatments

  • Treat the cause shown by the whole AKI evaluation rather than treating an index

  • Repeat assessment when the clinical trajectory and initial urine data conflict

  • Correct perfusion, stop toxins, relieve obstruction, or treat immune/infectious disease as indicated

  • Obtain nephrology or microscopy expertise when active sediment or unexplained rapid progression is present

Nursing Priorities

  • Obtain a fresh clean urine specimen and review cells, casts, crystals, protein, and blood because sediment may reveal tubular, glomerular, interstitial, obstructive, or pigment-related injury.

  • Interpret FeNa and FeUrea with urine output, timing, CKD, sepsis, contrast, obstruction, diuretics, and fluid therapy because threshold shortcuts are neither perfectly sensitive nor specific for prerenal physiology.

  • Trend creatinine, urine output, electrolytes, sediment evolution, hemodynamics, and response to cause-directed treatment because AKI phenotype can change and one index cannot replace the clinical course.

  • Escalate nephrology review for dysmorphic red cells, red-cell casts, heavy proteinuria, unusual crystals, or unexplained persistent AKI because biopsy, serology, antidote, or urgent disease-specific therapy may be needed.

  • Activate urgent treatment for hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, anuria, or uremic complications regardless of FeNa or FeUrea because complication severity determines immediate safety actions.

Complications

  • Misclassifying ATN as dehydration and causing fluid overload

  • Missing glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, obstruction, or pigment injury

  • Delaying disease-specific therapy

  • Unnecessary discontinuation of beneficial medicines based on a weak index

Contraindications

  • Do not use FeNa alone to diagnose prerenal AKI or ATN

  • Do not assume FeUrea is definitive during diuretic therapy

  • Do not use a BUN/creatinine ratio as a substitute for history, examination, sediment, and imaging

Red Flags

  • Red-cell casts, dysmorphic hematuria, or rapidly increasing proteinuria

  • Crystals with toxin exposure, severe flank pain, or abrupt oliguria

  • Hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, or anuria

  • Persistent worsening AKI despite correction of apparent perfusion causes

Patient Education

  • Explain that urine microscopy can show what part of the nephron is injured, while FeNa and FeUrea are context-dependent clues rather than definitive diagnoses.

  • Teach patients that diuretics, chronic kidney disease, sepsis, and recent fluids can alter urine indices, so treatment should not follow one percentage alone.

Nclex Traps

  • FeNa below 1% is not synonymous with dehydration

  • Diuretics weaken FeNa interpretation but do not make FeUrea infallible

  • Muddy brown casts support tubular injury

  • Red-cell casts point toward glomerular inflammation

Causal Links

  • Intact sodium-avid tubules -> low fractional sodium excretion -> possible prerenal pattern

  • Tubular epithelial injury -> impaired sodium reclamation and cellular shedding -> higher FeNa plus granular casts

  • Diuretic blockade -> increased urine sodium independent of perfusion -> FeNa misclassification

  • Glomerular capillary injury -> dysmorphic red cells and red-cell casts -> nephritic localization
