Definition

Pulmonary embolism after surgery develops when a postoperative venous thrombus embolizes to pulmonary arteries, abruptly increasing dead space and right-ventricular afterload. The characteristic pattern is sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, syncope, unexplained hypoxemia, or hypotension after surgery, sometimes without leg symptoms. Confirm or stage it with clinical probability assessment with CT pulmonary angiography when appropriate; distinguish it from atelectasis, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, fat embolism, and hemorrhage by imaging, electrocardiography, biomarkers, and the full operative context. The priority is to support oxygenation and circulation immediately because obstructive shock can complicate Pulmonary embolism after surgery. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • clinical probability assessment with CT pulmonary angiography when appropriate

  • ventilation-perfusion imaging when contrast or radiation considerations favor it

  • bedside echocardiography and leg ultrasound when an unstable patient cannot travel

  • Distinguish Pulmonary embolism after surgery from atelectasis, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, fat embolism, and hemorrhage by imaging, electrocardiography, biomarkers, and the full operative context.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • support oxygenation and circulation immediately

  • start anticoagulation after weighing surgical and neuraxial bleeding

  • use reperfusion or embolectomy for selected high-risk cases through a multidisciplinary response

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for syncope, refractory hypoxemia, hypotension, rising lactate, chest pain with shock, or cardiac arrest because pulmonary embolism response-team evaluation for thrombolysis, thrombectomy, surgery, or circulatory support is time critical.

  • Treat sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, syncope, hypoxemia, or unexplained postoperative hypotension as possible pulmonary embolism because surgery and immobility create a high-risk venous-thromboembolism setting.

  • Apply oxygen, elevate the head, establish vascular access, obtain a 12-lead electrocardiogram, and prepare definitive imaging without unnecessary ambulation because exertion can worsen oxygen demand and unstable right-heart strain.

  • Assess blood pressure, mental status, jugular venous pressure, chest pain, oxygen need, urine output, lactate, and signs of deep-vein thrombosis because shock and right-ventricular failure determine the urgency of advanced therapy.

  • Administer prescribed anticoagulation after bleeding-risk review and monitor wounds, drains, hemoglobin, platelets, and anticoagulant effect because treatment prevents clot propagation but can worsen recent surgical bleeding.

Complications

  • obstructive shock can complicate Pulmonary embolism after surgery.

  • cardiac arrest can complicate Pulmonary embolism after surgery.

  • right-ventricular failure can complicate Pulmonary embolism after surgery.

  • recurrent embolism or chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension

  • major operative-site can complicate Pulmonary embolism after surgery.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, hypotension, or signs of obstructive shock

  • Rapidly increasing oxygen requirement or severe respiratory distress

  • New right-heart strain with rising lactate or oliguria

  • Cardiac arrest or recurrent collapse after recent surgery

Patient Education

  • Explain that walking and prescribed compression or anticoagulant prevention lower clot risk, but new breathlessness, chest pain, coughing blood, or fainting requires emergency help.

  • Review anticoagulant timing, bleeding precautions, incision observation, and the need to avoid stopping therapy without the treating team.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal chest radiograph does not exclude pulmonary embolism

  • Postoperative D-dimer elevation is nonspecific and should not drive imaging by itself
