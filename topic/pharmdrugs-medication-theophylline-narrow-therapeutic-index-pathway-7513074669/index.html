Used To Treat

Selected chronic reversible-airway obstruction when safer inhaled strategies are inadequate or unsuitable. It is not preferred first-line asthma or COPD therapy and is not a rapid substitute for inhaled rescue treatment.

Description

Theophylline can relax airway smooth muscle and reduce airway responsiveness, but the dose that helps is uncomfortably close to the dose that causes vomiting, atrial or ventricular arrhythmias, and seizures. Clearance changes dramatically with age, smoking, fever, heart failure, liver disease, and interacting drugs. A previously stable dose can therefore become toxic without any dosing error. The clinically important skill is not memorizing one level; it is recognizing what changed the patient's metabolism and obtaining a correctly timed concentration when symptoms or circumstances change.

Mechanism

Theophylline inhibits phosphodiesterase isozymes, especially PDE3 and to a lesser extent PDE4 at relevant airway effects, which reduces cAMP breakdown and supports bronchodilation. Adenosine-receptor antagonism contributes to CNS, cardiac, gastric, and other effects, while additional anti-inflammatory mechanisms are proposed but not fully established. Hepatic CYP1A2, CYP3A4, and CYP2E1 metabolism creates many interactions. Tobacco or marijuana smoke induces clearance; stopping smoke exposure can sharply raise concentration. Sustained fever, liver dysfunction, heart failure, sepsis, and some inhibitors reduce clearance, allowing accumulation. Concentration-dependent receptor and PDE effects explain why early nausea or tremor can precede arrhythmia and seizure.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index and nonlinear elimination can make a small dose increase produce a disproportionate concentration rise. New vomiting, persistent nausea, insomnia, tremor, agitation, tachycardia, irregular rhythm, hypotension, or seizure is toxicity until assessed. Hold further doses, obtain a timed level and metabolic panel, place severe cases on cardiac monitoring, and involve poison control/toxicology for significant exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, reflux, diarrhea, headache, insomnia, tremor, agitation, diuresis, and tachycardia

  • Supraventricular or ventricular arrhythmia, hypotension, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and seizures in severe toxicity

Contraindications

  • Serious hypersensitivity and uncontrolled seizure or tachyarrhythmia risk require specialist assessment.

  • Do not increase a dose for symptoms until adherence, timing, concentration, smoking, fever, disease, formulation, and interactions are reviewed.

Nursing Essentials

  • Document formulation, dose time, sample time, food instructions, smoking status, fever, new drugs, liver/heart status, and symptoms with every level.

  • Extended-release products are not automatically interchangeable; do not crush unless the exact product permits it.

Interactions

  • Ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine, cimetidine, several macrolides, and other inhibitors can raise levels; rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and smoke exposure can lower them.

  • Stopping smoking or resolving an inducer can raise levels even though the medication list appears unchanged; caffeine and sympathomimetics add stimulation.

Key Labs

  • Timed serum theophylline concentration interpreted with the product and clinical target, ECG, potassium, magnesium, glucose, acid-base status, renal/liver function

  • Fever duration, smoking change, heart failure, seizure activity, vomiting, and rhythm monitoring

Nclex Traps

  • A therapeutic range is not a guarantee of safety; toxicity can occur at lower levels in susceptible patients.

  • Nicotine is not the main inducer: combustion smoke induces metabolism, so switching from cigarettes to nicotine replacement can reduce clearance.
