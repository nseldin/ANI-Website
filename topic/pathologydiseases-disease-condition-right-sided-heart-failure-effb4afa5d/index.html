Definition

Right-sided heart failure develops when right-ventricular pump failure raises systemic venous pressure and reduces left-sided preload, often from left-heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, infarction, or lung disease. The characteristic pattern is jugular venous distention, hepatomegaly, ascites, edema, weight gain, fatigue, and low output with relatively clear or congested lungs depending on cause. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for right-ventricular size, function, valves, and pulmonary pressure; distinguish it from cirrhosis, nephrotic edema, venous obstruction, constrictive pericarditis, and isolated left failure by jugular waveform, echo, organ studies, and hemodynamics. The priority is to treat the left-heart, pulmonary, ischemic, valvular, or congenital cause because cardiogenic shock can complicate Right-sided heart failure. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for right-ventricular size, function, valves, and pulmonary pressure

  • electrocardiogram, biomarkers, liver and kidney tests, imaging, and volume trends

  • right-heart catheterization when hemodynamics or pulmonary-hypertension classification will change care

  • Distinguish Right-sided heart failure from cirrhosis, nephrotic edema, venous obstruction, constrictive pericarditis, and isolated left failure by jugular waveform, echo, organ studies, and hemodynamics.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • treat the left-heart, pulmonary, ischemic, valvular, or congenital cause

  • use careful diuresis to reduce venous congestion without collapsing preload

  • support oxygenation and rhythm and refer advanced disease early

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for hypotension, syncope, chest pain, rising oxygen need, new tachyarrhythmia, cool extremities, oliguria, rising lactate, or rapidly worsening edema and abdominal pain because acute right-ventricular failure can progress to multiorgan shock.

  • Assess jugular venous pressure, edema, ascites, hepatomegaly, weight, dyspnea, chest pain, perfusion, and the likely pulmonary, left-heart, infarct, valve, or congenital cause because right-ventricular treatment depends on afterload and loading conditions.

  • Trend blood pressure, rhythm, oxygenation, daily weight, strict intake and output, urine output, creatinine, sodium, potassium, liver tests, and mental status because venous congestion impairs kidneys and liver while low forward flow threatens perfusion.

  • Administer prescribed diuretics and cause-directed therapy with frequent volume reassessment because relieving congestion improves organ function but abrupt or excessive preload loss can collapse right-ventricular output.

  • Avoid unplanned fluid boluses and review ventilator pressure or pulmonary vasodilator changes with the team because excess volume and increased pulmonary vascular resistance can both worsen ventricular dilation and septal interaction.

Complications

  • cardiogenic shock can complicate Right-sided heart failure.

  • severe hepatic and kidney congestion

  • ascites can complicate Right-sided heart failure.

  • malabsorption can complicate Right-sided heart failure.

  • atrial arrhythmia and thromboembolism

  • hypotension and kidney injury from excessive preload reduction

Red Flags

  • Hypotension, syncope, cool extremities, or altered consciousness

  • New chest pain, severe hypoxemia, or rapid atrial arrhythmia

  • Oliguria, rising lactate, or rapidly worsening kidney or liver function

  • Abruptly increasing jugular venous pressure, edema, ascites, or abdominal pain

Patient Education

  • Teach daily weight, edema, abdominal swelling, breathing, and medication tracking because systemic congestion may worsen before lung crackles appear.

  • Explain why salt, fluid, diuretic, oxygen, and activity plans are individualized: both too much volume and too little filling can impair a vulnerable right ventricle.

Nclex Traps

  • Right-ventricular infarction may worsen with nitrates or aggressive diuresis

  • Peripheral edema alone does not establish right-heart failure
