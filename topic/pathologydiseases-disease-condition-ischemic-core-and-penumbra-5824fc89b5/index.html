Risk Factors

  • Proximal LVO and poor collaterals accelerate core growth because a large territory loses both direct and backup flow.

  • Hypotension, hypoxemia, fever, severe glucose disturbance, or anemia can worsen penumbral survival because delivery falls or metabolic demand rises.

  • Fast spontaneous or treatment-mediated reperfusion can preserve tissue, while delay allows irreversible injury to spread.

Signs Symptoms

  • The neurologic deficit reflects dysfunctional core plus penumbra, so severity alone does not reveal how much tissue is already irreversible.

  • Fluctuation with pressure or position can occur when penumbral flow depends on fragile collaterals.

  • Sudden improvement may reflect recanalization, but persistent occlusion or re-occlusion remains possible and still requires evaluation.

Diagnostics

  • NCCT identifies hemorrhage and major established changes but does not directly map all viable penumbra.

  • CTA identifies the occluded artery and collateral routes, while CTP estimates core-flow mismatch and MRI DWI/ADC identifies restricted diffusion with optional perfusion or FLAIR comparison.

  • Interpret imaging quality, onset/LKW, disabling deficit, baseline function, and treatment pathway together because no single color map decides eligibility by itself.

Labs

  • Glucose is immediate because hypoglycemia mimics stroke and true metabolic failure also harms threatened neurons.

  • Oxygenation, hemoglobin, temperature, and hemodynamic data contextualize delivery to the penumbra.

  • Renal and contrast-risk assessment matters, but a laboratory delay can itself cost tissue, so teams follow emergency imaging protocols rather than a universal pause.

Treatments

  • Reopen eligible occluded vessels rapidly with IV thrombolysis and/or thrombectomy because benefit comes from rescuing viable tissue before core expands.

  • Support oxygenation, temperature, glucose, and perfusion because secondary physiologic insults can convert penumbra into infarct.

  • Do not pursue futile delay for perfect imaging when standard-window eligibility is already clear because the 2026 guideline emphasizes rapid IV thrombolysis without advanced imaging for eligible disabling deficits within 4.5 hours.

Nursing Priorities

  • Explain that the ischemic core is already severely injured while the penumbra is hypoperfused but potentially salvageable because reperfusion aims to preserve threatened tissue rather than revive irreversibly infarcted cells.

  • Establish last-known-well, baseline function, glucose, deficit severity, and treatment contraindications while expediting noncontrast, vascular, and selected perfusion imaging because time and tissue both inform reperfusion decisions.

  • Prevent hypoxia, fever, severe hypo- or hyperglycemia, aspiration, and avoidable hypotension while following the individualized blood-pressure plan because systemic insults can collapse collateral flow and recruit penumbra into the core.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, blood pressure, oxygenation, glucose, temperature, rhythm, and new headache or vomiting because neurologic worsening may reflect core expansion, edema, reocclusion, or hemorrhage.

  • Escalate for NIH Stroke Scale increase, recurrent deficit, treatment-delay milestone breach, falling pressure with neurologic worsening, reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, severe headache, vomiting, or seizure because salvageable tissue and post-reperfusion safety are both time sensitive.

Complications

  • Core expansion produces greater disability, edema, and herniation risk.

  • Reperfusion into severely injured microvasculature can cause hemorrhagic transformation because the blood-brain barrier is disrupted.

  • No-reflow can persist despite opening the large artery because microvascular obstruction and tissue injury limit effective perfusion.

Contraindications

  • Do not describe penumbra as mildly injured but safe tissue because it is unstable and time-limited.

  • Do not promise that all DWI-positive tissue is permanently dead or that all perfusion mismatch will recover because imaging is probabilistic.

  • Do not delay a clearly eligible standard-window thrombolysis case solely to obtain advanced mismatch imaging.

Red Flags

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, recurrent focal deficit, or rapid loss of previously preserved function

  • Falling blood pressure with worsening neurologic findings or severe refractory hypoxemia

  • Reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, posturing, severe headache, or repeated vomiting

  • Seizure, aspiration, airway compromise, or a critical delay in reperfusion transfer

Patient Education

  • Every minute matters because threatened penumbral brain may become irreversibly injured while the artery remains blocked.

  • Do not delay emergency care to watch symptoms at home because temporary improvement can reflect unstable collateral blood flow.

Nclex Traps

  • Penumbra is dysfunctional but potentially viable; core is already severely injured.

  • Clock time matters, but collateral-dependent tissue time differs among patients.

  • Reperfusion rescues threatened tissue; it does not regenerate established infarct.

Ischemic core and penumbra

Category

Acute stroke pathophysiology

Definition

The ischemic core is brain tissue with such severe and sustained blood-flow failure that irreversible cellular injury is already likely. The penumbra is surrounding hypoperfused tissue that has lost normal function but remains viable for a limited time because collateral blood supplies enough energy to delay cell death. Reperfusion aims to rescue penumbra, not revive established core. The boundary changes over time and differs among patients, which is why clock time and tissue imaging complement rather than replace one another.

Etiology

Core and penumbra form after an arterial occlusion or critical low-flow state. Occlusion size and location, collateral circulation, systemic pressure, oxygen content, glucose, temperature, prior vascular adaptation, and time determine how quickly threatened tissue crosses into infarction.

Pathology

Core tissue experiences profound ATP failure, membrane depolarization, calcium influx, excitotoxicity, cytotoxic edema, and structural injury. In the penumbra, electrical function fails before membrane integrity is lost, so the patient has a deficit even though some neurons remain recoverable. Without restored flow, excitotoxic, inflammatory, microvascular, and edema processes recruit penumbra into the core.

Pathophysiology

Collateral vessels create a gradient rather than a sharp ring around the core. Good collaterals slow core growth, producing a 'slow progressor' who may retain treatable tissue later; poor collaterals produce rapid core expansion. DWI and low ADC approximate cytotoxic injury, while CTP or MR perfusion estimates delayed or reduced flow. These are imperfect models affected by motion, timing, thresholds, reperfusion, and technical quality, so imaging must be interpreted with the clinical syndrome and vessel study. The 2026 guideline supports advanced-imaging selection for some unknown-onset or 4.5-to-9-hour thrombolysis cases and selected extended-window EVT because physiology, not the clock alone, can identify remaining benefit.
