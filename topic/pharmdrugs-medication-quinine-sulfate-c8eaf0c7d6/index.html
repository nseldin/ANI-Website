Used To Treat

Uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria in combination with doxycycline, tetracycline, or clindamycin according to patient factors and current guidance. It is not approved for nocturnal leg cramps, malaria prevention, or stand-alone radical cure.

Description

Quinine sulfate is a cinchona-alkaloid antimalarial that concentrates in the acidic Plasmodium food vacuole and disrupts conversion of toxic free heme into inert hemozoin, killing blood-stage parasites. Its narrow safety margin can stimulate pancreatic insulin release and cause hypoglycemia while producing cinchonism with tinnitus, hearing or vision change, headache, nausea, and dizziness. It can also prolong QT conduction or trigger severe immune thrombocytopenia and hemolysis. Quinine must be paired with a slower partner such as doxycycline, tetracycline, or clindamycin because combination therapy improves cure and limits recrudescence.

Mechanism

Blood-stage intraerythrocytic Plasmodium digests hemoglobin to obtain amino acids, releasing ferric heme that catalyzes oxidative damage. The parasite normally crystallizes heme into inert hemozoin. Protonated quinine accumulates in the acidic food vacuole, binds heme, and interferes with hemozoin formation, increasing toxic soluble heme and oxidative membrane injury. Quinine also affects parasite nucleic-acid and membrane processes but heme detoxification is the central teaching model. In humans, blockade of cardiac sodium and potassium currents prolongs QRS/QT at higher exposure, while stimulation of pancreatic beta-cell insulin release can cause hypoglycemia, especially in severe malaria or pregnancy. Drug-dependent antibodies explain abrupt thrombocytopenia or hemolytic-uremic syndromes.

Boxed Warning

Boxed warning: quinine used for nocturnal leg cramps can cause life-threatening thrombocytopenia and hemolytic-uremic syndrome/thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, severe hypersensitivity, QT dysrhythmia, and death; the risk outweighs any unproven benefit. Use only for an approved or guideline-supported indication. Stop urgently for bleeding, petechiae, dark urine, jaundice, renal decline, severe rash, syncope, palpitations, or recurrent hypoglycemia. Re-exposure after immune thrombocytopenia can trigger a faster and more severe reaction.

Adverse Effects

  • Cinchonism: tinnitus, hearing impairment, headache, nausea, dizziness, diaphoresis, blurred vision, and GI upset

  • Hypoglycemia, QT prolongation/torsades, thrombocytopenia, HUS/TTP, hemolysis, severe hypersensitivity, liver injury, and hypotension

Contraindications

  • Prolonged QT, prior quinine-associated thrombocytopenia/TTP/HUS, G6PD-related severe reactions, hypersensitivity to quinine or mefloquine/quinidine, optic neuritis, and myasthenia gravis under label restrictions.

  • Do not use for leg cramps or confuse quinine with quinidine, a related class IA antiarrhythmic.

Nursing Essentials

  • Give the complete combination regimen and monitor glucose, ECG, hearing/vision symptoms, platelets, renal function, and parasite response. Tinnitus is a concentration clue, not trivia.

  • Review every medication for CYP3A4, CYP2D6, P-glycoprotein, QT, digoxin, and anticoagulation interactions before the first dose.

Interactions

  • Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors can raise quinine and QT toxicity; inducers can lower efficacy. Quinine inhibits CYP2D6 and P-glycoprotein and can raise digoxin or other substrate exposure.

  • Other QT-prolonging antimalarials, macrolides, antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, and electrolyte disturbances magnify dysrhythmia risk.

Key Labs

  • Parasite smears/density, glucose, CBC/platelets, creatinine, liver tests, potassium/magnesium, ECG and hemolysis studies when indicated

  • Cinchonism symptoms, bleeding, dark urine, syncope, adherence to partner drug, pregnancy status, and species-directed radical-cure need

Nclex Traps

  • Quinine treats blood-stage malaria and needs a partner; it is not a benign remedy for leg cramps.

  • Quinine can cause hypoglycemia by increasing insulin release, so diaphoresis or confusion may be metabolic rather than only malaria progression.
