Definition

Orthopnea is a positional respiratory symptom: breathlessness begins or worsens while lying flat and improves when the upper body is raised or the person sits or stands.

Plain Language

Orthopnea means it is harder to breathe while lying flat and easier to breathe upright.

Why It Matters

Orthopnea is a classic clue to pulmonary congestion from heart failure, but obesity, diaphragm or neuromuscular weakness, ascites, and lung disease can also cause it. New severe orthopnea with hypoxemia, frothy sputum, chest pain, confusion, or rapidly increasing effort requires urgent assessment.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

Ask whether breathlessness begins or worsens when reclining, how quickly it occurs, how many pillows or what sleeping position is needed, and whether sitting or standing relieves it; assess onset, chest pain, cough, edema, weight trend, lung sounds, oxygenation, respiratory effort, cardiac findings, and associated nighttime episodes without forcing the patient flat.

Positive Finding

The patient reports reproducible breathlessness when recumbent that improves after the upper body is raised or the patient sits or stands.

Mechanism

Recumbency shifts blood centrally and can increase pulmonary vascular congestion while reducing lung compliance in left-heart failure; position can also worsen diaphragmatic mechanics or airway and lung loading in noncardiac disorders.

Clinical Interpretation

The provoked Orthopnea symptom raises concern for breathlessness caused or worsened by lying flat, classically from pulmonary congestion in heart failure but also possible with obesity, diaphragmatic or neuromuscular weakness, ascites, pleural disease, or obstructive lung disease because recumbency shifts blood centrally and can increase pulmonary vascular congestion while reducing lung compliance in left-heart failure; position can also worsen diaphragmatic mechanics or airway and lung loading in noncardiac disorders. Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Orthopnea to focus evaluation of breathlessness caused or worsened by lying flat, classically from pulmonary congestion in heart failure but also possible with obesity, diaphragmatic or neuromuscular weakness, ascites, pleural disease, or obstructive lung disease and the reviewed alternatives: paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, platypnea, reflux-related discomfort, positional upper-airway obstruction. Evidence Limitations

Sleeping elevated by preference, reflux, pain, pregnancy, or nasal obstruction does not establish orthopnea; patient-reported pillow count varies, and absence does not exclude heart or lung disease. Therefore, Orthopnea cannot confirm or exclude breathlessness caused or worsened by lying flat, classically from pulmonary congestion in heart failure but also possible with obesity, diaphragmatic or neuromuscular weakness, ascites, pleural disease, or obstructive lung disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea

  • platypnea

  • reflux-related discomfort

  • positional upper-airway obstruction

False Positive Situations

A false-positive response can reflect another painful or sensory process such as paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, platypnea, reflux-related discomfort, positional upper-airway obstruction, or a trigger that was not applied as defined.

False Negative Situations

Orthopnea may not reproduce when the trigger is incomplete, symptoms are intermittent, treatment has reduced the response, or communication is limited.

Modern Status

Current clinically important symptom and a classic congestion clue, but it is not specific for heart failure and is distinct from waking later with paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. Interpret Orthopnea in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Sleeping elevated by preference, reflux, pain, pregnancy, or nasal obstruction does not establish orthopnea; patient-reported pillow count varies, and absence does not exclude heart or lung disease.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Orthopnea, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Ask whether breathlessness begins or worsens when reclining, how quickly it occurs, how many pillows or what sleeping position is needed, and whether sitting or standing relieves it; assess onset, chest pain, cough, edema, weight trend, lung sounds, oxygenation, respiratory effort, cardiac findings, and associated nighttime episodes without forcing the patient flat. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not force a breathless patient supine to reproduce symptoms; keep the patient in the position of easiest breathing and urgently escalate new severe orthopnea with hypoxemia, frothy sputum, chest pain, diaphoresis, confusion, hypotension, or rapidly increasing work of breathing.
