Definition

AV fistula infection is infection of a native hemodialysis fistula or its cannulation sites. Although less common than catheter infection, it can spread rapidly into the high-flow bloodstream and cause bacteremia, endocarditis, metastatic infection, thrombosis, or catastrophic bleeding from damaged access tissue.

Pathology

Local cellulitis, abscess, infected thrombus, pseudoaneurysm, or deeper access-wall infection may be present. High flow can disseminate organisms, while inflammation weakens vessel and overlying skin.

Pathophysiology

Skin organisms introduced at cannulation can colonize tissue or thrombus and seed blood with each high-flow dialysis session. Staphylococcus aureus is a major concern because it adheres, invades, and causes endocarditis and metastatic abscess. Infection-related edema and endothelial injury can trigger thrombosis; tissue destruction over an aneurysm can culminate in rupture and exsanguinating hemorrhage.

Etiology

Repeated needle puncture, contaminated cannulation, buttonhole-tract colonization, poor skin integrity, scratching or scab removal, diabetes, immunosuppression, bacteremia, pseudoaneurysm, and adjacent cellulitis create entry or persistence pathways.

Risk Factors

  • Buttonhole cannulation, repeated difficult cannulation, or skin breakdown

  • Diabetes, immunosuppression, poor hygiene access, or nasal/skin colonization

  • Pseudoaneurysm, thrombosis, hematoma, or prior access infection

  • Recent bacteremia or invasive procedure

Signs Symptoms

  • Redness, warmth, swelling, tenderness, drainage, pustule, ulcer, or changing scab

  • Fever, chills, rigors, malaise, or hypotension during dialysis

  • Pain with cannulation or new bleeding

  • Thrill may remain present, weaken, or disappear if thrombosis occurs

Diagnostics

  • Inspect the full access and surrounding skin before cannulation

  • Obtain blood and drainage cultures when indicated before avoidable antimicrobial delay

  • Use ultrasound or surgical evaluation for abscess, infected thrombus, pseudoaneurysm, or deep involvement

  • Assess for endocarditis and metastatic infection in bacteremia

Labs

  • CBC and inflammatory markers can support but do not exclude infection

  • Blood cultures are central when fever or systemic symptoms occur

  • Potassium and volume matter if the access cannot be used

  • Hemoglobin and coagulation context matter when tissue is bleeding or friable

Treatments

  • Do not cannulate through infected skin

  • Give culture-guided antimicrobial therapy with urgent dialysis-access and infectious-disease input according to severity

  • Drain abscess and revise or remove infected tissue/access when source control requires it

  • Arrange alternative dialysis access when treatment cannot wait

Nursing Priorities

  • Inspect the AV fistula before every use for redness, warmth, swelling, tenderness, drainage, skin breakdown, aneurysmal change, and track marks because localized infection can seed the bloodstream.

  • Palpate the thrill and auscultate the bruit while assessing hand perfusion, edema, fever, chills, and hemodynamics because infection, thrombosis, stenosis, steal, and pseudoaneurysm can coexist.

  • Obtain ordered blood and drainage cultures before antibiotics when this does not delay sepsis treatment because microbiologic identification guides therapy and evaluation for metastatic infection.

  • Administer prescribed antibiotics and coordinate nephrology, surgery, and an alternative dialysis-access plan before cannulating suspicious tissue because infected or structurally weak fistula segments may rupture or fail.

  • Activate emergency care for sepsis, absent thrill, rapidly expanding swelling, brisk fistula bleeding, distal ischemia, or suspected endocarditis because access infection can threaten life, limb, and dialysis continuity.

Complications

  • Bacteremia, sepsis, endocarditis, vertebral or other metastatic infection

  • Access thrombosis and loss

  • Pseudoaneurysm, rupture, severe hemorrhage

  • Temporary catheter need and recurrent infection

Contraindications

  • Do not inherit or use the unsafe alias 'atrioventricular' for this dialysis access

  • Do not cannulate through infected or ulcerated tissue

  • Do not dismiss fever because a thrill is still present

Red Flags

  • Fever or rigors with hypotension, confusion, tachycardia, or rising lactate

  • Absent thrill or bruit, sudden access dysfunction, or acute arm swelling

  • Brisk bleeding, expanding pseudoaneurysm, thinning shiny skin, or exposed graft material

  • Cold painful hand, sensory loss, weakness, chest pain, or new cardiac murmur

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to wash the access arm, check the thrill and skin every day, and report warmth, drainage, swelling, fever, a weaker vibration, or prolonged bleeding immediately.

  • Explain that blood pressure cuffs, venipuncture, tight clothing, scratching, and sleeping on the access arm can damage flow or skin defenses and increase complications.

Nclex Traps

  • A present thrill does not rule out infection

  • Fever during dialysis can be access bacteremia

  • Never cannulate infected skin

  • Infected aneurysmal tissue can bleed catastrophically
