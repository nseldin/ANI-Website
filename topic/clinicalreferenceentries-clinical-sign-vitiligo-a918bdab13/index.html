Definition

Vitiligo is an acquired disorder in which functioning melanocytes, the cells that make skin pigment, are lost. It produces flat, clearly bordered lighter or white patches without surface scale or thickening.

Plain Language

Vitiligo causes patches of skin, mucosa, or hair to lose color because pigment-making cells are no longer working there.

Why It Matters

Vitiligo is not contagious, but depigmented skin burns more easily and the visible change can cause major psychosocial harm. It can occur with autoimmune thyroid disease and other autoimmune disorders, so the pattern, spread, associated symptoms, and uncertain mimics need appropriate clinical evaluation.

Assessment Method

Inspect the full skin surface in neutral light with consent and document color, borders, distribution, symmetry, hair and mucosal involvement, onset, spread, itch, trauma-related new lesions, sun sensitivity, family history, and autoimmune symptoms; use Wood-lamp or dermatology assessment when appearance is uncertain.

Positive Finding

Flat, well-demarcated lighter or milky-white macules and patches occur without scale or thickening; affected hair may turn white and lesions may be symmetric, segmental, acral, facial, mucosal, or trauma-associated.

Mechanism

Immune injury removes or disables melanocytes, the epidermal cells that produce skin pigment, so affected skin and sometimes hair lose melanin.

Evidence Limitations

Contrast varies by baseline skin tone, tanning, lighting, and disease activity; several infections, inflammatory disorders, chemical exposures, scars, and congenital lesions can look pale or white. Therefore, Vitiligo cannot confirm or exclude acquired immune-mediated loss of functioning melanocytes, sometimes associated with autoimmune thyroid disease, alopecia areata, pernicious anemia, type 1 diabetes, or another autoimmune condition by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as tinea versicolor, pityriasis alba, postinflammatory hypopigmentation, chemical leukoderma.

False Negative Situations

Vitiligo may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of acquired immune-mediated loss of functioning melanocytes, sometimes associated with autoimmune thyroid disease, alopecia areata, pernicious anemia, type 1 diabetes, or another autoimmune condition.
