Definition

Mydriasis means pupillary dilation: one or both pupils are larger than expected for the lighting and the person's baseline.

Plain Language

Mydriasis means an unusually large or dilated pupil.

Why It Matters

Mydriasis may be normal in darkness or caused by medication, but a new fixed or poorly reactive dilated pupil can indicate third-nerve compression, severe hypoxia, brain herniation, eye trauma, or acute angle-closure glaucoma. Associated pain, redness, vision change, abnormal eye movement, or reduced consciousness makes the finding urgent.

Associated Conditions

  • normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation

Assessment Method

Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document shape, symmetry and direct, consensual and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines, plants and chemical exposure, and assess visual acuity, eye pain and redness, eyelids, extraocular movements, diplopia, mental status, vital signs, skin moisture, bladder and bowel findings, trauma, and the neurologic examination.

Positive Finding

One or both pupils are larger than expected for the lighting and baseline and constrict poorly or not at all to light when the dilated pupil is pathologic.

Mechanism

Excess sympathetic activation of the iris dilator, reduced parasympathetic input to the iris sphincter, direct pharmacologic blockade, traumatic sphincter damage, or failure of oculomotor pathways shifts the pupil toward dilation.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Mydriasis finding raises concern for normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation because excess sympathetic activation of the iris dilator, reduced parasympathetic input to the iris sphincter, direct pharmacologic blockade, traumatic sphincter damage, or failure of oculomotor pathways shifts the pupil toward dilation. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Mydriasis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Mydriasis to focus evaluation of normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation and the reviewed alternatives: physiologic dark response, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve palsy, pharmacologic dilation, traumatic mydriasis, acute angle-closure glaucoma. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document shape, symmetry and direct, consensual and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines, plants and chemical exposure, and assess visual acuity, eye pain and redness, eyelids, extraocular movements, diplopia, mental status, vital signs, skin moisture, bladder and bowel findings, trauma, and the neurologic examination. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Mydriasis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Pupils normally enlarge in darkness and with arousal, diameter varies with age and drugs, systemic exposure is often bilateral while accidental one-eye contamination can be unilateral, and visual inspection alone cannot distinguish pharmacologic blockade from neurologic denervation. Therefore, Mydriasis cannot confirm or exclude normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • physiologic dark response

  • Adie tonic pupil

  • third-nerve palsy

  • pharmacologic dilation

  • traumatic mydriasis

  • acute angle-closure glaucoma

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as physiologic dark response, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve palsy, pharmacologic dilation, traumatic mydriasis, acute angle-closure glaucoma.

False Negative Situations

Mydriasis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation.

Modern Status

Current descriptive finding, not proof of anticholinergic exposure or brain death; symmetry, reactivity, consciousness, eye findings, drug exposure, and neurologic context determine meaning. Interpret Mydriasis in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Pupils normally enlarge in darkness and with arousal, diameter varies with age and drugs, systemic exposure is often bilateral while accidental one-eye contamination can be unilateral, and visual inspection alone cannot distinguish pharmacologic blockade from neurologic denervation.

Safety Precautions

Observe Mydriasis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document shape, symmetry and direct, consensual and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines, plants and chemical exposure, and assess visual acuity, eye pain and redness, eyelids, extraocular movements, diplopia, mental status, vital signs, skin moisture, bladder and bowel findings, trauma, and the neurologic examination. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not perform unsupervised pharmacologic testing; a new fixed or poorly reactive dilated pupil with severe headache, ptosis, diplopia, abnormal eye movement, focal deficit, reduced consciousness, hypoxia or trauma is an emergency, and a painful red eye with blurred vision, halos, nausea or vomiting requires urgent assessment for acute angle closure.

Communicate the finding with its suspected association, normal darkness or arousal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic drugs, topical dilating agents, Adie tonic pupil, third-nerve compression, iris trauma, severe hypoxia, or intracranial herniation, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.
