Definition

Digoxin toxicity develops when excess sodium-potassium ATPase inhibition raises intracellular calcium while increasing vagal and ectopic electrical effects, especially with kidney dysfunction or electrolyte disturbance. The characteristic pattern is nausea, anorexia, confusion, color-vision change, bradycardia, atrioventricular block, atrial tachycardia with block, or ventricular dysrhythmia. Confirm or stage it with immediate electrocardiogram and continuous rhythm monitoring; distinguish it from acute coronary syndrome, hyperkalemia from kidney failure, beta-blocker or calcium-channel blocker toxicity, and primary conduction disease by exposure and rhythm pattern. The priority is to stop digoxin and correct contributing electrolytes cautiously because ventricular fibrillation can complicate Digoxin toxicity. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • immediate electrocardiogram and continuous rhythm monitoring

  • potassium, magnesium, calcium, creatinine, glucose, and an appropriately timed digoxin concentration

  • medication, interaction, dose, formulation, and acute-versus-chronic exposure review

  • Distinguish Digoxin toxicity from acute coronary syndrome, hyperkalemia from kidney failure, beta-blocker or calcium-channel blocker toxicity, and primary conduction disease by exposure and rhythm pattern.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • stop digoxin and correct contributing electrolytes cautiously

  • give digoxin immune Fab for life-threatening dysrhythmia, severe hyperkalemia, or other accepted indications

  • use poison-center guidance and supportive rhythm care

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for unstable bradycardia, ventricular arrhythmia, high-grade block, severe hyperkalemia, hypotension, syncope, or altered mental status because expert toxicology and critical-care support are required.

  • Stop further digoxin and obtain the medication timing, kidney function, electrolytes, and interacting drugs because impaired clearance and potassium or magnesium abnormalities increase toxicity.

  • Place the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring and obtain serial electrocardiograms because digoxin can produce bradyarrhythmias, conduction block, or ventricular dysrhythmias.

  • Administer ordered digoxin-specific antibody fragments for life-threatening toxicity and monitor potassium and rhythm because reversal can rapidly shift potassium and change cardiac conduction.

Complications

  • ventricular fibrillation can complicate Digoxin toxicity.

  • high-grade block can complicate Digoxin toxicity.

  • cardiac arrest can complicate Digoxin toxicity.

  • severe hyperkalemia in acute poisoning

  • Fab-related potassium shift and recurrence monitoring needs

Red Flags

  • Unstable bradycardia or high-grade atrioventricular block

  • Ventricular arrhythmia, syncope, or hypotension

  • Severe hyperkalemia in acute toxicity

  • Altered mental status with significant cardiac findings

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient not to repeat a missed or vomited dose without instructions and to keep laboratory and kidney-function follow-up.

  • Review early toxicity symptoms such as nausea, poor appetite, confusion, visual change, slow pulse, or palpitations and when to seek urgent care.

Nclex Traps

  • A serum level drawn soon after a dose can be misleading

  • After immune Fab, routine total digoxin assays do not reflect active unbound drug
