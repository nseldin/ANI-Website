Definition

Nystagmus means involuntary rhythmic eye movement. The eyes may drift and quickly correct in a jerk pattern or move back and forth more evenly in a pendular pattern.

Plain Language

Nystagmus is repetitive eye movement that the person is not intentionally making.

Why It Matters

The direction, trigger, fixation response, and accompanying hearing or neurologic findings can help distinguish a benign positional or peripheral vestibular cause from medication toxicity, brainstem or cerebellar disease, or stroke. Sudden persistent nystagmus with inability to walk, direction change with gaze, a spontaneous vertical pattern, focal neurologic findings, severe head or neck pain, or reduced consciousness is an emergency.

Associated Conditions

  • physiologic end-gaze or optokinetic responses, infantile nystagmus, peripheral vestibular disorders such as benign positional vertigo or vestibular neuritis, central brainstem or cerebellar disease, demyelination, medication or alcohol toxicity, and visual impairment

Assessment Method

Observe in primary gaze and during controlled horizontal and vertical gaze, document onset, direction by the quick phase when present, horizontal, vertical or torsional plane, amplitude, frequency, gaze dependence, fixation effect, positional or head-motion trigger, and associated vertigo, hearing change, oscillopsia, gait, ataxia, diplopia, dysarthria, headache, medications and focal neurologic findings.

Positive Finding

The eyes repeatedly and rhythmically drift and correct in a jerk pattern or oscillate back and forth in a pendular pattern; the movement may be spontaneous, gaze-evoked, positional, or triggered.

Mechanism

Asymmetric vestibular tone or instability in gaze-holding, ocular-motor, cerebellar or visual feedback circuits drives the eyes away from the target and may generate corrective quick phases, while pendular forms reflect continuous ocular-motor instability.

Clinical Interpretation

Evidence Limitations

Subtle movement can be missed, visual fixation can suppress some peripheral patterns, extreme end-gaze can produce a physiologic response, medications alter eye movements, and observation alone cannot reliably identify the lesion or exclude stroke. Therefore, Nystagmus cannot confirm or exclude physiologic end-gaze or optokinetic responses, infantile nystagmus, peripheral vestibular disorders such as benign positional vertigo or vestibular neuritis, central brainstem or cerebellar disease, demyelination, medication or alcohol toxicity, and visual impairment by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • ocular flutter

  • opsoclonus

  • saccadic intrusions

  • voluntary eye oscillation

  • eyelid or head tremor

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as ocular flutter, opsoclonus, saccadic intrusions, voluntary eye oscillation, eyelid or head tremor.

False Negative Situations

Nystagmus may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of physiologic end-gaze or optokinetic responses, infantile nystagmus, peripheral vestibular disorders such as benign positional vertigo or vestibular neuritis, central brainstem or cerebellar disease, demyelination, medication or alcohol toxicity, and visual impairment.

Modern Status

Current pattern-dependent sign, not a diagnosis; the direction, trigger, fixation response and associated neurologic or auditory findings help distinguish physiologic, peripheral vestibular, central, congenital, and toxic causes.
