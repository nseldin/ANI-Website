Class

Shorter-acting thiazide available in oral and intravenous formulations

Used To Treat

Hypertension and edema under oral labeling; IV chlorothiazide is used when oral medication is impractical, including selected monitored sequential-nephron-blockade strategies. It is not an IV loop and does not produce furosemide-equivalent physiology.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: IV access does not make chlorothiazide a benign or automatically effective add-on. Sequential use with a loop can rapidly cause profound sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, volume, pressure, and kidney-function changes. Reconstitute and administer the injection exactly according to product and institutional instructions, and reassess before repeat dosing.

Mechanism

Chlorothiazide is delivered to the distal tubular lumen and inhibits NCC, reducing sodium-chloride reabsorption. More sodium reaches ENaC, where sodium uptake drives potassium and hydrogen secretion, and volume contraction activates aldosterone. The result can be hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. NCC blockade reduces the kidney's ability to dilute urine, so vasopressin and free-water intake can produce severe hyponatremia. Lower intracellular sodium increases basolateral sodium-calcium exchange and reduces urinary calcium. When given after a loop, chlorothiazide blocks the distal segment that has adapted to reclaim loop-delivered sodium, creating sequential nephron blockade. IV delivery bypasses intestinal absorption but still requires renal perfusion and tubular secretion to reach the luminal target; poor perfusion or anuria therefore cannot be solved merely by choosing the IV route.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check pressure, orthostasis, weight, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, creatinine/eGFR, glucose, and uric acid when relevant before and after initiation or escalation.

  • Teach earlier-day dosing, slow position changes, photosensitivity precautions, and urgent reporting of confusion, seizure, severe weakness, palpitations, eye pain, or sudden visual change.

  • For IV use, verify reconstitution, compatibility, line, rate, recent loop exposure, and a defined urine-response window. The route solves absorption, not monitoring.

Contraindications

  • Anuria and hypersensitivity are core contraindication patterns. Symptomatic hyponatremia, major potassium or magnesium loss, hypotension, or evolving kidney injury requires reassessment.

  • Do not assume every thiazide is ineffective below one eGFR threshold; agent, dose, route, indication, and actual response matter.

  • The injection label contraindicates anuria and chlorothiazide hypersensitivity. Clarify severe hepatic disease because electrolyte shifts can precipitate encephalopathy.

Interactions

  • Lithium exposure can rise as sodium depletion increases proximal lithium reabsorption; NSAIDs can blunt effect and worsen kidney function.

  • SSRIs, carbamazepine, desmopressin, and high water intake magnify hyponatremia; low potassium increases digoxin-related arrhythmia risk.

  • Concurrent loop therapy is often intentional but sharply increases depletion risk; neuromuscular blockers and antihypertensives may have greater effects.

Key Labs

  • Blood pressure, orthostasis, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, glucose, uric acid, weight, and urine calcium when that is the indication.

  • Check sooner in older adults, low body mass, high water intake, CKD, or interacting medication use because severe sodium decline can appear quickly.

Nclex Traps

  • Thiazides lower urinary calcium while raising uric acid; those effects come from different transport responses.

  • They can cause profound hyponatremia because the diluting segment is blocked while concentrating ability and vasopressin action remain.

  • IV chlorothiazide is still a distal NCC blocker. It is chosen for parenteral delivery, not because it becomes a loop diuretic.

Description

Chlorothiazide, historically branded Diuril, blocks NCC in the early distal convoluted tubule to increase sodium-water excretion and is one of the few thiazides available intravenously. Its oral absorption is relatively low and variable, so IV administration can be useful when gut edema, vomiting, an unavailable enteral route, or a critical-care plan makes oral delivery unreliable. It is a weaker stand-alone natriuretic than a loop. In resistant congestion it may be added after an adequate IV loop to block downstream sodium reclamation. The parenteral route makes onset more controllable but does not remove severe hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, alkalosis, hypotension, or kidney risk. Chlorothiazide and metolazone both provide distal blockade. Selection reflects route, absorption, availability, cost, prior response, and protocol rather than a universal superiority claim.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, volume depletion, hyperuricemia or gout, photosensitivity, and glucose intolerance follow NCC blockade and compensatory distal transport.

  • Severe neurologic hyponatremia, arrhythmia, pancreatitis, severe skin reactions, and acute angle-closure glaucoma or myopia require urgent action.
