Used To Treat

HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus infections according to drug, route, severity, immune status, timing, and indication. Oral valacyclovir and famciclovir improve outpatient exposure, while intravenous acyclovir is used when rapid reliable systemic exposure is needed for severe HSV or VZV disease.

Description

Acyclovir, valacyclovir, and famciclovir exploit an enzyme that herpes-infected cells make. Viral thymidine kinase performs the first phosphorylation step far more efficiently in infected cells than in uninfected cells. Host kinases then form active triphosphates that inhibit viral DNA polymerase. Valacyclovir is the L-valyl prodrug of acyclovir and mainly solves acyclovir's poor oral bioavailability. Famciclovir is converted to penciclovir, whose triphosphate persists longer inside infected cells but usually slows DNA elongation rather than producing the strict chain termination associated with acyclovir triphosphate. The pathway explains both selectivity and resistance.

Mechanism

HSV or VZV thymidine kinase converts acyclovir or penciclovir to a monophosphate; cellular guanylate and nucleoside-diphosphate kinases add the next two phosphates. Acyclovir triphosphate competes with deoxyguanosine triphosphate for viral DNA polymerase and, because it lacks a normal 3-prime hydroxyl group, becomes incorporated and terminates or functionally arrests the growing viral DNA chain. Penciclovir triphosphate also competitively inhibits viral polymerase, but its incorporated form is not an obligate immediate chain terminator. Loss or alteration of viral thymidine kinase prevents activation and can cause cross-resistance among acyclovir, valacyclovir, and famciclovir; polymerase mutations provide another route. Foscarnet bypasses phosphorylation and therefore may retain activity against thymidine-kinase-deficient virus, although polymerase resistance can still occur.

Boxed Warning

These three agents have no current U.S. boxed warning, but renal and neurologic toxicity can become severe when acyclovir exposure rises. Acyclovir is eliminated mainly by the kidney and can precipitate in renal tubules when intravenous infusion is too rapid, hydration is poor, or dosing is not adjusted for kidney function. Accumulation can produce confusion, tremor, hallucinations, myoclonus, or seizures. Valacyclovir has also been associated with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura/hemolytic uremic syndrome in severely immunocompromised people receiving very high doses. The absence of a box does not remove dose, hydration, or neurologic surveillance.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, headache, diarrhea, and local infusion-site irritation

  • Crystal-associated acute kidney injury, neurotoxicity, severe skin or hypersensitivity reactions, and rare hematologic microangiopathy in high-risk valacyclovir exposure

Contraindications

  • Previous serious hypersensitivity to the active drug or its prodrug; valacyclovir allergy also implies acyclovir exposure.

  • Recalculate dose and interval for renal impairment, dehydration, older age, nephrotoxic co-medications, and severe illness rather than using a standard outpatient schedule.

Nursing Essentials

  • Start treatment early when replication is active, but do not promise eradication: latent HSV and VZV genomes remain in sensory ganglia because these drugs inhibit replicating virus rather than eliminating latency.

  • For IV acyclovir, verify dilution, infusion time, hydration, urine output, creatinine trend, and neurologic status. Pain may improve before lesions stop shedding, so transmission counseling still matters.

Interactions

  • Nephrotoxins and drugs competing for renal tubular secretion can increase exposure or kidney injury.

  • Probenecid can reduce acyclovir renal clearance; mycophenolate and other renally handled drugs may add exposure or toxicity in susceptible patients.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR, hydration and urine output; CBC and liver tests when disease severity, prolonged therapy, or immunosuppression warrants them

  • Lesion evolution, pain, ocular or neurologic involvement, immune status, adherence, transmission precautions, and evidence of resistance if lesions progress despite adequate exposure

Nclex Traps

  • Valacyclovir is not a different antiviral target; it is a better-absorbed prodrug that becomes acyclovir.

  • These drugs shorten or suppress active disease but do not remove latent virus from ganglia and do not guarantee that transmission cannot occur.
