Plain Language

Body dysmorphic disorder causes an intense preoccupation with one or more perceived appearance flaws that other people do not see or consider slight. The person repeatedly checks, compares, camouflages, seeks reassurance, picks skin, or pursues cosmetic changes, and the distress can severely disrupt daily life.

Why It Matters

BDD carries high depression, self-harm, and suicide risk, and repeated cosmetic or dermatologic procedures often fail to resolve the preoccupation and can increase conflict or harm. Nurses should ask directly about appearance rituals and suicide, assess skin or procedure injury, distinguish BDD from eating disorders and psychosis, and connect the patient with BDD-specific CBT and evidence-bounded treatment.

Definition

Body dysmorphic disorder is an obsessive-compulsive and related disorder involving preoccupation with perceived appearance defects that are not observable or appear slight to others, accompanied by repetitive behaviors or mental acts such as mirror checking, grooming, reassurance, comparing, or camouflaging. The pattern causes significant distress or impairment and is not better explained by weight or body-fat concern occurring within an eating disorder. Insight ranges from good to absent or delusional, and absent insight does not by itself change BDD into a primary psychotic disorder.

Pathology

BDD is maintained by selective attention to perceived flaws, distorted appearance appraisal, shame, comparison, checking, reassurance, avoidance, camouflage, and procedure seeking. These behaviors reduce distress briefly but keep appearance beliefs central. It is a clinical syndrome, not proof that no physical feature exists; slight or real differences can coexist with disproportionate preoccupation and impairment.

Pathophysiology

  • Visual and self-referential processing differences are research findings, not diagnostic tests. No scan or biomarker confirms BDD.

  • Checking, comparison, reassurance, concealment, and avoidance act like compulsive safety behaviors; BDD-focused CBT and exposure with response prevention target the cycle.

Etiology

No single cause explains BDD. Genetic vulnerability, perfectionism, bullying, teasing, discrimination, trauma, social comparison, appearance-focused environments, and other mental disorders may contribute. Social media may intensify comparison for some people but is not a universal cause.

Risk Factors

  • Family history of obsessive-compulsive related disorders, anxiety, or depression

  • Bullying, teasing, appearance-based discrimination, trauma, or repeated critical comparison

  • OCD, depression, social anxiety, eating disorder, substance use, or perfectionistic traits

  • Cosmetic, dermatologic, dental, hair, or fitness settings where repeated procedure seeking can occur without recognition of the underlying disorder

Signs Symptoms

  • Hours of worry about skin, hair, nose, teeth, face, body symmetry, scars, genital appearance, muscle size, or another perceived flaw

  • Mirror checking or avoidance, grooming, skin picking, measuring, comparing, photographing, reassurance-seeking, changing clothes, camouflaging, or researching procedures

  • Avoiding school, work, intimacy, daylight, photographs, exercise settings, or health care because of shame

  • Repeated cosmetic, dermatologic, dental, or surgical consultations with dissatisfaction or shifting concerns

  • Muscle dysmorphia may involve belief that the body is insufficiently muscular, with compulsive exercise, rigid diet, supplement use, or anabolic-steroid risk

Diagnostics

  • Ask sensitively about time spent on appearance concerns, the perceived defect, repetitive behaviors, avoidance, impairment, insight, procedure history, skin picking, exercise, diet, supplements, anabolic steroids, depression, self-harm, and suicide.

  • Determine whether concern is primarily about weight or fat within an eating disorder, gender incongruence, a culturally normative practice, a genuine medical lesion with proportionate concern, OCD, social anxiety, depression, or psychosis.

  • For patients repeatedly seeking cosmetic or dermatologic procedures, screen for BDD and assess expectations, capacity, coercion, and suicide risk before intervention.

  • BDD scales can support assessment and outcome tracking but do not independently diagnose the disorder.

Labs

  • No laboratory test, imaging study, dermatologic photograph, or cosmetic opinion confirms or excludes BDD.

  • Use targeted evaluation for actual skin disease, infection, wound, endocrine or nutritional effects, eating disorder, anabolic-steroid complications, or medication issues while avoiding endless low-value testing driven by reassurance.

  • Normal findings do not make the distress fake; a real minor feature does not exclude disproportionate BDD.

Assessment

Use private, nonjudgmental questions and do not debate whether the person is attractive. Assess exact appearance beliefs, time consumed, rituals, avoidance, insight, depression, suicide, self-harm, skin damage, procedure or injection complications, eating and exercise patterns, supplements or steroids, substances, and functional loss. Ask what action the person plans if the perceived defect cannot be changed.

Differential

  • Eating disorders when the central concern is body weight or fat, while recognizing possible comorbidity

  • OCD, social anxiety disorder, major depression, illness anxiety disorder, delusional disorder, psychosis, or gender dysphoria

  • Proportionate concern about a visible lesion, disfigurement, injury, or discrimination without the full repetitive and impairing BDD pattern

  • Excoriation disorder, trichotillomania, avoidant personality disorder, autism-related interests, or trauma-related shame

  • Substance or medication effects, anabolic-steroid use, body-building practices, or neurologic and cognitive disorders

Treatments

  • Offer BDD-specific CBT including cognitive work, exposure and response prevention, reduction of checking, comparison, camouflage and reassurance, attention training, and relapse planning.

  • For adults with moderate impairment, offer a choice of intensive BDD-specific CBT or an SSRI; severe impairment supports combined CBT and SSRI treatment according to guideline and patient factors.

  • Fluoxetine is the representative guideline-supported initial generic for adult BDD and is off-label for this indication. Explain delayed benefit, monitor closely, and follow the current product label for contraindications and interactions.

  • Children and young people should receive developmentally adapted family-involved CBT first. Specialist-guided fluoxetine may be added after multidisciplinary review for significant impairment or inadequate response and remains off-label for BDD.

  • Poor response requires review of diagnosis, adherence, adequate CBT and medication trials, comorbidity, suicide risk, and referral to a specialist BDD team; cosmetic intervention is not a substitute for evidence-based psychiatric care.

Contraindications

  • Do not dismiss the patient as vain, argue about appearance, repeatedly reassure, or promise that a cosmetic procedure will cure the disorder.

  • Do not use antipsychotic monotherapy routinely for BDD, including absent insight or beliefs of delusional intensity, without another supported indication and specialist formulation.

  • Do not combine fluoxetine with an MAOI, ignore serotonergic interactions, or stop it abruptly; account for its long half-life and current label when switching medicines.

  • Do not approve an elective appearance procedure without assessing BDD, expectations, capacity, coercion, self-harm, and suicide risk when warning signs are present.

Safety Considerations

  • Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plans, prior attempts, self-surgery, deliberate injury, and what the person might do if appearance cannot be changed.

  • Assess infection, bleeding, burns, tissue injury, injected substances, wound complications, overexercise, dehydration, restrictive diet, supplements, and anabolic-steroid effects.

  • During SSRI initiation and dose changes, monitor restlessness or akathisia, increased anxiety, suicidal thinking, mania, serotonin toxicity, interactions, and discontinuation symptoms.

  • Limit medication quantities and increase contact when overdose or suicide risk is high; urgent risk requires crisis intervention rather than routine follow-up.

Nursing Priorities

  • Establish suicide and self-harm safety before extended appearance discussion.

  • Validate distress without agreeing that the perceived defect is severe or providing repeated appearance reassurance.

  • Document rituals, avoidance, procedure history, wounds, substances, nutrition, exercise, function, and insight in objective language.

  • Reinforce CBT targets and monitor fluoxetine adherence, adverse effects, activation, mood, sleep, and suicide risk.

Red Flags

  • Suicidal intent, recent attempt, self-surgery, dangerous skin picking, injected substances, or a plan to harm self or a clinician after procedure refusal

  • Severe infection, bleeding, wound breakdown, toxic exposure, dehydration, malnutrition, syncope, rhabdomyolysis concern, or anabolic-steroid complications

  • New severe agitation, akathisia, mania, psychosis, or escalating suicidality after medication change

  • Complete school or work withdrawal, inability to eat or leave home, repeated hazardous procedures, or rapidly escalating debt and conflict

Complications

  • Suicide, self-harm, depression, social isolation, school or job loss, relationship impairment, and substance misuse

  • Scarring, infection, nerve or tissue injury, disfigurement, anesthesia risk, debt, and dissatisfaction after repeated procedures

  • Eating disorder, compulsive exercise, supplement toxicity, anabolic-steroid harms, and muscle injury in muscle dysmorphia

  • Conflict, threats, legal problems, or loss of care when clinicians focus only on procedures and miss BDD

Prognosis

BDD is often persistent without treatment but can improve substantially with BDD-specific CBT and, for selected patients, an SSRI. Improvement includes less ritual time, fewer procedures, better attendance and relationships, and reduced suicide risk; residual appearance concerns do not mean treatment failed.

Prevention

  • Recognize repeated cosmetic consultation, severe appearance shame, ritual checking, camouflage, and avoidance early.

  • Address bullying, discrimination, social comparison, depression, eating disorder, and anabolic-steroid or supplement risk.

  • Build a relapse plan for checking, reassurance, social media comparison, procedure seeking, and suicidal thinking after improvement.

Patient Education

  • BDD is not vanity. The distress and repetitive behaviors are real and can be severe even when others see little or no defect.

  • The goal is not endless reassurance or perfect appearance; treatment reduces the preoccupation and restores life.

  • CBT with exposure and response prevention and selected SSRIs can help. Fluoxetine use for BDD is off-label and requires monitoring.

  • Seek urgent help for suicidal thoughts, self-surgery, dangerous procedures or injections, severe wounds, starvation, or marked agitation after a medication change.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need family-involved CBT, bullying and school assessment, direct suicide monitoring, and specialist oversight of any SSRI.

  • Muscle dysmorphia requires assessment of compulsive training, injury, dietary restriction, supplements, anabolic steroids, cardiac and endocrine effects, and suicide risk.

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding change medication and procedure risk; coordinate psychiatric, obstetric, dermatologic, and primary care decisions.

  • People with visible differences or experiences of discrimination need validation of real social harm while clinicians separately assess disproportionate preoccupation and rituals.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not provide repeated appearance reassurance; validate distress and redirect to function and treatment.

  • Absent insight or delusional intensity can occur within BDD and does not automatically mean primary psychosis.

  • Cosmetic procedures are not first-line treatment and may not relieve the disorder.

  • Suicide assessment is a priority; fluoxetine is an off-label representative option, not a casual cosmetic-anxiety prescription.
