Contraindications

  • Severe hypotension or cardiogenic shock, severe LV dysfunction/heart failure, unpaced sick sinus syndrome or second-/third-degree AV block, accessory-pathway atrial fibrillation/flutter, ventricular tachycardia, and serious hypersensitivity.

  • IV beta blockers and IV verapamil must not be given together or close in time. Repeated IV doses in major hepatic/renal failure can accumulate and generally should be avoided.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify the rhythm, QRS width, accessory-pathway status, ventricular function, pressure, pulse, PR interval, bowel baseline, and recent beta blocker/digoxin/antiarrhythmic therapy. Use continuous ECG/BP and resuscitation capability for IV administration.

  • Teach constipation prevention with individualized fluid, fiber, mobility, and bowel planning when appropriate; verify immediate- versus extended-release formulation and do not crush a modified-release product unless its exact label permits.

Interactions

  • Beta blockers add AV block and myocardial depression; IV coadministration is contraindicated. Digoxin levels can rise roughly 50% to 75% with some oral verapamil products, so dose reduction and level/clinical monitoring may be required.

  • CYP3A inhibitors and grapefruit can raise verapamil; inducers such as rifampin reduce it. Current Verelan PM labeling limits simvastatin to 10 mg/day and lovastatin to 40 mg/day. Verapamil can also raise carbamazepine, cyclosporine, alcohol, and neuromuscular-blocker effects.

Key Labs

  • Heart rate, ECG/PR/QRS and rhythm, pressure, perfusion, ventricular function, respiratory/heart-failure findings, and bowel pattern. Monitor continuously during IV use.

  • Digoxin, liver tests, renal/hepatic context, and selected interacting-drug concentrations or CK when clinically indicated; in overdose trend glucose, potassium, calcium, lactate, acid-base status, and cardiac function.

Nclex Traps

  • Constipation points toward verapamil, but the lifesaving distinction is stronger cardiac depression than a dihydropyridine such as amlodipine.

  • Do not give an AV-node blocker to pre-excited atrial fibrillation. Slowing the normal gate can accelerate conduction down the accessory pathway and trigger ventricular fibrillation.

Description

Verapamil is the most cardiac-predominant commonly used calcium channel blocker. It slows SA- and AV-node conduction, prolongs AV-node refractoriness, reduces myocardial contractility, and also dilates arteries. This makes it effective for selected AV-node-dependent supraventricular tachycardia and rate control, but it can produce profound bradycardia, heart block, hypotension, or decompensated systolic failure, especially with beta blockers or digoxin. Constipation is its classic noncardiac clue because calcium-channel blockade reduces intestinal smooth-muscle contractility and slows transit. IV verapamil must be given slowly under continuous ECG and blood-pressure monitoring, and it must not be used for wide-complex ventricular tachycardia or pre-excited atrial fibrillation.

Used To Treat

IV verapamil converts selected AV-node-dependent paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia and temporarily controls ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation/flutter without an accessory pathway. Oral products treat hypertension, angina, and selected arrhythmias depending on formulation and specialist plan.

Mechanism

Verapamil blocks open and inactivated L-type calcium channels in nodal tissue, myocardium, and vascular smooth muscle. AV-node phase-0 depolarization depends on calcium influx, so verapamil slows conduction and lengthens refractoriness in a rate-related manner, interrupting AV-node reentry and filtering atrial impulses. In cardiomyocytes, less trigger calcium means less sarcoplasmic-reticulum calcium release and weaker contraction; in arterioles, reduced calcium-calmodulin-myosin light-chain kinase signaling lowers afterload. In gut smooth muscle, the same reduction in intracellular calcium decreases propulsive contraction and prolongs transit, explaining constipation. Verapamil also inhibits CYP3A and P-glycoprotein, which explains clinically important increases in digoxin and selected statin or substrate exposure.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: IV verapamil is contraindicated in severe hypotension/cardiogenic shock, severe congestive heart failure, unpaced sick sinus syndrome or second-/third-degree AV block, ventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation/flutter with an accessory pathway, and patients receiving IV beta blockers. Give IV doses over at least two minutes with continuous ECG and pressure monitoring; use at least three minutes in older adults. IV verapamil and IV beta blockers should not be administered within a few hours because both depress AV conduction and contractility. Verapamil is not removed by hemodialysis, so severe overdose requires prolonged hemodynamic rescue rather than relying on dialysis.

Adverse Effects

  • Constipation, dizziness, headache, bradycardia, PR prolongation/AV block, hypotension, edema, nausea, fatigue, and gingival overgrowth.

  • High-grade AV block, asystole, ventricular fibrillation after rhythm misdiagnosis, cardiogenic shock, pulmonary edema, severe hepatic injury, and prolonged toxicity in overdose.
