Definition

C. difficile infection develops when toxin-producing Clostridioides difficile proliferates after microbiome disruption and injures colonic epithelium. The characteristic pattern is new clinically significant watery diarrhea with abdominal pain, leukocytosis, fever, or severe colitis after antibiotics or healthcare exposure. Confirm or stage it with test only unformed stool from a compatible symptomatic patient using the local multistep or molecular algorithm; distinguish it from laxative diarrhea, tube-feeding diarrhea, other infection, inflammatory bowel disease, and colonization by symptoms, medication timing, and appropriate testing. The priority is to stop unnecessary antibiotics and laxatives because toxic megacolon can complicate C. difficile infection. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • test only unformed stool from a compatible symptomatic patient using the local multistep or molecular algorithm

  • CBC, creatinine, albumin, lactate, and abdominal examination for severity

  • imaging and surgical assessment for ileus, megacolon, or perforation

  • Distinguish C. difficile infection from laxative diarrhea, tube-feeding diarrhea, other infection, inflammatory bowel disease, and colonization by symptoms, medication timing, and appropriate testing.

Labs

  • WBC may rise or fall

  • lactate may rise with sepsis

  • CRP/ESR may rise with inflammation

  • organ labs can worsen in severe infection

Treatments

  • stop unnecessary antibiotics and laxatives

  • give guideline-directed oral therapy for initial, severe, fulminant, or recurrent disease

  • use contact precautions, soap-and-water hand hygiene, environmental sporicidal cleaning, and early surgery for fulminant complications

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for hypotension, ileus, toxic megacolon, peritoneal signs, rising lactate, severe leukocytosis, or worsening kidney injury because surgical and critical-care evaluation may be needed.

  • Initiate contact precautions and use soap-and-water hand hygiene after care because spores persist in the environment and resist routine alcohol hand rub.

  • Administer the prescribed C. difficile antibiotic and avoid unnecessary systemic antibiotics and acid suppression because microbiome disruption promotes persistence and recurrence.

  • Measure stool frequency and character and assess abdominal pain, distention, hydration, fever, and medication exposure because severity can change rapidly.

  • Monitor white blood cell count, creatinine, electrolytes, urine output, lactate, and abdominal findings to detect dehydration, acute kidney injury, or fulminant colitis.

Complications

  • toxic megacolon can complicate C. difficile infection.

  • perforation can complicate C. difficile infection.

  • shock can complicate C. difficile infection.

  • kidney injury can complicate C. difficile infection.

  • recurrence can complicate C. difficile infection.

  • malnutrition can complicate C. difficile infection.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension, ileus, or toxic megacolon

  • Peritoneal signs or rapidly increasing distention

  • Rising lactate or severe leukocytosis

  • Worsening kidney injury or markedly decreasing urine output

Patient Education

  • Teach soap-and-water handwashing, bathroom and high-touch surface cleaning, and completion of the prescribed antibiotic course.

  • Explain that recurrent watery diarrhea, fever, severe pain, distention, or faintness after treatment requires prompt reassessment.

Nclex Traps

  • A positive molecular test in a patient without diarrhea may represent colonization

  • Alcohol hand rub does not reliably remove spores from hands
