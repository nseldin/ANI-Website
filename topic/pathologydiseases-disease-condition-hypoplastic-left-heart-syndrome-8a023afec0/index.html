Definition

Hypoplastic left heart syndrome develops when the left ventricle, mitral or aortic structures, and ascending aorta are severely underdeveloped, making systemic output dependent on fetal shunts after birth. The characteristic pattern is a newborn who becomes cyanotic, tachypneic, cool, acidotic, or poorly perfused as the ductus arteriosus closes, sometimes with weak pulses and little murmur. Confirm or stage it with prenatal or urgent postnatal echocardiography; distinguish it from persistent pulmonary hypertension, sepsis, coarctation, and other ductal-dependent lesions by echocardiographic anatomy. The priority is to maintain ductal patency with prostaglandin infusion because cardiogenic shock when the duct closes can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • prenatal or urgent postnatal echocardiography

  • preductal and postductal oxygen saturation, blood gas, lactate, perfusion, and organ-function trends

  • anatomic and hemodynamic assessment for staged palliation or transplant planning

  • Distinguish Hypoplastic left heart syndrome from persistent pulmonary hypertension, sepsis, coarctation, and other ductal-dependent lesions by echocardiographic anatomy.

Labs

  • do not apply adult ranges without checking age

  • glucose, bilirubin, CBC, and electrolytes often require age-specific interpretation

Treatments

  • maintain ductal patency with prostaglandin infusion

  • balance pulmonary and systemic blood flow while supporting ventilation and perfusion

  • proceed to staged surgical palliation, hybrid therapy, or transplantation through a congenital-heart team

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for rapidly falling perfusion, rising lactate, severe acidosis, apnea, weak pulses, or decreasing urine output because ductal restriction or imbalanced circulation can cause shock.

  • Administer prescribed prostaglandin infusion without interruption because systemic blood flow depends on keeping the ductus arteriosus open before staged palliation.

  • Monitor oxygen saturation within the congenital-cardiac target rather than trying to normalize it because excessive pulmonary flow can steal output from systemic organs.

  • Assess pulses, capillary refill, blood pressure, temperature, urine output, lactate, feeding tolerance, and mental status to detect low systemic perfusion.

  • Prepare airway and ventilation support for apnea during prostaglandin therapy because respiratory depression can occur while ductal patency remains essential.

Complications

  • cardiogenic shock when the duct closes

  • pulmonary overcirculation with systemic hypoperfusion

  • interstage thrombosis can complicate Hypoplastic left heart syndrome.

  • arrhythmia can complicate Hypoplastic left heart syndrome.

Red Flags

  • Weak pulses, rising lactate, or severe metabolic acidosis

  • Rapidly decreasing urine output or altered responsiveness

  • Apnea during prostaglandin therapy

  • Abrupt saturation or perfusion change suggesting ductal restriction

Patient Education

  • Explain that oxygen levels may have a special target because the single ventricle must balance blood flow to lungs and body.

  • Teach caregivers the interstage monitoring plan for feeding, weight, color, breathing, saturation when prescribed, and immediate cardiac-team contact triggers.

Nclex Traps

  • More oxygen is not always better because lowering pulmonary resistance can steal flow from the systemic circulation

  • A quiet murmur does not exclude a critical ductal-dependent lesion
