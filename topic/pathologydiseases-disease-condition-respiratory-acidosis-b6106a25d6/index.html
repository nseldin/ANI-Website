Pathology

PaCO2 rises when CO2 production divided by effective alveolar ventilation rises. Hypercapnia dilates cerebral vessels and can cause headache, confusion, somnolence, asterixis, and raised intracranial pressure, while acidemia impairs cardiovascular function and electrical stability. Supplemental oxygen can correct hypoxemia without removing CO2, so an acceptable SpO2 does not prove adequate ventilation. In severe asthma or obstructive disease, a PaCO2 that normalizes or rises despite intense effort can signal fatigue and impending respiratory failure rather than improvement.

Etiology

Opioids, sedatives, seizures with postictal depression, brainstem injury, severe obesity-hypoventilation, COPD, advanced asthma with fatigue, upper-airway obstruction, pneumonia or pulmonary edema with exhaustion, neuromuscular weakness, spinal cord disease, chest-wall restriction, large dead space, ventilator failure, and inadequate set minute ventilation can cause CO2 retention. Increased CO2 production from fever, overfeeding, shivering, or malignant hyperthermia becomes important when ventilation cannot rise to match it.

Signs Symptoms

  • Slow, shallow, irregular, or visibly ineffective breathing; dyspnea; headache; flushed skin; confusion; somnolence; asterixis; and reduced consciousness can occur.

  • Accessory-muscle use, inability to speak, silent chest, paradoxical movement, or falling effort suggests fatigue even if respiratory rate is not low.

  • Chronic hypercapnia may cause fewer symptoms at a given PaCO2 because renal adaptation buffers pH, but an acute rise on top of chronic disease can still be life-threatening.

Diagnostics

  • Assess airway patency, respiratory rate and depth, minute ventilation, work and fatigue, mental status, SpO2, oxygen device, lung and neurologic findings, medications, and ventilator circuit immediately.

  • Use ABG when precise PaCO2 or arterial oxygenation changes management; VBG can screen trends in selected settings but cannot supply PaO2 and PvCO2 is not identical to PaCO2.

  • Compare HCO3 with acute and chronic expectations and investigate an added metabolic process when it does not fit.

Treatments

  • Restore effective ventilation by opening and protecting the airway, reversing a specific depressant when indicated, treating bronchospasm or obstruction, clearing secretions, and using noninvasive or invasive ventilatory support according to severity and contraindications.

  • Give oxygen for hypoxemia with an individualized target and close ventilation reassessment; fear of CO2 retention is not a reason to leave true hypoxemia untreated.

  • Treat infection, edema, neuromuscular crisis, equipment failure, fever, or excess metabolic load and avoid abrupt overcorrection of long-standing hypercapnia because retained bicarbonate can cause post-hypercapnic alkalosis.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess respiratory rate and depth, airway patency, breath sounds, sedation, neuromuscular strength, chest mechanics, and mental status because alveolar hypoventilation retains carbon dioxide and lowers pH.

  • Trend pH, PaCO2, bicarbonate, oxygenation, potassium, end-tidal CO2 when reliable, and neurologic status because acute hypercapnia causes greater acidemia than chronic renal compensation at the same PaCO2.

  • Verify blood-gas timing and compare with prior baseline before labeling compensation because chronic lung or neuromuscular disease may normally carry elevated bicarbonate while a new rise signals decompensation.

  • Reverse prescribed sedative causes, clear secretions, position, and support ventilation while titrating oxygen to the ordered target because oxygen treats hypoxemia but does not correct inadequate carbon-dioxide elimination.

  • Escalate immediately for falling pH, rising PaCO2 with somnolence, shallow respirations, inability to protect the airway, severe hypoxemia, or respiratory exhaustion because ventilatory failure may require noninvasive or invasive support.

Complications

  • Hypercapnic encephalopathy, aspiration, hypoxemia, dysrhythmia, hypotension, coma, cardiac arrest, and death can occur.

  • Rapid loss of ventilation can cause a steep PaCO2 rise and pH fall because CO2 stores accumulate continuously.

  • Abrupt normalization of chronic PaCO2 can produce severe metabolic alkalosis, seizures, dysrhythmia, and difficult ventilator liberation.

Patient Education

  • Explain that respiratory acidosis results from inadequate ventilation rather than simply low oxygen, so effective breathing support must remove retained carbon dioxide.

  • Teach patients using opioids, sedatives, or home ventilation to seek urgent help for unusual sleepiness, slowed breathing, morning headaches, or new confusion.

Nclex Traps

  • PaCO2 reflects ventilation; PaO2 and SpO2 reflect oxygenation.

  • A normal SpO2 on supplemental oxygen can coexist with dangerous hypercapnia.

  • In severe asthma, a rising or normalizing PaCO2 can mean fatigue and failure.

Definition

Respiratory acidosis is a primary rise in PaCO2 caused by alveolar hypoventilation—ventilation that is inadequate for carbon dioxide production. The retained CO2 increases dissolved carbonic acid and lowers pH by lowering the bicarbonate-to-CO2 ratio. Acute disease usually causes acidemia because kidneys have had little time to respond; chronic disease can have a near-normal pH because renal bicarbonate retention has developed, but the ventilatory disorder remains present.

Pathophysiology

CO2 rapidly crosses membranes and combines with water, so acute PaCO2 elevation lowers pH before renal adaptation. As a screening estimate, acute respiratory acidosis raises HCO3 about 1 mEq/L and lowers pH about 0.08 for every 10-mm Hg PaCO2 rise above 40. Over several days, increased bicarbonate reclamation and ammonium excretion produce chronic adaptation: HCO3 rises about 3.5-4 and pH falls about 0.03 per 10-mm Hg. More bicarbonate than expected suggests concurrent metabolic alkalosis; less suggests metabolic acidosis. These are approximate screening relationships, not ventilator or treatment targets.

Risk Factors

  • Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, gabapentinoids, anesthetics, and combined CNS depressants reduce respiratory drive and airway protection.

  • COPD, severe asthma, sleep-disordered breathing, obesity hypoventilation, kyphoscoliosis, neuromuscular disease, and airway obstruction reduce ventilatory reserve.

  • Ventilator disconnection, tube obstruction, excessive dead space, low minute ventilation, fatigue, fever, and high carbohydrate load can raise PaCO2.

Labs

  • Primary pattern is high PaCO2 with low pH in acute disease; chronic adaptation raises HCO3 and can bring pH toward the reference interval.

  • Potassium, chloride, chemistry bicarbonate, renal function, glucose, toxicology, infection studies, and imaging identify contributors and mixed disorders.

  • Capnography trends exhaled CO2 and ventilation but can diverge from PaCO2 with dead space, poor perfusion, or sampling problems.

Contraindications

  • Do not give sedatives simply to slow agitation until hypoxemia, hypercapnia, pain, withdrawal, delirium, and airway danger are assessed.

  • Do not use SpO2 as a ventilation measurement or assume oxygen alone treats CO2 retention.

  • Do not force PaCO2 immediately to 40 in chronic hypercapnia without a deliberate specialist plan.

Red Flags

  • Rising PaCO2 with somnolence, confusion, or inability to protect the airway

  • pH below 7.20 or rapidly worsening acidemia

  • Bradypnea, apnea, weak cough, or severe respiratory-muscle fatigue

  • Hypoxemia below target despite oxygen or signs of impending arrest
