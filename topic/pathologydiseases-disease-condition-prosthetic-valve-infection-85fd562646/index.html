Definition

Prosthetic valve infection develops when microorganisms adhere to prosthetic material and form vegetations or perivalvular infection that can destroy tissue and embolize. The characteristic pattern is persistent fever, new regurgitation, heart failure, conduction delay, embolic findings, or bacteremia in a person with a prosthetic valve. Confirm or stage it with multiple blood-culture sets before antibiotics when stable; distinguish it from sterile thrombus, valve thrombosis, pannus, noncardiac infection, and culture-negative endocarditis by cultures, imaging, serology, and prosthetic function. The priority is to start organism- and prosthesis-appropriate intravenous antibiotics after cultures because valve dehiscence can complicate Prosthetic valve infection. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • multiple blood-culture sets before antibiotics when stable

  • transesophageal echocardiography with repeat or advanced imaging when suspicion remains

  • ECG, CBC, inflammatory, kidney, urine, neurologic, and embolic assessment

  • Distinguish Prosthetic valve infection from sterile thrombus, valve thrombosis, pannus, noncardiac infection, and culture-negative endocarditis by cultures, imaging, serology, and prosthetic function.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • start organism- and prosthesis-appropriate intravenous antibiotics after cultures

  • obtain early endocarditis surgical-team review

  • remove infected material for heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess, dehiscence, or major embolic risk when indicated

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for pulmonary edema, shock, new heart block, persistent bacteremia, prosthetic dehiscence, focal neurologic deficit, acute limb ischemia, severe abdominal pain, or recurrent emboli because invasive infection can cause catastrophic valve failure or abscess.

  • Obtain ordered blood-culture sets from separate sites before antimicrobials when the patient is stable enough because organism identification is crucial and prior antibiotics can sterilize cultures without curing prosthetic infection.

  • Assess fever, rigors, new murmur, heart-failure findings, prosthetic click change, embolic skin findings, neurologic status, abdominal pain, limb perfusion, and recent dental, device, or bloodstream exposure because prosthetic infection can destroy tissue and embolize widely.

  • Administer organism-directed intravenous therapy exactly on schedule and monitor cultures, blood counts, kidney and liver function, drug levels when ordered, and vascular-access sites because prolonged combination therapy can be toxic while missed exposure permits persistent biofilm infection.

  • Maintain rhythm monitoring and coordinate transthoracic, transesophageal, and adjunct imaging because new conduction delay, paravalvular leak, abscess, dehiscence, or obstruction often requires surgery rather than antibiotics alone.

Complications

  • valve dehiscence can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • abscess can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • heart block can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • heart failure can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • stroke can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • systemic emboli can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • mycotic aneurysm can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

  • sepsis can complicate Prosthetic valve infection.

Red Flags

  • Acute pulmonary edema, hypotension, or rapidly worsening valve dysfunction

  • New PR prolongation, atrioventricular block, or unstable rhythm

  • Persistent positive cultures or fever despite appropriate therapy

  • Focal neurologic deficit, acute limb ischemia, or severe splenic-type pain

Patient Education

  • Teach completion of the entire antimicrobial course and meticulous line care because symptoms may improve before prosthetic biofilm infection is eradicated.

  • Explain oral hygiene, follow-up cultures and imaging, and the clinician-directed dental prophylaxis plan; prophylactic antibiotics are reserved for specific procedures and risks rather than taken casually.

Nclex Traps

  • A negative initial echocardiogram does not exclude prosthetic-valve endocarditis

  • New atrioventricular block can signal a perivalvular abscess
