Definition

Raynaud phenomenon develops when exaggerated digital vasoconstriction causes episodic ischemia, either idiopathically or from connective-tissue, vascular, occupational, or medication causes. The characteristic pattern is cold- or stress-triggered sharply demarcated white-blue-red finger color change with numbness or pain. Confirm or stage it with history and examination including symmetry, pulses, ulcers, and triggers; distinguish it from acrocyanosis, embolism, thoracic outlet disease, vasculitis, erythromelalgia, and peripheral artery disease by episodic pattern, pulses, warmth response, and tissue injury. Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • history and examination including symmetry, pulses, ulcers, and triggers

  • nailfold capillaroscopy and autoimmune testing when secondary disease is suspected

  • vascular studies when fixed obstruction, tissue loss, or asymmetric findings are present

  • Distinguish Raynaud phenomenon from acrocyanosis, embolism, thoracic outlet disease, vasculitis, erythromelalgia, and peripheral artery disease by episodic pattern, pulses, warmth response, and tissue injury.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • avoid cold, nicotine, vibration, and provoking medicines

  • use a vasodilator such as a calcium-channel blocker when symptoms remain significant

  • urgently treat critical ischemia and the underlying secondary disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate urgently for an attack that does not resolve with warming, severe rest pain, a new ulcer, black tissue, absent pulse, spreading erythema, purulent drainage, or fever because critical ischemia or infection threatens the digit.

  • Document cold- or stress-triggered color sequence, symmetry, duration, pain, numbness, medicines, occupational vibration, and autoimmune symptoms because asymmetric, prolonged, painful attacks with tissue injury suggest secondary rather than uncomplicated primary Raynaud phenomenon.

  • Inspect fingers and toes for temperature, capillary refill, pulses, sensation, ulcers, fissures, infection, and necrosis because repeated vasospasm can progress from reversible ischemia to permanent tissue loss in severe secondary disease.

  • Move the patient to warmth, remove wet or constrictive items, and warm the affected digits gradually without direct high heat because gentle rewarming restores flow while numb skin is vulnerable to thermal injury.

  • Administer prescribed vasodilator therapy and monitor blood pressure, dizziness, edema, headache, attack frequency, and wound healing because calcium-channel blockers or other agents may reduce spasm but can cause hypotension.

Complications

  • digital ulcer can complicate Raynaud phenomenon.

  • infection can complicate Raynaud phenomenon.

  • gangrene can complicate Raynaud phenomenon.

  • amputation can complicate Raynaud phenomenon.

  • medication-related hypotension can complicate Raynaud phenomenon.

  • missed systemic sclerosis or vascular occlusion

Red Flags

  • Persistent white or blue digit with severe pain despite gradual warming

  • New ulcer, black tissue, blistering, or rapidly spreading discoloration

  • Absent pulse, cool insensate digit, or sudden loss of movement

  • Spreading erythema, purulent drainage, fever, chills, or systemic illness

Patient Education

  • Teach layered warmth for the whole body, gloves before cold exposure, stress-management strategies, and avoidance of nicotine because each reduces reflex peripheral vasoconstriction.

  • Tell the patient to photograph prolonged attacks and report new sores or asymmetric symptoms because those changes can reveal secondary autoimmune or vascular disease early.

Nclex Traps

  • Primary Raynaud phenomenon should not cause tissue loss

  • A painful persistently pale finger with a weak pulse is not routine reversible vasospasm
