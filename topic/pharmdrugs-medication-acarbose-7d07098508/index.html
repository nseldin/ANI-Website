Contraindications

  • Cirrhosis

  • Inflammatory bowel disease, colonic ulceration, intestinal obstruction/predisposition, or chronic intestinal disease with marked digestion/absorption disorder

  • DKA; significant renal dysfunction under labeling

Nursing Essentials

  • Give with the first bite; taking it after carbohydrate has already been digested loses the target window.

  • Ensure the patient carries dextrose/glucose tablets or gel, not only candy/table sugar, when combined therapy can cause hypoglycemia.

Interactions

  • Insulin and sulfonylureas add hypoglycemia and may need reduction.

  • Digestive-enzyme products and intestinal adsorbents can reduce acarbose efficacy.

  • Digoxin exposure can change; monitor if clinically relevant.

Key Labs

  • Postmeal glucose and A1c

  • AST/ALT according to labeling, especially during the first year or higher doses

  • GI pattern, meal carbohydrate, renal function, and hypoglycemia plan

Nclex Traps

  • Treat a low with dextrose, not sucrose, because alpha-glucosidase blockade delays table-sugar breakdown.

  • Acarbose must arrive with the first bite.

  • Gas is explained by fermentation, but severe focal pain, obstruction signs, or liver injury requires evaluation.

Class

Intestinal alpha-glucosidase and pancreatic alpha-amylase inhibitor

Used To Treat

Adjunct to nutrition and activity for type 2 diabetes, especially postmeal glucose excursions. It is taken with the first bite of each main meal because its target is carbohydrate digestion in the intestinal lumen. It does not treat type 1 diabetes or DKA.

Mechanism

Acarbose is a competitive digestive-enzyme inhibitor. It inhibits the pancreatic alpha-amylase enzyme, which cuts starch into oligosaccharides, and membrane-bound intestinal alpha-glucosidase enzymes, which create absorbable glucose from oligosaccharides and disaccharides. Delayed hydrolysis spreads carbohydrate absorption across a longer interval and lowers the early postprandial peak without causing malabsorption of pure glucose. Bacterial fermentation of carbohydrate delivered to the colon generates hydrogen, methane, short-chain fatty acids, gas, and osmotic water. The mechanism explains first-bite timing and why oral D-glucose/dextrose works for rescue while table sugar, sucrose, is too slow.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Acarbose can cause dose-related aminotransferase elevation and rare severe hepatic injury; monitor liver tests under the dose/interval in labeling. It is contraindicated in cirrhosis, inflammatory bowel disease, colonic ulceration, obstruction/predisposition, and major digestion/absorption disorders. Severe abdominal symptoms are not automatically harmless gas.

Description

Acarbose is a minimally absorbed carbohydrate-digestion inhibitor that delays the postmeal glucose rise. It blocks intestinal brush-border alpha-glucosidases and pancreatic alpha-amylase, so complex starches and sucrose are broken into absorbable monosaccharides more slowly. Undigested carbohydrate reaches colonic bacteria, which explains flatulence, abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea. Used alone it does not force insulin and rarely causes hypoglycemia; if insulin or a secretagogue causes a low, use dextrose rather than sucrose because acarbose is blocking sucrose cleavage.

Adverse Effects

  • Flatulence, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and borborygmi from colonic fermentation

  • Dose-related AST/ALT elevation and rare jaundice/hepatitis

  • Hypoglycemia only when combined with insulin/secretagogues, but sucrose rescue may fail or be delayed
