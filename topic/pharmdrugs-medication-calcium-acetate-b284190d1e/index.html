Contraindications

  • Existing hypercalcemia; clarify unexplained elevated calcium, severe constipation or ileus, calcification burden, and concurrent calcium/vitamin D stacking.

  • Do not use as a calcium replacement product or take away from meals as though timing were optional; the indication is binding dietary phosphate in ESRD.

Nursing Essentials

  • Give with each phosphate-containing meal and titrate to phosphorus response only while calcium remains safe. Record skipped meals, enteral feeds, snacks, and binder timing because they explain apparent treatment failure.

  • Teach symptoms of hypercalcemia and constipation, review every calcium or vitamin-D source, and build a written separation schedule for interacting oral drugs.

Interactions

  • Separate clinically important oral drugs that bind polyvalent cations, including tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, levothyroxine, iron, and bisphosphonates, using the current label or pharmacist plan.

  • Hypercalcemia magnifies digoxin-related dysrhythmia; vitamin D, calcium supplements, calcium antacids, and high-calcium dialysate can add to the calcium load.

Key Labs

  • Serum phosphorus and calcium, calcium-phosphate burden according to renal protocol, PTH and vitamin D context, renal/dialysis status, and ECG or digoxin assessment when symptomatic.

  • Trend bowel function, mental status, appetite, weakness, meal adherence, binder timing, and signs of vascular or soft-tissue calcification.

Nclex Traps

  • Calcium acetate binds phosphate in the gut; it is not an electrolyte replacement and does not remove already absorbed phosphate directly.

  • The meal instruction has a mechanism: no dietary phosphate in the lumen means little useful phosphate binding.

Description

Calcium acetate is an oral phosphate binder for end-stage renal disease, the dialysis-dependent final stage of chronic kidney disease, that acts locally in the GI tract to bind dietary phosphate into poorly absorbed calcium-phosphate complexes excreted in stool. After the salt dissociates, released calcium and food phosphate exceed their local solubility and precipitate instead of being absorbed. This is why it must be taken with meals: a dose taken when no dietary phosphate is present has little useful substrate to capture. It lowers serum phosphorus over time but adds an absorbable calcium load. Too much calcium can cause constipation, nausea, confusion, arrhythmia, vascular or soft-tissue calcification, and greater digoxin toxicity. The therapeutic balance is therefore phosphate reduction without hypercalcemia, not simply giving more capsules until phosphorus falls.

Used To Treat

Hyperphosphatemia in adults with end-stage renal disease, the dialysis-dependent final stage of chronic kidney disease, usually alongside dietary phosphate management. It lowers absorption of phosphate eaten with the dose; it does not repair kidney filtration.

Mechanism

In the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, calcium acetate dissociates and provides ionic calcium that binds phosphate entering from food. Calcium and phosphate exceed their local solubility product and precipitate as poorly absorbed salts that leave in stool, reducing net phosphate absorption. Some calcium is absorbed systemically, so vitamin D therapy, calcium supplements, antacids, dialysate calcium, and bone turnover change the hypercalcemia risk. Chronic high calcium-phosphate burden promotes vascular smooth-muscle osteogenic signaling and tissue calcification. Calcium also chelates or adsorbs selected oral drugs, reducing their bioavailability; separation is therefore a pharmacokinetic necessity, not a convenience instruction.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Hypercalcemia is the label contraindication and principal hazard. During dose titration, monitor calcium frequently and reduce or interrupt calcium acetate and contributing vitamin D/calcium if calcium rises. Severe hypercalcemia can cause delirium, coma, and dysrhythmia and may require hemodialysis. Hypercalcemia increases digoxin toxicity because excess extracellular calcium promotes triggered cardiac activity. Avoid unreviewed calcium supplements and calcium-containing antacids.

Adverse Effects

  • Constipation, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, abdominal discomfort, and mild asymptomatic hypercalcemia.

  • Severe hypercalcemia with confusion, weakness, ileus, arrhythmia, coma, digoxin toxicity, and chronic vascular or soft-tissue calcification.
