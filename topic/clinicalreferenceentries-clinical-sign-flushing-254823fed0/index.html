Definition

Flushing is a clinician-observed episode in which the skin, most often on the face, neck, ears, or upper chest, visibly changes from baseline and feels warm because surface blood flow increases. A reported feeling of sudden warmth is a symptom; the visible color and warmth change is the sign.

Plain Language

Flushing means the skin suddenly changes color and feels warmer because more blood is moving through vessels near the surface.

Why It Matters

Flushing is often caused by heat, emotion, menopause, alcohol, or medication, but the trigger and accompanying findings matter. Sudden generalized flushing with hives, swelling, breathing difficulty, low blood pressure, or rapid progression can be anaphylaxis and requires emergency treatment.

Assessment Method

Inspect the face, neck, ears, and upper trunk in neutral light while comparing with baseline; palpate for warmth and ask about onset, duration, triggers, recurrence, sweating, itching, hives, swelling, breathing difficulty, wheeze, diarrhea, headache, palpitations, medications, alcohol, and exposures, then assess vital signs and perfusion when the episode is acute.

Positive Finding

A transient red, pink, dusky, or otherwise visibly changed area develops with warmth, usually over the face, neck, ears, or upper chest; the patient may report a sudden heat sensation and sweating may be present or absent.

Mechanism

Autonomic signals or vasoactive mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, or serotonin dilate superficial skin vessels, increasing local blood flow and heat; thermoregulation, emotion, substances, medications, and disease can activate different pathways.

Evidence Limitations

Visibility varies with baseline skin tone, lighting, makeup, room temperature, and whether the episode has resolved; warmth can be reported without visible color change, and flushing alone neither identifies its mediator nor diagnoses anaphylaxis, a neuroendocrine tumor, or another cause. Therefore, Flushing cannot confirm or exclude increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from physiologic heat or emotion, autonomic activation, menopause, alcohol, medications, anaphylaxis, mast-cell mediators, or neuroendocrine disorders by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as fever-related color change, rosacea, sunburn, contact dermatitis, facial plethora.

False Negative Situations

Flushing may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from physiologic heat or emotion, autonomic activation, menopause, alcohol, medications, anaphylaxis, mast-cell mediators, or neuroendocrine disorders.
