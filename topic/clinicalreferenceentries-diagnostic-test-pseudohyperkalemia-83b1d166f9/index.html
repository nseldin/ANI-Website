Summary

Pseudohyperkalemia is a falsely high measured potassium caused by potassium leaving blood cells after the sample has been collected; the patient's circulating potassium is not actually elevated by that artifact. It is a preanalytic or in vitro problem, not a harmless form of true hyperkalemia. Recognition matters in both directions: unnecessary potassium-lowering treatment can cause dangerous hypokalemia, while assuming every unexpected result is an artifact can delay treatment of genuine hyperkalemia. The safe approach joins the result to symptoms, ECG, kidney function, medications, cell counts, the laboratory's hemolysis information, collection and transport history, and a promptly obtained comparison specimen when the patient is stable enough for confirmation.

Why It Matters

A pseudohyperkalemia result suggests post-collection potassium release in the specimen, commonly from hemolysis or another collection or handling artifact; thrombocytosis or fragile leukocytes can also produce matrix-dependent patterns. Treating an artifact can cause dangerous hypokalemia, but dismissing true hyperkalemia can miss cardiac danger. Reconcile the result with symptoms, ECG, kidney and medication context, and specimen quality; obtain laboratory-directed repeat testing when the patient is stable, and never delay emergency hyperkalemia care for instability or compatible ECG changes.

Quick Answer

First decide whether the patient may be in immediate danger. Weakness, paralysis, hemodynamic instability, a rapidly rising potassium, major renal or tissue injury, or ECG changes compatible with hyperkalemia require emergency stabilization according to protocol; do not postpone membrane stabilization while debating specimen quality. When the patient is stable and the result conflicts with the clinical picture, inspect the hemolysis flag and collection conditions, review platelet and white-cell counts, and repeat potassium using a deliberately atraumatic collection and rapid processing plan developed with the laboratory. Serum, plasma, and promptly analyzed whole blood are different specimen contexts, so compare results only after identifying the matrix and method. Classic pseudohyperkalemia may produce a high serum value with a more representative plasma or whole-blood value. Reverse pseudohyperkalemia, usually discussed with fragile leukocytes, can produce a falsely high plasma result while serum or carefully handled whole blood is lower. No single comparison rule is infallible.

Sections

  • Label

    Definition and specimen context

    Text

    Potassium is concentrated inside red cells, platelets, and white cells. A chemistry analyzer measures the potassium present in the submitted liquid after collection and processing; it cannot automatically know how much potassium was circulating in the patient and how much leaked from cells in the tube. Serum is the fluid remaining after blood clots. Plasma is separated from anticoagulated blood and retains clotting proteins. Whole-blood testing, often on a blood-gas or point-of-care analyzer, measures an unseparated sample quickly. Because clotting, anticoagulants, delay, transport, centrifugation, and analyzer method differ, results from these matrices are related but not interchangeable. Pseudohyperkalemia names a clinically important mismatch: the reported value is high because of events outside the body while the in vivo potassium is lower.

  • Label

    Mechanism: how potassium becomes falsely elevated

    Text

    Cell membranes normally preserve a steep potassium gradient. Mechanical injury, osmotic stress, clotting-related platelet activation, temperature extremes, or fragile abnormal blood cells can disrupt those membranes after venipuncture. Potassium then moves from the high-concentration intracellular space into the surrounding serum or plasma. Even a modest amount of cell rupture can noticeably raise the measured concentration because extracellular potassium is normally much lower. This is why visible or instrument-detected hemolysis is such an important clue. The artifact may occur during collection, transport, storage, or separation rather than at the bedside. In contrast, true hyperkalemia reflects impaired renal excretion, movement of potassium out of cells within the body, excessive load in a susceptible patient, or tissue breakdown; it affects the circulating extracellular compartment and can change cardiac excitability.

  • Label

    Collection and handling causes

    Text

    Common contributors include a difficult or traumatic venipuncture, very small needles, forceful syringe transfer, vigorous tube shaking, drawing through a problematic intravenous catheter, prolonged tourniquet time, repeated fist clenching, underfilled or incorrectly filled tubes, delayed separation, inappropriate temperature, and rough or prolonged transport. Contamination is another mechanism: potassium-containing IV fluid or potassium-EDTA from the wrong tube sequence can create a high result that is not simple hemolysis. A pneumatic tube may damage exceptionally fragile leukemic cells even when it causes no problem for most samples. The corrective plan should target the suspected cause—fresh peripheral draw away from infusions, minimal tourniquet and fist activity, correct tube and fill, gentle mixing, rapid hand delivery when indicated, and prompt analysis—not merely repeat the same flawed process.

  • Label

    Thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, and reverse pseudohyperkalemia

    Text

    During clot formation, platelets release potassium. Marked thrombocytosis can therefore raise serum potassium more than plasma potassium, while the patient's circulating value remains closer to the plasma result. Marked leukocytosis can create artifacts because fragile white cells rupture with handling, delay, centrifugation, or transport. The direction is not guaranteed. Reverse pseudohyperkalemia describes the pattern in which plasma potassium is falsely higher than serum, reported particularly with fragile leukemic cells; heparin exposure and mechanical transport may contribute. A rapidly analyzed, carefully collected whole-blood sample can help, but whole-blood analyzers may not report a hemolysis index and are not immune to collection error. Cell counts create suspicion, not a diagnosis, and patients with hematologic malignancy can also develop real hyperkalemia from kidney injury or tumor lysis.

  • Label

    Assessment: artifact versus true hyperkalemia

    Text

    Reconcile four streams of evidence. First, assess symptoms and circulation: weakness, ascending paralysis, palpitations, syncope, hypotension, or shock increase concern, although true hyperkalemia can initially be silent. Second, obtain and interpret an ECG promptly when the elevation is significant or clinically concerning; peaked T waves, conduction slowing, loss of P waves, QRS widening, or a sine-wave pattern support electrical danger, but a normal ECG does not prove potassium is safe. Third, review prior and serial potassium, creatinine, urine output, acid-base status, glucose, tissue injury, and medications that alter potassium. Fourth, inspect hemolysis information, platelet and leukocyte counts, infusion site, tube type, transport, and processing. Concordance supports a conclusion; discordance requires deliberate confirmation and continued surveillance.

  • Label

    Diagnostic workflow and confirmation

    Text

    Notify the responsible clinician and laboratory when a clinically important result appears inconsistent. Confirm the specimen matrix, analyzer method, hemolysis index or comment, collection site, and whether an infusion was running. If the patient has dangerous clinical or ECG features, begin emergency care and redraw concurrently. If stable, obtain a fresh specimen under controlled conditions, avoiding fist pumping and traumatic handling. The laboratory may recommend paired serum and heparinized plasma, or immediate whole-blood analysis with rapid hand transport, based on thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, and local equipment. Compare samples drawn close in time because real potassium can change. Review CBC and renal/metabolic context. A repeated high, nonhemolyzed value with a compatible cause should be treated as true until a qualified clinical-laboratory evaluation shows otherwise.

  • Label

    Interpretation, limitations, and false reassurance

    Text

    A hemolysis flag increases suspicion but does not quantify the patient's true potassium and does not exclude simultaneous genuine hyperkalemia. Absence of a flag also does not exclude platelet- or leukocyte-related release, contamination, or cell injury that the analyzer cannot detect. A normal ECG cannot safely rule out hyperkalemia, and ECG changes are not perfectly ordered by potassium level. Serum commonly runs slightly higher than plasma because clotting releases platelet potassium; a difference becomes meaningful only in laboratory and clinical context rather than by memorizing one universal cutoff. A normal whole-blood result can be persuasive when collection and analysis are sound, but point-of-care devices may not identify hemolysis. Treatment itself changes later comparisons. Document exactly which specimen and method produced each result so an apparent trend is not a matrix artifact.

  • Label

    Emergency safety: when confirmation must not delay care

    Text

    Pseudohyperkalemia is a diagnostic consideration, not permission to wait through instability. Never delay emergency stabilization when clinical or ECG danger exists. If the patient has a malignant dysrhythmia, severe compatible ECG changes, profound weakness, rapidly worsening renal failure, major tissue destruction, or another high-risk clinical picture, follow the emergency hyperkalemia pathway while obtaining a reliable repeat. Intravenous calcium stabilizes the cardiac membrane but does not remove potassium; insulin with glucose and selected other measures shift potassium intracellularly; elimination strategies remove it. Those distinctions matter because treating an artifact aggressively can overshoot into hypokalemia, hypoglycemia, fluid or acid-base harm, or delayed care for the real problem. Reassess ECG, glucose, potassium, and symptoms after intervention. Clinical urgency should be based on the whole risk picture, not either the number or the artifact hypothesis alone.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Verify the result was acknowledged, place the patient on monitoring when indicated, obtain a 12-lead ECG promptly, and assess weakness, sensation, rhythm, perfusion, renal history, urine output, diabetes, acidosis, burns or crush injury, recent chemotherapy, transfusion, and potassium-altering medications. Check whether blood was drawn near a potassium-containing infusion and whether the laboratory reported hemolysis. For a repeat, use the ordered tube and a clean peripheral collection when possible, limit tourniquet time, avoid repetitive fist clenching, mix rather than shake, label the matrix correctly, and arrange the laboratory-recommended transport. If potassium-lowering therapy is given, monitor glucose and repeat studies at the prescribed times because intracellular shifts can wear off and treatment can produce hypokalemia. Never independently cancel urgent therapy solely because a sample looked difficult.

  • Label

    Documentation and escalation

    Text

    Record the original potassium with time, specimen type, collection site, hemolysis indicator, relevant ECG findings, symptoms, vital signs, kidney function, urine output, platelet and white-cell counts, infusions, medications, who was notified, orders received, treatment, and repeat values. Label paired results as serum, plasma, or whole blood rather than writing only 'repeat potassium.' Escalate immediately for ECG change, dysrhythmia, instability, severe or rapidly increasing result, oliguria, major tissue injury, or new neuromuscular symptoms. Also escalate a persistent mismatch so the laboratory can recommend handling for subsequent specimens. A durable chart note about leukocyte fragility or matrix-dependent artifact can prevent repeated harm, but it must not become an automatic dismissal of future high results.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and clinical reasoning traps

    Text

    Pseudohyperkalemia does not mean mild hyperkalemia, and it is not corrected by simply subtracting a standard amount. Hemolyzed does not mean useless: it is a warning that demands contextual review and usually recollection, while other analytes may also be affected. A patient may have both artifact and true hyperkalemia. A normal ECG does not exclude dangerous potassium, and an abnormal ECG can have another cause. Serum is not inherently more accurate than plasma, nor is bedside whole blood always definitive. Thrombocytosis most often influences serum through clotting; fragile leukocytes can create several patterns, including reverse pseudohyperkalemia. The safest reasoning avoids two symmetric errors—treating every number without question and dismissing every surprising number as laboratory error.
