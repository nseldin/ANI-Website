Definition

Pulmonary hypertension develops when pulmonary vascular resistance rises from arterial remodeling, left-heart pressure, lung disease, thromboembolism, or mixed mechanisms, straining the right ventricle. The characteristic pattern is progressive exertional dyspnea, fatigue, syncope, chest pressure, loud pulmonic closure, edema, and right-heart signs. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for probability and right-heart effects; distinguish it from left-heart failure, chronic lung disease, deconditioning, anemia, and chronic thromboembolic disease by invasive hemodynamics and cause testing. The priority is to treat the underlying group-specific cause because right-ventricular failure can complicate Pulmonary hypertension. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for probability and right-heart effects

  • right-heart catheterization to confirm hemodynamics and classify pre- versus postcapillary disease

  • cause workup with lung testing, imaging, sleep, thromboembolic, autoimmune, hepatic, and cardiac assessment

  • Distinguish Pulmonary hypertension from left-heart failure, chronic lung disease, deconditioning, anemia, and chronic thromboembolic disease by invasive hemodynamics and cause testing.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • treat the underlying group-specific cause

  • use pulmonary vasodilator therapy only when classification supports it

  • manage oxygenation, volume, exercise, pregnancy risk, and transplantation referral through a specialty team

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for syncope, hypotension, new arrhythmia, rapidly increasing edema, chest pain, severe hypoxemia, or oliguria because pulmonary hypertensive crisis or right-ventricular failure may be developing.

  • Assess dyspnea, syncope, chest pain, edema, jugular venous pressure, and activity tolerance because worsening pulmonary vascular resistance strains the right ventricle.

  • Administer prescribed pulmonary vasodilator and oxygen therapy without interruption because abrupt withdrawal or hypoxemia can trigger decompensation.

  • Balance fluid and diuretic therapy while monitoring blood pressure and kidney function because both volume overload and excessive preload reduction can impair right-heart output.

  • Monitor oxygen saturation, rhythm, weight, urine output, perfusion, electrolytes, and signs of right-sided failure to detect progression.

Complications

  • right-ventricular failure can complicate Pulmonary hypertension.

  • syncope can complicate Pulmonary hypertension.

  • arrhythmia can complicate Pulmonary hypertension.

  • sudden death can complicate Pulmonary hypertension.

  • hemoptysis and thromboembolism can complicate Pulmonary hypertension.

  • hypotension or pulmonary edema from inappropriate vasodilator use

Red Flags

  • Syncope, hypotension, or rapidly declining perfusion

  • Severe hypoxemia or escalating respiratory support

  • New arrhythmia or ischemic chest pain

  • Rapidly worsening edema, ascites, or oliguria

Patient Education

  • Teach strict adherence to pulmonary hypertension medicines and specialist follow-up because sudden interruption can cause rapid deterioration.

  • Review individualized oxygen, activity, pregnancy, altitude, and fluid guidance and the symptoms that require emergency care.

Nclex Traps

  • Pulmonary hypertension is not one disease and therapies are not interchangeable across groups

  • Do not diagnose or classify it from an estimated echo pressure alone
