Definition

Migraine is a recurrent neurologic disorder in which the brain becomes temporarily hypersensitive to internal and external signals. Head pain is common but is only one part of an attack; nausea, light or sound sensitivity, aura, fatigue, cognitive slowing, and post-attack symptoms may be equally disabling.

Pathology

Routine brain imaging is usually structurally normal because migraine is primarily a disorder of neural signaling rather than a mass, infection, or fixed vascular blockage. Aura reflects a propagating wave of altered cortical activity, while pain is generated through trigeminal sensory pathways and the meninges rather than injury to brain tissue itself.

Pathophysiology

  • Inherited susceptibility alters sensory processing, brainstem and hypothalamic networks, and the threshold for trigeminovascular activation. During an attack, trigeminal afferents release mediators including calcitonin gene-related peptide, which amplify meningeal nociception and central sensitization.

  • Cortical spreading depolarization is the leading mechanism for typical aura. These mechanisms explain why movement, light, sound, smell, and touch can become painful and why an attack may continue after the original trigger has ended.

Etiology

Migraine is multifactorial, with substantial genetic susceptibility and variable environmental or physiologic triggers. Hormonal fluctuation, sleep disruption, stress shifts, dehydration, skipped meals, illness, and selected sensory exposures can lower an individual attack threshold; they trigger attacks but do not by themselves create the underlying disorder. Some uncommon migraine syndromes are monogenic, but most migraine is not explained by one gene or one blood-vessel abnormality.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of migraine

  • Female sex and reproductive-hormone fluctuation after puberty

  • Sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, epilepsy, and other conditions that can increase attack burden

  • Frequent use of acute headache medicines, which can transform episodic migraine into medication-overuse headache

Signs Symptoms

  • Moderate or severe headache that may be throbbing, unilateral or bilateral, and worsened by ordinary activity

  • Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, osmophobia, neck discomfort, and impaired concentration

  • Reversible visual, sensory, speech-language, or less commonly motor aura that usually evolves over minutes rather than reaching maximum intensity instantly

  • Premonitory symptoms such as yawning, mood change, food craving, fatigue, or neck stiffness and a postdrome with exhaustion or cognitive fog

  • Some attacks produce aura, nausea, dizziness, or sensory symptoms with little or no head pain

Diagnostics

  • Diagnosis is clinical and uses the attack pattern, neurologic examination, and International Classification of Headache Disorders criteria; a headache diary helps establish duration, associated features, disability, triggers, and medication use.

  • Typical recurrent migraine with a normal neurologic examination does not automatically require imaging. MRI, vascular imaging, lumbar puncture, or other testing is selected when onset, examination, age, systemic illness, pregnancy, trauma, immune status, or a changing pattern suggests a secondary cause.

  • Aura must be distinguished from transient ischemia, seizure, retinal disease, and other focal neurologic disorders. Gradual spread and fully reversible positive symptoms support aura, but a first, prolonged, atypical, or motor event still needs urgent assessment.

Labs

  • There is no blood test or biomarker that confirms migraine.

  • Pregnancy testing, inflammatory markers, metabolic studies, toxicology, infection testing, or cerebrospinal-fluid studies are ordered only when the history raises a specific secondary diagnosis.

  • Normal tests do not make a dangerous new headache benign when red-flag features remain present.

Assessment

Assess onset and time to peak, similarity to established attacks, headache location and quality, aura evolution, associated nausea and sensory sensitivity, medication use, pregnancy status, vital signs, and a complete neurologic examination. The priority is to recognize a secondary headache before treating a familiar symptom pattern; thunderclap onset, fever, meningismus, papilledema, trauma, focal deficit, persistent altered mental status, or a major pattern change redirects the evaluation.

Differential

Differentiate migraine from tension-type and cluster headache, medication-overuse headache, seizure, and intracranial pressure disorders. Immediately consider subarachnoid hemorrhage, cervical artery dissection, cerebral venous thrombosis, stroke, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, meningitis or encephalitis, giant cell arteritis, acute angle-closure glaucoma, intracranial mass, and preeclampsia when the context or red flags fit.

Treatments

  • Acute treatment is taken early in the attack and may include acetaminophen or a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, a triptan, a gepant, a ditan, and an antiemetic according to comorbidity, pregnancy status, and prior response.

  • Preventive therapy is considered when attacks are frequent, prolonged, disabling, poorly controlled, or acute medicines are unsafe. Options include selected antihypertensives, antiseizure medicines, antidepressants, calcitonin gene-related peptide pathway therapies, and onabotulinumtoxinA for eligible chronic migraine.

  • Regular sleep, hydration, meals, physical activity, stress management, and an individualized trigger plan raise the attack threshold. Behavioral therapy and selected neuromodulation devices can complement medication.

  • Emergency treatment prioritizes exclusion of secondary causes, hydration and antiemesis when needed, and non-opioid migraine-directed therapy.

Contraindications

  • Triptans and ergot derivatives are generally avoided in significant ischemic vascular disease, prior stroke, or uncontrolled hypertension; product-specific screening is required.

  • Valproate is contraindicated for migraine prevention during pregnancy and in a person who may become pregnant but is not using effective contraception because fetal harm can occur early in gestation; use current product labeling and specialist reproductive-risk counseling.

  • Combined hormonal contraceptives are U.S. MEC category 4 in migraine with aura, meaning the method should not be used because the health risk is unacceptable; confirm the headache subtype and use individualized contraceptive counseling.

  • Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs require renal, gastrointestinal, bleeding, cardiovascular, and pregnancy review.

  • Opioids and butalbital-containing products are not routine migraine therapy because dependence, sedation, poorer migraine control, and medication-overuse headache can result.

Safety Considerations

  • A familiar migraine history does not protect a patient from a new stroke, hemorrhage, infection, glaucoma, or hypertensive pregnancy disorder; compare the current event with the established pattern.

  • Limit acute-medication frequency according to the specific drug and treatment plan to reduce medication-overuse headache.

  • Migraine with aura modestly increases ischemic stroke risk; smoking adds vascular risk, and combined hormonal contraception should not be used under the U.S. MEC because of unacceptable risk.

  • Preventive medicines should be tapered or changed with clinician guidance rather than stopped abruptly.

Nursing Priorities

  • Establish exact onset, time to peak intensity, similarity to prior attacks, neurologic findings, fever, trauma, pregnancy status, medication exposure, and anticoagulant use before labeling the event migraine.

  • Provide a quiet, dim environment; control nausea; support oral or intravenous hydration when clinically indicated; and reassess pain, function, neurologic status, and adverse effects after treatment.

  • Document acute-medication days and preventive adherence, not only pain intensity, because escalating rescue use may be driving chronic symptoms.

  • Teach the patient to track attack pattern and functional recovery so therapy is judged by restored function as well as numerical pain reduction.

Red Flags

  • Thunderclap headache or pain reaching maximum intensity within seconds to a minute

  • New focal deficit, persistent confusion, seizure, syncope, papilledema, meningismus, fever, or reduced consciousness

  • New or markedly changed headache during pregnancy or postpartum, after trauma, with cancer or immunosuppression, or while anticoagulated

  • New headache after age fifty, progressive daily worsening, exertional onset, or a first atypical or prolonged aura

Complications

  • Status migrainosus, dehydration, and emergency-care use

  • Medication-overuse headache and progression to chronic migraine

  • Functional disability, sleep disruption, anxiety, depression, and reduced school or work participation

  • Rare migrainous infarction or persistent aura complication

Prognosis

Migraine usually follows a relapsing course whose frequency changes with age, hormones, health, and treatment. Many people achieve substantial control when acute therapy is taken early, preventive treatment matches their risks, and medication overuse is prevented. A change in pattern always deserves reassessment rather than being assumed to be ordinary progression.

Prevention

Use consistent sleep, meals, hydration, activity, and caffeine patterns rather than an excessively restrictive trigger list. Use a preventive medicine or device when the attack burden warrants it and review effectiveness after an adequate trial. Treat comorbid sleep, mood, and pain disorders, prevent medication overuse, stop smoking, and review estrogen exposure when migraine with aura or other vascular risks are present.

Patient Education

  • Migraine is a neurologic disorder, not a failure to cope and not simply dilated blood vessels.

  • Take the agreed acute medicine early, but do not keep escalating rescue use without contacting the prescriber.

  • Keep a simple diary of onset, associated symptoms, menstrual or sleep context, medicines taken, and return to function.

  • Seek emergency help for thunderclap onset, new weakness or speech difficulty, seizure, fever with neck stiffness, fainting, or an attack that is distinctly unlike prior migraine.

Special Populations

  • Children may have shorter, bilateral attacks and prominent abdominal symptoms; school function and weight-based prescribing require pediatric review.

  • Pregnancy and lactation change the safety of acute and preventive medicines and lower the threshold to assess secondary headache causes.

  • A first migraine-like syndrome in an older adult requires evaluation for secondary vascular, inflammatory, ocular, and structural disease.

Nclex Traps

  • Migraine is not confirmed by a normal CT; it is a clinical diagnosis made after red flags are assessed.

  • Aura is a focal neurologic symptom, but first-time weakness or aphasia is treated as possible stroke until evaluated.

  • The safest routine plan favors migraine-specific and non-opioid therapy while monitoring total acute-medication use.

  • Do not place every patient with migraine in a dark room before completing the neurologic and red-flag assessment.
