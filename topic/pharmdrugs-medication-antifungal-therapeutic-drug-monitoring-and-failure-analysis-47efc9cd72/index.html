Used To Treat

A diagnostic framework for low response, unexpected toxicity, or breakthrough infection during systemic antifungal therapy.

Description

Therapeutic drug monitoring measures concentration to answer a defined question: did enough active drug reach plasma without entering a toxic range? It is particularly useful for itraconazole, voriconazole, and selected posaconazole therapy because absorption and metabolism vary, and for flucytosine because renal accumulation drives marrow and hepatic toxicity. A level is not a standalone verdict. Failure can occur despite an adequate concentration when the organism is resistant, the infected catheter or necrotic tissue remains, the site is poorly penetrated, the diagnosis is wrong, or immune recovery has not occurred. Conversely, a low level may reflect missed doses, wrong formulation, food or acid mismatch, tube-feed interference, vomiting/diarrhea, an enzyme inducer, rapid metabolism, or a sample drawn at the wrong time.

Mechanism

Trough sampling near the end of a dosing interval approximates minimum steady-state exposure, but steady state and target differ by drug. Voriconazole has nonlinear adult pharmacokinetics, so dose changes can produce disproportionate level changes. Itraconazole has an active hydroxy metabolite and formulation-dependent absorption; the reported assay may include or separate them. Posaconazole suspension exposure depends more on food and GI function than delayed-release tablets. Flucytosine is renally eliminated, so falling GFR prolongs exposure and allows host cells and gut flora to generate toxic fluorinated metabolites. The 'why' behind TDM is therefore prevention of two opposite errors: assuming a prescribed dose equals delivered exposure, and escalating a fully delivered drug when the real problem is source or resistance.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Never delay urgent active therapy while waiting for a level in a deteriorating patient. Draw the sample correctly, but simultaneously reassess diagnosis, organism, source, site, interactions, adherence, and organ function.

Adverse Effects

  • Underexposure risks progression and resistance selection; overexposure risks hepatic, neurologic, visual, QT, marrow, or other drug-specific toxicity.

  • Blind dose escalation can convert a source-control failure into toxicity without improving infection.

Contraindications

  • Do not compare a random concentration to a trough target without exact draw time and dosing history.

  • Do not apply one laboratory's target to every organism, site, prophylaxis, and treatment indication without specialist interpretation.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record last dose time, formulation, route, meal/tube-feed timing, missed or vomited doses, interacting medicines, sample time, and clinical question on the level request.

  • When a result returns, close the loop: document dose or interaction change, repeat-level plan, toxicity assessment, and whether source control or susceptibility still needs action.

Interactions

  • Strong inducers can make levels subtherapeutic; inhibitors or hepatic failure can make them toxic. Acid suppression and enteral nutrition alter selected oral formulations.

  • Renal decline is a pharmacokinetic interaction with flucytosine even without a second drug because clearance itself changes.

Key Labs

  • Drug-specific trough, organism MIC/susceptibility, liver tests, creatinine/eGFR, CBC for flucytosine, electrolytes/ECG for QT-active azoles, and cultures or imaging that measure disease response.

  • Concentration-to-MIC relationships may be useful but are not equally validated for every drug-organism pair.

Nclex Traps

  • A therapeutic level proves exposure, not cure.

  • A low level is a clue to investigate delivery; it is not automatically proof that the prescribed dose was too small.

Source Note

IDSA aspergillosis TDM recommendations and current U.S. triazole and flucytosine labeling.

    Pediatric caution

    Note

    Pediatric indication, formulation, dose, organ maturity, and exposure differ by drug. Use the age- and weight-specific label or infectious-disease protocol rather than scaling an adult regimen by intuition.

  • Type

    geriatric

    Label

    Older adult caution

    Note

    Older adults more often have kidney or liver dysfunction, electrolyte vulnerability, QT-active polypharmacy, anticoagulants, transplant drugs, and frailty. Review the exact agent and formulation before dosing.

  • Type

    pregnancy

    Label

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Pregnancy and lactation decisions are organism-, site-, severity-, and drug-specific. Azoles, polyenes, echinocandins, and local agents do not share one reproductive-risk rule; obtain specialist guidance for invasive infection.
