Definition

Stridor is a harsh, usually high-pitched breathing sound produced when air passes through a narrowed upper airway, most often the larynx or upper trachea.

Plain Language

Stridor is a loud harsh sound from the throat or upper airway, usually heard while breathing in.

Why It Matters

Stridor can signal dangerous upper-airway narrowing. Stridor at rest with drooling, voice change, swelling, cyanosis, exhaustion, or difficulty handling saliva requires emergency airway assessment; a fading sound can mean airflow is failing rather than improving.

Associated Conditions

  • upper-airway narrowing or obstruction caused by edema, infection, a foreign body, allergic swelling, trauma, a mass, vocal-cord dysfunction, or fixed laryngeal or tracheal stenosis

Assessment Method

Assess airway and breathing before prolonged auscultation; keep an acutely distressed child calm, listen over the neck and chest without forcing the throat open, and document timing, loudest location, voice, cough, drooling, retractions, oxygenation, mental status, onset, and recent choking, allergy, infection, trauma, or airway procedure.

Positive Finding

A harsh high-pitched sound is heard loudest over the neck, usually during inspiration; expiratory or biphasic timing can occur when obstruction is intrathoracic or fixed.

Mechanism

Rapid turbulent airflow through a critically narrowed laryngeal or tracheal passage creates vibration that is transmitted loudly over the neck; respiratory-phase timing partly reflects the level and whether the narrowing changes with pressure.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Stridor response raises concern for upper-airway narrowing or obstruction caused by edema, infection, a foreign body, allergic swelling, trauma, a mass, vocal-cord dysfunction, or fixed laryngeal or tracheal stenosis because rapid turbulent airflow through a critically narrowed laryngeal or tracheal passage creates vibration that is transmitted loudly over the neck; respiratory-phase timing partly reflects the level and whether the narrowing changes with pressure. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Stridor does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Stridor to focus evaluation of upper-airway narrowing or obstruction caused by edema, infection, a foreign body, allergic swelling, trauma, a mass, vocal-cord dysfunction, or fixed laryngeal or tracheal stenosis and the reviewed alternatives: wheezing, stertor, transmitted upper-airway noise, laryngospasm. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Assess airway and breathing before prolonged auscultation; keep an acutely distressed child calm, listen over the neck and chest without forcing the throat open, and document timing, loudest location, voice, cough, drooling, retractions, oxygenation, mental status, onset, and recent choking, allergy, infection, trauma, or airway procedure. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Stridor as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Wheezing and transmitted airway noise can sound similar, crying or agitation alters intensity, and absence of stridor does not exclude severe or complete obstruction. Therefore, Stridor cannot confirm or exclude upper-airway narrowing or obstruction caused by edema, infection, a foreign body, allergic swelling, trauma, a mass, vocal-cord dysfunction, or fixed laryngeal or tracheal stenosis by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • wheezing

  • stertor

  • transmitted upper-airway noise

  • laryngospasm

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from wheezing, stertor, transmitted upper-airway noise, laryngospasm, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Stridor may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current high-priority airway sign; its timing and location can help localize obstruction, but loudness alone does not measure severity and complete obstruction may become quiet. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Stridor, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Assess airway and breathing before prolonged auscultation; keep an acutely distressed child calm, listen over the neck and chest without forcing the throat open, and document timing, loudest location, voice, cough, drooling, retractions, oxygenation, mental status, onset, and recent choking, allergy, infection, trauma, or airway procedure. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Stridor at rest with drooling, tripod posture, voice change, cyanosis, exhaustion, altered consciousness, rapidly increasing swelling, or inability to handle secretions is an airway emergency; keep the patient upright and calm, avoid blind throat examination, and activate controlled expert airway management.
