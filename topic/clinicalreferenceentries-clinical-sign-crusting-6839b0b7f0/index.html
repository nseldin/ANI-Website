Definition

Crusting means serum (clear wound fluid), blood, or pus has dried on the skin and formed scab-like material. It is a secondary change that usually covers an earlier lesion or break in the skin.

Plain Language

Crusting is dried skin fluid, blood, or pus on the surface.

Why It Matters

Crust can hide the original lesion. Honey-colored crust supports impetigo in the right setting, but crust does not automatically mean infection; repeated bleeding and crusting on a nonhealing growth can also require skin-cancer evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • serum, blood, or pus drying on the skin after a vesicle, pustule, erosion, excoriation, wound, infection, inflammation, trauma, or bleeding lesion

Assessment Method

Inspect without forceful removal and document color, thickness, adherence, distribution, underlying or surrounding redness, warmth, swelling, pain, drainage, odor, bleeding, lesion evolution, scratching, wound history, medications, fever, infection exposure, and eye or mucosal involvement.

Positive Finding

Dried adherent material composed of serum, blood, or pus forms a scab-like layer on the skin surface.

Mechanism

Fluid released from damaged or inflamed skin loses water and dries, leaving proteins, cells, blood, or purulent debris attached to the surface.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Crusting finding raises concern for serum, blood, or pus drying on the skin after a vesicle, pustule, erosion, excoriation, wound, infection, inflammation, trauma, or bleeding lesion because fluid released from damaged or inflamed skin loses water and dries, leaving proteins, cells, blood, or purulent debris attached to the surface. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Crusting does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Crusting to focus evaluation of serum, blood, or pus drying on the skin after a vesicle, pustule, erosion, excoriation, wound, infection, inflammation, trauma, or bleeding lesion and the reviewed alternatives: scale, eschar, dried wound exudate, hyperkeratosis, foreign material. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect without forceful removal and document color, thickness, adherence, distribution, underlying or surrounding redness, warmth, swelling, pain, drainage, odor, bleeding, lesion evolution, scratching, wound history, medications, fever, infection exposure, and eye or mucosal involvement. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Crusting as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Color and thickness do not reliably identify the cause, cleansing or treatment may remove the finding, and crust can conceal the primary lesion or an underlying erosion. Therefore, Crusting cannot confirm or exclude serum, blood, or pus drying on the skin after a vesicle, pustule, erosion, excoriation, wound, infection, inflammation, trauma, or bleeding lesion by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • scale

  • eschar

  • dried wound exudate

  • hyperkeratosis

  • foreign material

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as scale, eschar, dried wound exudate, hyperkeratosis, foreign material.

False Negative Situations

Crusting may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of serum, blood, or pus drying on the skin after a vesicle, pustule, erosion, excoriation, wound, infection, inflammation, trauma, or bleeding lesion.

Modern Status

Current secondary morphology, not a diagnosis; honey-colored crusting supports impetigo in the right pattern, while recurrent bleeding and crusting on a nonhealing growth may require evaluation for skin cancer.
