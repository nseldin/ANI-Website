Definition

Malnutrition develops when energy, protein, or micronutrient intake and absorption fail to meet metabolic needs, causing loss of muscle, immune function, and tissue repair capacity. The characteristic pattern is unintentional weight loss, reduced intake, muscle and fat wasting, weakness, poor wound healing, edema, or functional decline. Confirm or stage it with validated nutrition screening followed by dietitian assessment; distinguish it from cachexia, sarcopenia, dehydration, depression, food insecurity, malabsorption, and edema from organ disease by history and body-composition findings. The priority is to treat the underlying intake, swallowing, absorption, inflammation, and access barriers because refeeding syndrome with phosphate depletion and cardiac or respiratory failure can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Weakness, sweating, tremor, palpitations, mental-status change, weight change, thirst/polyuria, muscle cramps/spasm, paresthesias, fatigue, temperature intolerance, or abnormal growth/nutrition cues can reveal metabolic instability.

  • Escalate severe hypo/hyperglycemia, seizure, dysrhythmia, tetany/laryngospasm, adrenal crisis, thyroid storm, refeeding electrolyte collapse, or DKA/HHS signs.

Diagnostics

  • validated nutrition screening followed by dietitian assessment

  • weight trajectory, intake, physical examination, function, swallowing, dental, gastrointestinal, medication, and social evaluation

  • electrolytes and deficiency testing chosen by risk rather than albumin alone

  • Distinguish Malnutrition from cachexia, sarcopenia, dehydration, depression, food insecurity, malabsorption, and edema from organ disease by history and body-composition findings.

Labs

  • glucose may be high or low depending on condition

  • sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate changes can become life-threatening

  • anion gap and ketones matter in DKA

Treatments

  • treat the underlying intake, swallowing, absorption, inflammation, and access barriers

  • provide individualized oral, enteral, or parenteral nutrition

  • advance calories and electrolytes cautiously when refeeding risk is high

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for severe hypophosphatemia or other electrolyte change, new edema or heart failure, dysrhythmia, delirium, seizure, aspiration, inability to protect the airway, or hemodynamic instability because refeeding or the underlying disease can rapidly become life-threatening.

  • Screen recent intake, unintentional weight loss, swallowing, gastrointestinal loss, inflammation, food access, functional decline, substance use, and physical signs of muscle and fat loss because body mass index and serum albumin alone can miss clinically important malnutrition.

  • Obtain a dietitian-led nutrition assessment and trend consistent weights, intake percentage, handgrip or function, wounds, edema, stool or emesis losses, glucose, electrolytes, blood count, and micronutrients when indicated because response must be measured across intake, body stores, and recovery.

  • Choose the safest oral, enteral, or parenteral route with swallowing and aspiration precautions and verify tube or line care because nutrition only helps when delivery reaches the patient without causing aspiration, infection, or access injury.

  • Start and advance repletion according to refeeding risk while checking phosphate, potassium, magnesium, glucose, fluid balance, rhythm, respiratory strength, and thiamine need because insulin-driven shifts can cause dysrhythmia, heart failure, seizure, and ventilatory weakness.

Complications

  • refeeding syndrome with phosphate depletion and cardiac or respiratory failure

  • infection can complicate Malnutrition.

  • pressure injury can complicate Malnutrition.

  • falls can complicate Malnutrition.

  • delayed healing can complicate Malnutrition.

  • aspiration or line complications from nutrition support

Red Flags

  • Falling phosphate, potassium, or magnesium with weakness or electrocardiographic change

  • New edema, crackles, hypoxemia, tachycardia, or heart-failure findings after feeding begins

  • Delirium, ataxia, seizure, severe glucose instability, or suspected thiamine deficiency

  • Choking, aspiration, recurrent vomiting, or inability to maintain a safe nutrition route

Patient Education

  • Explain that nutrition treatment may need to start slowly when intake has been very low because rapid feeding can shift electrolytes into cells faster than the body can adapt.

  • Build the discharge plan around affordable preferred foods, symptoms, culture, chewing and swallowing ability, and practical support because a technically ideal plan is ineffective if it cannot be followed.

Nclex Traps

  • Albumin is influenced by inflammation and fluid status and is not a stand-alone nutrition marker

  • A severely depleted patient can be harmed by calories advanced too quickly
