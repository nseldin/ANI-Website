Used To Treat

Current labeled uses include empirical therapy in febrile neutropenia, cryptococcal meningitis in patients with HIV, visceral leishmaniasis, and susceptible systemic fungal infection when conventional amphotericin fails or cannot be used because of renal impairment or toxicity. It is also central in guideline-directed mucormycosis and selected invasive molds.

Description

Liposomal amphotericin B, branded AmBisome, packages the ergosterol-binding, membrane-pore-forming polyene antifungal in true unilamellar phospholipid vesicles for IV infusion, changing free-drug distribution and generally lowering nephrotoxicity and acute infusion reactions versus amphotericin B deoxycholate. That safety advantage is why it is preferred for many prolonged, CNS, renal-risk, or highly invasive indications. It does not make the drug benign: AKI, potassium/magnesium wasting, anemia, infusion reactions, and anaphylaxis remain possible. Amphotericin formulations are not interchangeable, and liposomal amphotericin is not dose equivalent to deoxycholate or amphotericin B lipid complex.

Mechanism

The liposome remains intact in plasma long enough to reduce immediate interaction with mammalian membranes and is taken into reticuloendothelial and infected tissues. At fungal sites, amphotericin reaches the cell wall and binds ergosterol, forming pores that leak monovalent ions and kill the cell. Reduced free renal exposure explains lower nephrotoxicity compared with deoxycholate, while retained amphotericin activity explains continued fungal killing. Liposomes do not remove the molecule's ability to interact with cholesterol or trigger inflammation, so monitoring remains necessary.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not substitute another amphotericin product on a milligram-for-milligram basis. Severe anaphylaxis can occur; verify exact product and stop the infusion immediately for a severe reaction.

Adverse Effects

  • Infusion fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, back/chest pain, flushing, dyspnea, blood-pressure or heart-rate change, and rare anaphylaxis.

  • Creatinine elevation/AKI, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, anemia, thrombocytopenia or leukopenia, and liver-test abnormalities.

Contraindications

  • Known serious hypersensitivity to amphotericin B deoxycholate or formulation components unless benefit outweighs risk under specialist direction.

  • Do not confuse liposomal amphotericin with amphotericin B lipid complex; both are lipid products but have different structures, doses, and evidence.

Nursing Essentials

  • Reconstitute and dilute only with the label-specified fluids and technique; amphotericin products have important incompatibilities. Use the specified filter and infusion time, and keep product identity visible through preparation and administration.

  • Trend creatinine, urine output, potassium, magnesium, CBC, liver tests, fluid balance, and infusion symptoms. Review the need for hydration and premedication using the patient and product-specific protocol.

Interactions

  • Nephrotoxins and electrolyte-wasting drugs still add risk despite liposomal delivery.

  • Flucytosine combination in cryptococcal meningitis increases efficacy but renal injury from amphotericin can reduce flucytosine clearance, so CBC, renal function, and drug exposure are causally connected.

Key Labs

  • Creatinine/eGFR, urine output, potassium, magnesium, bicarbonate, CBC, hepatic tests, infusion vitals, cultures/antigen or imaging appropriate to the infection.

  • Monitor response at the disease site; serum drug levels are not routinely used to titrate liposomal amphotericin.

Nclex Traps

  • Liposomal delivery lowers toxicity relative to deoxycholate; it does not change the fundamental ergosterol-binding mechanism.

  • The formulation name belongs in every handoff because the same active drug can have very different dose ranges.
