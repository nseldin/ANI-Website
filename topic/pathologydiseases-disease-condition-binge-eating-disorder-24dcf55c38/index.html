Plain Language

Binge-eating disorder causes repeated episodes of eating an unusually large amount while feeling unable to stop, followed by significant distress. Unlike bulimia nervosa, the person does not regularly use vomiting, fasting, laxatives, or driven exercise to compensate for the binges.

Why It Matters

BED can cause severe shame, depression, suicide risk, social and financial harm, and worsening metabolic or gastrointestinal illness, yet it is often missed when care focuses only on body weight. The treatment goal is stopping loss-of-control eating and restoring health and function, not forcing weight loss.

Definition

Binge-eating disorder is defined by recurrent objective binge-eating episodes with loss of control, associated characteristic features, marked distress, and an average frequency of at least weekly for three months under DSM-5-TR. The episodes are not accompanied by regular inappropriate compensatory behavior and do not occur exclusively during bulimia nervosa or anorexia nervosa. Body size is not a diagnostic criterion.

Pathology

There is no diagnostic lesion or body-size pattern. Repeated large episodes can produce abdominal pain, reflux, sleep disruption, and functional impairment. Metabolic, cardiovascular, liver, joint, or sleep disorders may coexist, but neither obesity nor a laboratory abnormality proves BED, and BED can occur without obesity.

Pathophysiology

  • Dietary restriction, irregular eating, reward learning, emotional regulation, stress, sleep, impulsivity, and altered hunger or satiety signaling may interact, but no one mechanism explains every case.

  • Shame and attempts at rigid compensation can increase later hunger or distress and sustain a binge-restriction cycle even when the person does not meet bulimia criteria.

  • Weight stigma and repeated ineffective weight-loss treatment can delay recognition and worsen avoidance of healthcare.

Etiology

BED is multifactorial. Genetic vulnerability, mood or anxiety symptoms, ADHD or impulsivity, trauma, chronic dieting, food insecurity, sleep problems, weight stigma, and social context may contribute without individually proving cause. It is not caused by laziness or lack of discipline.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of an eating disorder, depression, anxiety, ADHD, substance use, or trauma-related symptoms

  • Chronic restrictive dieting, weight cycling, food insecurity, poor sleep, and repeated weight stigma

  • Any body size, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, or age can be affected; higher weight is neither required nor sufficient

  • Medications or conditions that change appetite require assessment but do not by themselves establish BED

Signs Symptoms

  • Eating an unusually large amount in a defined period with a sense of being unable to stop or control the episode

  • Eating rapidly, until uncomfortably full, when not physically hungry, alone because of embarrassment, or followed by disgust, depression, or guilt

  • Marked distress, secrecy, food spending or scarcity after episodes, disrupted routines, and avoidance of social eating or healthcare

  • Depression, anxiety, ADHD symptoms, trauma-related symptoms, substance use, self-harm, suicidal thinking, sleep disturbance, reflux, or abdominal discomfort

  • No regular compensatory vomiting, fasting, laxative or diuretic misuse, or driven exercise pattern; if present, reassess for bulimia or another eating disorder

Diagnostics

  • Ask privately about episode size, loss of control, associated features, frequency, duration, distress, secrecy, restriction, night eating, compensatory behavior, food access, triggers, medicines, substances, body-image concerns, mood, trauma, and functional effects.

  • Apply current DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 requirements after differentiating objective binges from subjective overeating and confirming that regular compensation is absent. A screening score or obesity diagnosis does not confirm BED.

  • Assess vital signs, weight trajectory without stigma, abdominal and metabolic symptoms, sleep, medicines, pregnancy possibility, depression, self-harm, suicide risk, and co-occurring psychiatric or medical illness.

  • Evaluate medical conditions based on symptoms and risk rather than ordering a universal BED laboratory panel.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses BED. Select glucose or A1c, lipids, liver measures, renal testing, pregnancy testing, thyroid testing, or other studies when history, examination, medicine choice, or comorbidity makes them relevant.

  • Before and during medication, use label-directed cardiovascular, psychiatric, substance-use, growth or weight, and interaction monitoring; testing is individualized.

  • A normal metabolic panel does not make binge-related distress or functional impairment unimportant.

Assessment

Establish whether the episodes meet objective binge and loss-of-control criteria, whether compensatory behavior changes the diagnosis, and whether suicide risk, severe depression, substance use, acute abdominal symptoms, pregnancy, or another medical condition requires urgent attention. Discuss weight only as clinically relevant and do not equate BED treatment with a demand for weight loss.

Differential

Differentiate BED from bulimia nervosa, anorexia nervosa binge-eating/purging subtype, OSFED low-frequency BED, night eating syndrome, emotional or situational overeating without recurrent loss of control and impairment, medication-related appetite change, Prader-Willi or other genetic syndromes, bipolar mania, ADHD-related impulsivity, substance use, and endocrine or sleep disorders. Obesity is a medical condition, not an eating-disorder diagnosis.

Treatments

  • Offer eating-disorder-focused cognitive behavioral therapy or interpersonal psychotherapy in individual or group format. Guided self-help may be appropriate in some stepped-care systems with follow-up and escalation when response is inadequate.

  • Establish regular adequate eating, reduce rigid restriction, identify episode triggers and consequences, and address food access, sleep, mood, trauma, and weight stigma. Treatment targets binge cessation, distress, health, and function rather than weight loss alone.

  • For adults who prefer medication or do not respond adequately to psychotherapy alone, guideline-supported options include an antidepressant or lisdexamfetamine. Lisdexamfetamine is labeled for moderate-to-severe BED in adults, not pediatric BED, obesity, or weight loss, and requires abuse, cardiovascular, psychiatric, sleep, appetite, and interaction review.

  • Treat confirmed metabolic, sleep, gastrointestinal, mood, anxiety, ADHD, or substance-use conditions in parallel without making weight loss a condition for receiving BED care.

Contraindications

  • Lisdexamfetamine is not indicated for weight loss or obesity and is not established for pediatric BED; do not extend the adult BED indication to other populations.

  • Do not use lisdexamfetamine in a patient with a label contraindication such as hypersensitivity to amphetamine products, and review current product warnings, interacting medicines, cardiovascular disease, psychosis or mania risk, and substance-use risk before prescribing.

  • Do not prescribe a highly restrictive diet that predictably intensifies binge-restriction cycling without an individualized medical and eating-disorder plan.

  • Do not use purging, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, or driven exercise as weight or binge management.

Safety Considerations

  • Ask directly about suicide, self-harm, severe depression, substance use, trauma and abuse, food insecurity, and access to lethal means; shame can hide both bingeing and acute risk.

  • Lisdexamfetamine is a controlled stimulant with abuse, misuse, addiction, overdose, cardiovascular, psychiatric, sleep, and appetite risks. Assess risk before prescribing, store securely, and monitor throughout treatment.

  • New severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, chest symptoms, or altered consciousness requires medical evaluation rather than being attributed automatically to bingeing.

  • Pregnancy and lactation require individualized psychotherapy and medication review; do not continue or stop a stimulant without the responsible prescriber.

Nursing Priorities

  • Use nonstigmatizing questions to characterize objective binge episodes, loss of control, restriction, compensation, food access, mood, suicide risk, medicines, substances, sleep, and function.

  • Monitor treatment by binge frequency and loss of control, distress, regular eating, physical symptoms, function, adverse effects, and safety rather than weight alone.

  • For lisdexamfetamine, verify the exact adult BED indication, baseline risk review, secure storage teaching, and prescribed cardiovascular, psychiatric, sleep, appetite, and misuse monitoring.

  • Coordinate therapy, nutrition, primary care, psychiatry, pharmacy, and treatment of metabolic or sleep disease while protecting the patient from shaming or conflicting restrictive advice.

Red Flags

  • Suicidal intent, recent serious self-harm, severe depression with inability to care for basic needs, dangerous substance use, or inability to maintain safety

  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain or distention, hematemesis, persistent vomiting, chest pain, syncope, altered consciousness, or other acute medical deterioration

  • New psychosis, mania, severe agitation, severe hypertension, chest pain, or signs of stimulant overdose during medication treatment

  • Food insecurity so severe that the patient or dependent household members cannot meet basic nutrition needs

Complications

  • Depression, anxiety, self-harm, suicide, substance use, sleep disruption, shame, isolation, school or work impairment, financial harm, and reduced quality of life

  • Reflux, abdominal pain, gastrointestinal distress, and injury from unsafe compensatory behavior if the syndrome changes

  • Coexisting type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, fatty liver disease, hypertension, sleep apnea, or joint disease may increase overall health burden but are not caused or diagnosed by BED alone

  • Stimulant adverse effects, misuse, addiction, cardiovascular events, or psychiatric activation when medication is inappropriately selected or monitored

Prognosis

Evidence-based psychotherapy and, for selected adults, medication can substantially reduce or stop binge episodes. Course may fluctuate with stress, restriction, mood, sleep, food access, and stigma. Improvement should be judged by loss-of-control eating, distress, health, safety, and function, not a scale number alone.

Prevention

  • Address recurrent loss-of-control eating early, use regular adequate nutrition, and avoid praise for extreme restriction or weight cycling.

  • Reduce weight-stigmatizing care, screen for food insecurity and mood symptoms, and provide access to evidence-based therapy without requiring weight loss first.

  • Create a relapse plan around early restriction, secrecy, increasing episode urges, sleep change, mood decline, missed therapy, and unsafe medication use.

Patient Education

  • BED is a real eating disorder, not a failure of willpower, and it can occur at any body size.

  • Regular compensation such as vomiting or laxative use suggests a different or additional eating-disorder pattern; tell the clinician because the medical risks and treatment change.

  • Therapy is a first-line treatment. Lisdexamfetamine is an option only for moderate-to-severe BED in adults when a prescriber determines it is appropriate; it is not a weight-loss drug and must not be shared.

  • Seek urgent help for suicidal intent, severe abdominal or chest pain, vomiting blood, fainting, new psychosis or mania, or suspected stimulant overdose.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need family, growth, school, neurodevelopmental, food-access, and safeguarding assessment. The adult lisdexamfetamine BED indication must not be generalized to them.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require coordinated nutritional, obstetric, mood, and medication assessment; body change may intensify symptoms or relapse risk.

  • People in larger bodies may receive only obesity treatment while BED is missed; screen for loss of control and distress before intensifying restriction.

  • ADHD, bipolar disorder, substance-use disorder, cardiovascular disease, and sleep disorder require careful differential and medication-risk review before stimulant treatment.

Nclex Traps

  • BED requires recurrent loss-of-control binges and distress, not a particular weight.

  • Routine compensatory behavior points toward bulimia or another diagnosis, not BED.

  • Treatment does not center on weight loss; CBT or interpersonal psychotherapy targets the eating disorder directly.

  • Lisdexamfetamine is an adult moderate-to-severe BED option, not a pediatric BED or obesity drug, and has abuse and cardiovascular risks.

  • A screening questionnaire cannot diagnose BED or replace suicide and medical assessment.
