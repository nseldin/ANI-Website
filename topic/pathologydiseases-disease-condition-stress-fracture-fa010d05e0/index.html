Definition

Stress fracture develops when repetitive submaximal loading outpaces bone remodeling, creating microscopic failure in normal bone or insufficiency failure in weakened bone. The characteristic pattern is focal activity-related bone pain and tenderness that progresses toward pain at rest, often after a training, surface, footwear, nutrition, or menstrual change. Confirm or stage it with focused examination and plain radiograph, recognizing early films may be normal; distinguish it from muscle strain, shin splints, tendon injury, osteoid osteoma, infection, tumor, and acute fracture by focal bone tenderness and imaging. The priority is to stop impact loading and protect weight bearing according to site and grade because complete fracture can complicate Stress fracture. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Pain, swelling, deformity, wound depth, drainage, odor, tissue color, blistering, numbness/tingling, pulses, movement, sensation, capillary refill, fever, or mechanism of injury shapes tissue-risk assessment.

  • Escalate absent pulses, compartment syndrome signs, evisceration, circumferential burn compromise, spreading infection, necrosis, or pain out of proportion.

Diagnostics

  • focused examination and plain radiograph, recognizing early films may be normal

  • MRI for early detection and grading when diagnosis matters

  • bone-health, nutrition, vitamin D, endocrine, menstrual, medication, and training evaluation

  • Distinguish Stress fracture from muscle strain, shin splints, tendon injury, osteoid osteoma, infection, tumor, and acute fracture by focal bone tenderness and imaging.

Labs

  • CK and potassium may rise with muscle breakdown

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • albumin/prealbumin may reflect nutrition risk

Treatments

  • stop impact loading and protect weight bearing according to site and grade

  • correct training, energy availability, mineral, hormonal, and biomechanical contributors

  • obtain surgical review for high-risk tension-side or displaced sites

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for sudden severe pain, inability to bear weight, deformity, hip or groin pain, neurovascular change, or pain that persists despite unloading because complete fracture, a high-risk femoral-neck lesion, or an alternative diagnosis requires urgent orthopedic care.

  • Assess focal load-related bone pain, tenderness, swelling, night or rest pain, training changes, footwear, surface, menstrual or energy availability, nutrition, eating-disorder risk, bone disease, and medicines because repetitive loading outpaces remodeling when training stress or bone vulnerability rises.

  • Stop impact loading and provide prescribed protected weight bearing, boot, or crutches while documenting pain with walking because continued loading can propagate a microscopic injury into a displaced complete fracture.

  • Coordinate radiographs and MRI or other imaging when ordered and identify high-risk sites such as femoral neck, anterior tibia, navicular, or fifth metatarsal because early radiographs can be normal and poor-blood-supply locations heal slowly or displace.

  • Review calcium and vitamin D intake, overall energy and protein, menstrual and endocrine function, sleep, training recovery, and bone-density evaluation when indicated because correcting low energy availability and metabolic bone risk prevents delayed healing and recurrence.

Complications

  • complete fracture can complicate Stress fracture.

  • displacement can complicate Stress fracture.

  • nonunion can complicate Stress fracture.

  • avascular necrosis at high-risk sites

  • recurrent injury can complicate Stress fracture.

Red Flags

  • Sudden severe pain, crack or pop, deformity, or inability to bear weight

  • Hip or groin pain with weight bearing, night pain, or pain at rest

  • Cool pale limb, absent pulse, progressive numbness, or weakness

  • Persistent focal pain despite unloading or worsening at a known high-risk bone site

Patient Education

  • Stop the provoking impact activity rather than training through pain because repeated load can convert a healing bone stress injury into a complete fracture.

  • Return only after clinical clearance and increase duration, frequency, and intensity gradually with rest days because bone adaptation lags behind cardiovascular fitness.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal early radiograph does not exclude a stress fracture

  • Femoral-neck, navicular, anterior tibial, and fifth-metatarsal patterns need higher urgency
