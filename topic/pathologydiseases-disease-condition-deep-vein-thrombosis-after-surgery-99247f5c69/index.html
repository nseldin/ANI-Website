Definition

Deep vein thrombosis after surgery develops when postoperative venous stasis, endothelial injury, and transient hypercoagulability promote thrombus formation in a deep leg or pelvic vein. The characteristic pattern is new unilateral swelling, pain, warmth, or pitting edema after immobility or surgery, with pulmonary embolism symptoms treated as an emergency. Confirm or stage it with pretest-probability assessment followed by compression ultrasonography; distinguish it from cellulitis, hematoma, ruptured Baker cyst, superficial thrombophlebitis, and dependent edema by venous imaging and the full clinical pattern. The priority is to begin anticoagulation when benefits outweigh operative and neuraxial bleeding risks because pulmonary embolism with obstructive shock can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • pretest-probability assessment followed by compression ultrasonography

  • D-dimer only when probability and postoperative timing make it interpretable

  • repeat or whole-leg imaging when suspicion remains despite a nondiagnostic initial study

  • Distinguish Deep vein thrombosis after surgery from cellulitis, hematoma, ruptured Baker cyst, superficial thrombophlebitis, and dependent edema by venous imaging and the full clinical pattern.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • begin anticoagulation when benefits outweigh operative and neuraxial bleeding risks

  • use ordered mechanical prophylaxis and early ambulation for prevention

  • coordinate thrombectomy or specialty intervention for limb-threatening or selected extensive thrombosis

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, tachycardia with hypoxemia, phlegmasia, or neurovascular compromise because pulmonary embolism or limb-threatening venous obstruction may be occurring.

  • Compare both legs for swelling, warmth, color, tenderness, and circumference and assess catheter and operative risk factors because postoperative venous stasis and endothelial injury can produce an initially subtle clot.

  • Do not massage a suspected thrombosed limb and limit activity until the diagnostic and treatment plan is clarified because manipulation does not dissolve clot and may worsen pain or embolic risk.

  • Apply ordered mechanical prophylaxis correctly and promote frequent ambulation and leg exercises when safe because intermittent compression and calf-muscle pumping reduce venous stasis.

  • Administer prescribed anticoagulation after verifying bleeding risk, neuraxial-catheter timing, renal function, and recent surgery because thrombosis prevention must be balanced against operative-site or spinal bleeding.

  • Monitor platelet count, hemoglobin, wound drainage, stool and urine blood, limb findings, chest symptoms, and oxygenation to detect bleeding, clot extension, or pulmonary embolism.

Complications

  • pulmonary embolism with obstructive shock

  • phlegmasia and limb ischemia

  • post-thrombotic syndrome or major anticoagulant bleeding

Red Flags

  • Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, or hypoxemia

  • Rapidly increasing whole-limb swelling, cyanosis, or severe pain

  • Falling hemoglobin, uncontrolled wound bleeding, or new neurologic deficit on anticoagulation

  • Thrombocytopenia with new thrombosis after heparin exposure

Patient Education

  • Teach the reason for early walking, ankle pumps, compression devices, and prescribed anticoagulant doses even when the incision feels more urgent than clot prevention.

  • Tell the patient to seek emergency help for sudden chest pain, breathlessness, fainting, or coughing blood and to report unilateral leg swelling promptly.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not massage a suspected thrombosed limb

  • A negative D-dimer is not automatically reassuring soon after major surgery
