Definition

Shock index is the unitless calculation SI = HR / SBP: heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure. About 0.5-0.7 is a commonly cited resting-adult or general-triage reference; above 0.9 is a common adult emergency and trauma risk flag, and SI at or above 1 means heart rate numerically meets or exceeds systolic blood pressure. These values are population- and situation-specific warning clues, not universal cutoffs or a diagnosis of shock.

Plain Language

Shock index compares the pulse with the top blood-pressure number. A rising ratio can warn that circulation is under stress even before either vital sign looks dramatically abnormal.

Why It Matters

A rising or contextually elevated shock index can signal hemorrhage or circulatory deterioration before isolated heart rate or blood pressure looks dramatic. Age, pregnancy, beta blockers, vasoactive medicines, pacing, arrhythmia, fever, pain, anxiety, chronic blood pressure, athletic conditioning, and measurement error change interpretation, so nurses must trend the full perfusion picture and never delay hemorrhage control, sepsis treatment, or resuscitation to calculate it.

Assessment Method

After immediate airway, breathing, circulation, and hemorrhage priorities, use simultaneously measured heart rate and systolic blood pressure from validated readings, calculate the unitless shock index as SI = HR / SBP, and trend the result with mental status, skin perfusion, capillary refill, pulses, urine output, oxygenation, temperature, pain, rhythm, medications, age, pregnancy, and baseline.

Positive Finding

About 0.5-0.7 is a commonly cited resting-adult or general-triage reference; above 0.9 is a common adult emergency and trauma risk flag, while SI at or above 1 means heart rate numerically meets or exceeds systolic blood pressure; these are contextual clues rather than universal diagnostic cutoffs.

Mechanism

Circulatory stress often raises heart rate while falling stroke volume and vascular compensation reduce systolic pressure, so their ratio can worsen before either isolated vital sign appears dramatically abnormal.

Evidence Limitations

Beta blockers, pacemakers, arrhythmias, pain, fever, anxiety, pregnancy, age, chronic hypertension, athletic conditioning, vasoactive drugs, and measurement error alter the ratio; a normal value does not exclude compensated or late shock. Therefore, Shock index elevation cannot confirm or exclude compensatory tachycardia relative to systolic pressure during hemorrhage, hypovolemia, sepsis, cardiogenic or obstructive shock, severe trauma, or another acute circulatory stress by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as pain-related tachycardia, fever, arrhythmia, chronic low blood pressure.

False Negative Situations

Shock index elevation may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of compensatory tachycardia relative to systolic pressure during hemorrhage, hypovolemia, sepsis, cardiogenic or obstructive shock, severe trauma, or another acute circulatory stress.

Modern Status
