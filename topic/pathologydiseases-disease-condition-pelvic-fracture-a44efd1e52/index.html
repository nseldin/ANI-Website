Definition

Pelvic fracture develops when high-energy force or fragility breaks the pelvic ring and can disrupt venous plexuses, arteries, bladder, urethra, rectum, and nerves. The characteristic pattern is pelvic pain or instability with shock, perineal bruising, leg-length change, hematuria, or a dangerous mechanism. Confirm or stage it with trauma survey with pelvic radiograph and focused ultrasound; distinguish it from hip fracture, acetabular injury, spinal injury, abdominal hemorrhage, and isolated soft-tissue trauma by imaging and examination. The priority is to apply a pelvic binder at the greater trochanters for suspected unstable hemorrhagic injury because exsanguinating retroperitoneal hemorrhage can complicate Pelvic fracture. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Pain, swelling, deformity, wound depth, drainage, odor, tissue color, blistering, numbness/tingling, pulses, movement, sensation, capillary refill, fever, or mechanism of injury shapes tissue-risk assessment.

  • Escalate absent pulses, compartment syndrome signs, evisceration, circumferential burn compromise, spreading infection, necrosis, or pain out of proportion.

Diagnostics

  • trauma survey with pelvic radiograph and focused ultrasound

  • contrast CT and angiographic evaluation when stable enough

  • retrograde urethral or bladder imaging when blood at the meatus or urinary injury is suspected

  • Distinguish Pelvic fracture from hip fracture, acetabular injury, spinal injury, abdominal hemorrhage, and isolated soft-tissue trauma by imaging and examination.

Labs

  • CK and potassium may rise with muscle breakdown

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • albumin/prealbumin may reflect nutrition risk

Treatments

  • apply a pelvic binder at the greater trochanters for suspected unstable hemorrhagic injury

  • activate massive transfusion and hemorrhage-control pathways

  • coordinate embolization, preperitoneal packing, external fixation, or surgery

Nursing Priorities

  • Activate trauma and hemorrhage pathways, secure large-bore access, and trend blood pressure, mental status, skin perfusion, lactate, hemoglobin, and urine output because the pelvis can conceal life-threatening blood loss.

  • Apply an ordered pelvic binder centered over the greater trochanters and document time and skin checks because correct placement reduces pelvic volume while prolonged pressure can injure tissue.

  • Avoid repeated pelvic compression or logrolling unless essential because disrupting early clot formation can worsen hemorrhage.

  • Assess abdomen, perineum, urethral bleeding, urine, rectum or vagina when clinically directed, and distal pulses, movement, sensation, and limb length because genitourinary, bowel, nerve, and vascular injuries often coexist.

  • Prepare for balanced blood-product resuscitation, tranexamic acid when ordered, interventional radiology, operative control, or transfer; prevent hypothermia and monitor calcium during massive transfusion because high-energy force or fragility breaks the pelvic ring and can disrupt venous plexuses, arteries, bladder, urethra, rectum, and nerves.

  • Escalate immediately for persistent hypotension, rising lactate, expanding pelvic or flank swelling, gross hematuria, absent distal pulse, new neurologic loss, or uncontrolled pain because high-energy force or fragility breaks the pelvic ring and can disrupt venous plexuses, arteries, bladder, urethra, rectum, and nerves.

Complications

  • exsanguinating retroperitoneal hemorrhage can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • bladder can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • urethral can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • rectal can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • neurologic can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • reproductive injury can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • venous thrombosis can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • infection can complicate Pelvic fracture.

  • chronic pain can complicate Pelvic fracture.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension, narrowing pulse pressure, rising lactate, or declining mental status

  • Gross hematuria, blood at the urethra, perineal bruising, or inability to void

  • Absent or weakening distal pulse, cool limb, or new motor or sensory deficit

  • Increasing abdominal distention, uncontrolled pain, or ongoing transfusion need

Patient Education

  • Explain that serious internal bleeding can occur without a large external wound, which is why repeated circulation checks and urgent imaging or procedures are needed.

  • Ask the patient not to sit up, walk, or remove the binder until the trauma team clears movement; report increasing pelvic pressure, numbness, urinary difficulty, or breathlessness immediately.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not repeatedly rock an unstable pelvis during examination

  • Blood at the urethral meatus is a warning before blind catheterization
