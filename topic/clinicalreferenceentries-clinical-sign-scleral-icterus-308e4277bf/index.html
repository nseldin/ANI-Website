Definition

Scleral icterus is the commonly used name for yellow discoloration of the whites of the eyes caused by bilirubin buildup. More precisely, the conjunctiva—the clear tissue covering the white sclera—becomes yellow.

Plain Language

Scleral icterus means the whites of the eyes look yellow because bilirubin has built up.

Why It Matters

This is a visible form of jaundice and can signal red-blood-cell breakdown, liver injury, impaired bilirubin processing, or blocked bile flow. Eye color cannot identify the cause, so fever, right-upper-abdominal pain, low blood pressure, confusion, severe anemia, or rapid worsening requires urgent evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • hyperbilirubinemia from increased red-cell breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow

Assessment Method

Inspect the exposed bulbar conjunctiva over both sclerae in natural or neutral light and compare with baseline; assess skin and oral mucosa, urine and stool color, itching, abdominal pain, fever, weight change, medications and toxins, bleeding or hemolysis findings, mental status, vital signs, and ordered total and direct bilirubin and liver tests.

Positive Finding

The normally white ocular surface develops a diffuse yellow discoloration; although commonly called scleral icterus, the visible yellowing is primarily in the conjunctiva overlying the sclera.

Mechanism

Excess bilirubin circulates in blood and deposits in ocular surface tissue when production exceeds clearance or when hepatic processing, hepatocyte function, bile formation, or bile drainage is impaired.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Scleral icterus finding raises concern for hyperbilirubinemia from increased red-cell breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow because excess bilirubin circulates in blood and deposits in ocular surface tissue when production exceeds clearance or when hepatic processing, hepatocyte function, bile formation, or bile drainage is impaired. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Scleral icterus does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Evidence Limitations

Lighting, ocular-surface disease, localized yellow lesions, and examiner variation can alter appearance; the finding cannot identify the bilirubin fraction, concentration, or cause, and the traditional name is anatomically imprecise because the conjunctiva rather than bare sclera becomes yellow. Therefore, Scleral icterus cannot confirm or exclude hyperbilirubinemia from increased red-cell breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • carotenemia

  • pinguecula

  • yellow-lighting artifact

  • medication-related discoloration

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as carotenemia, pinguecula, yellow-lighting artifact, medication-related discoloration.

False Negative Situations

Scleral icterus may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of hyperbilirubinemia from increased red-cell breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow.

Modern Status

Current visible hyperbilirubinemia clue and ocular expression of jaundice, not a diagnosis and not a measure of bilirubin concentration.
