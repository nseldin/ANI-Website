Used To Treat

Reduction of elevated intracranial pressure and cerebral edema and reduction of elevated intraocular pressure under product labeling. Osmotic diuresis is part of the effect, but mannitol is not a routine edema drug because it initially expands intravascular volume.

Description

Osmotic diuretics increase the number of osmotically active particles in plasma and tubular fluid. IV mannitol, a six-carbon sugar alcohol, remains mainly extracellular, is freely filtered, and is minimally reabsorbed. In plasma it pulls water from cells across barriers that remain relatively impermeable to mannitol, lowering brain water or vitreous volume. In the nephron it holds water in the lumen, increasing urine flow and solute loss. This two-compartment action creates the central safety paradox: before the kidney excretes the water, mannitol expands plasma volume and can worsen pulmonary edema or heart failure; later, osmotic diuresis can cause hypovolemia, hypernatremia, or kidney injury. If the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, mannitol may enter injured tissue and weaken or reverse the gradient, so osmotherapy requires neurologic, hemodynamic, renal, and osmolar reasoning rather than routine diuretic habits.

Mechanism

An IV mannitol bolus raises extracellular tonicity. Water leaves cells until osmotic forces equilibrate, reducing intracranial volume when cerebral autoregulation and barrier integrity permit. Plasma expansion may transiently improve rheology and cerebral perfusion, but it also increases cardiac preload. Filtered mannitol cannot be reclaimed with sodium and water, so proximal and descending-limb water reabsorption falls and urine flow rises. Sodium can initially fall by dilution as intracellular water enters plasma, then rise as renal water loss exceeds sodium loss. Excess exposure can produce renal vasoconstriction, tubular vacuolization described as osmotic nephrosis, and acute kidney injury. Repeated doses can accumulate when clearance is poor or within tissue behind a damaged barrier, explaining rebound edema or pressure. Serum osmolality and osmolar gap are useful context but are not substitutes for the clinical response and local protocol.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Mannitol can cause fatal fluid and electrolyte shifts, pulmonary edema, acute kidney injury, and neurologic complications. Evaluate renal, cardiac, and pulmonary status before therapy and monitor during treatment; no urine response after a test or therapeutic dose is a danger signal, not a reason for blind repeat dosing.

Adverse Effects

  • Initial plasma-volume expansion can cause hypertension, pulmonary edema, or heart-failure decompensation; later osmotic loss can cause hypotension, dehydration, hypernatremia, hypokalemia, or other electrolyte shifts.

  • Acute kidney injury, osmotic nephrosis, rebound intracranial pressure, headache, nausea, confusion, seizure, infusion reactions, and tissue injury from extravasation are important complications.

Contraindications

  • Established anuria, severe hypovolemia, severe pulmonary congestion or pulmonary edema, active intracranial bleeding except during craniotomy, and mannitol hypersensitivity are central label contraindications.

  • Do not infuse a solution containing crystals. Inspect, warm to redissolve according to instructions, and use the required filter because crystals can form at lower temperatures.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm indication, airway and neurologic status, blood pressure, cardiac and pulmonary findings, urine output, creatinine/eGFR, sodium, potassium, and measured or calculated osmolality under protocol. Use a dedicated filtered line and do not administer through the same set as blood.

  • Reassess pupils, level of consciousness, pressure target, lung sounds, oxygenation, hemodynamics, urine response, sodium, osmolality, and kidney function. Stop and escalate for pulmonary edema, anuria, progressive osmolality, worsening kidney function, or neurologic deterioration.

Interactions

  • Other nephrotoxins increase acute kidney injury risk. Other diuretics can intensify volume and electrolyte loss; medications affected by changing sodium or volume, such as lithium, require review.

  • Concurrent blood products through the same tubing can cause pseudoagglutination or incompatibility concerns; follow the label and institutional line policy.

Key Labs

  • Urine output, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, sodium, potassium, measured serum osmolality, calculated osmolality and osmolar gap under protocol, pressure, oxygenation, lung examination, fluid balance, and neurologic or ocular endpoint.

  • An osmolar threshold is a safety aid, not a universal standalone stop rule. Interpret trend, renal clearance, dose timing, osmolar gap, and clinical response together.

Nclex Traps

  • Mannitol can worsen pulmonary edema even though it ultimately increases urine because its first effect pulls intracellular water into the circulation.

  • Mannitol is an extracellular osmole whose effect depends on water gradients and renal filtration; it is not a conventional sodium-transporter blocker.
