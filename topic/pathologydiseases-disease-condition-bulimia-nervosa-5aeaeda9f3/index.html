Plain Language

Bulimia nervosa is an eating disorder with repeated episodes of eating an unusually large amount while feeling unable to stop, followed by repeated attempts to prevent weight gain such as vomiting, fasting, laxative or diuretic misuse, or driven exercise. A person often has an average or higher weight, so appearance does not reveal the illness.

Why It Matters

Purging can rapidly cause dehydration, dangerous potassium and acid-base changes, abnormal heart rhythms, kidney injury, seizures, dental and esophageal damage, and death. Shame can hide the behavior, and depression, self-harm, or suicide risk may be more urgent than the eating-disorder diagnosis itself.

Definition

Bulimia nervosa is defined by recurrent binge-eating episodes with loss of control and recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors intended to prevent weight gain. Under DSM-5-TR, both occur on average at least weekly for three months, self-evaluation is unduly influenced by weight or shape, and the disturbance does not occur exclusively during anorexia nervosa. Frequency and duration help classify the syndrome but do not alone determine medical danger.

Pathology

There is no diagnostic lesion. Vomiting can erode enamel, enlarge salivary glands, injure the pharynx or esophagus, and produce volume depletion with chloride and potassium loss. Laxative, diuretic, or other medication misuse can cause additional fluid, renal, electrolyte, and bowel injury. Repeated bingeing may produce gastric distress, but body weight is not a reliable marker of severity.

Pathophysiology

  • Restriction and rigid food rules can increase hunger and vulnerability to binge eating; loss of control then triggers shame and compensatory behavior, temporarily reducing fear but reinforcing the cycle.

  • Vomiting commonly causes chloride loss and volume contraction with hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, while diarrhea from laxatives can produce other acid-base patterns. The actual pattern depends on behavior, timing, renal function, fluid intake, and coexposures.

  • Electrolyte change, dehydration, QT-risk medication, and underlying cardiac disease can combine to increase arrhythmia and seizure risk even when the patient does not look ill.

Etiology

Bulimia nervosa is multifactorial. Genetic vulnerability, dieting and restriction, mood or anxiety symptoms, reward and impulse processes, body-image overvaluation, trauma, weight stigma, developmental change, and social pressures may interact. No single family pattern, personality trait, or stressful event proves cause.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of an eating disorder, depression, anxiety, substance use, self-harm, or trauma-related symptoms

  • Rigid dieting, repeated weight cycling, weight stigma, appearance- or weight-focused sport or occupation, and poor body image

  • Adolescence and young adulthood are common periods of onset, but any age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, or body size can be affected

  • Easy access to laxatives, diuretics, diet products, emetics, or medicines that can be misused may increase medical risk

Signs Symptoms

  • Recurrent objectively large binge episodes with loss of control, rapid or secretive eating, eating beyond comfort, and intense shame or guilt

  • Self-induced vomiting, fasting, laxative or diuretic misuse, driven exercise, or other repeated weight-control behavior

  • Frequent bathroom use after meals, calluses over knuckles, dental erosion, sore throat, salivary swelling, reflux symptoms, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhea, edema, or irregular menses

  • Dizziness, fainting, weakness, thirst, palpitations, muscle cramps, or reduced urine output suggesting fluid or electrolyte effects

  • Weight and shape overvaluation, depression, anxiety, substance use, self-harm, suicidal thinking, secrecy, and social or financial impairment

Diagnostics

  • Ask privately about binge size, loss of control, frequency and duration, triggers, restriction, vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, exercise, weight and shape meaning, medicines, supplements, water loading, substance use, last episode, and functional impact. A patient may minimize behavior because of shame or fear.

  • Assess temperature, pulse, blood pressure and orthostatic change when safe, hydration, cardiac and neurologic status, abdomen, oral cavity and teeth, parotid enlargement, skin and hand findings, weight trajectory, pregnancy possibility, mood, suicide risk, abuse, and safeguarding.

  • Apply current DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 requirements. Distinguish bulimia from binge-eating disorder, anorexia nervosa binge-eating/purging subtype, OSFED low-frequency or limited-duration bulimia and purging disorder, and medical vomiting.

  • Determine urgency from current physiology, behavior, trajectory, ECG and laboratory findings, suicide risk, pregnancy, supports, and treatment response rather than weight or episode count alone.

Labs

  • Behavior- and risk-directed tests commonly include sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, phosphorus, renal function, glucose, and an ECG; add complete blood count, liver measures, pregnancy testing, amylase, or other studies when clinically indicated.

  • Repeat testing when purging continues, symptoms change, a QT-risk medicine is used, or an earlier result may not reflect current behavior. Normal values between episodes do not exclude serious recurrent risk.

  • Dental, gastrointestinal, cardiac, or toxicology evaluation is driven by symptoms such as erosion, bleeding, chest pain, severe abdominal pain, syncope, or suspected medication ingestion.

Assessment

First identify syncope, chest pain, severe palpitations, weakness, seizure, confusion, hematemesis, severe abdominal pain, dehydration, acute kidney injury, electrolyte disturbance, pregnancy concerns, suicide risk, or poisoning. Then characterize the binge-purge cycle and decide whether medical stabilization, crisis care, or specialist outpatient treatment is safe. Never assume average weight means lower risk.

Differential

Differentiate bulimia nervosa from anorexia nervosa binge-eating/purging subtype, binge-eating disorder without routine compensation, OSFED purging disorder or low-frequency bulimia, rumination disorder, gastrointestinal or neurologic vomiting, gastroparesis, substance or medication effects, obsessive-compulsive disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, and mood disorders. Bingeing or vomiting can occur in more than one condition and does not diagnose bulimia by itself.

Treatments

  • Correct urgent fluid, electrolyte, acid-base, cardiac, renal, gastrointestinal, and neurologic complications. Use oral replacement when safe and protocol-supported; severe disturbance or absorption problems may require monitored intravenous care.

  • Provide eating-disorder-focused cognitive behavioral therapy. Adolescents and emerging adults with an involved caregiver may receive eating-disorder-focused family-based treatment. Nutrition care establishes regular adequate eating and reduces restriction that drives the cycle.

  • For adults with moderate-to-severe bulimia nervosa, fluoxetine is a representative SSRI with a labeled indication and is used with eating-disorder-focused psychotherapy, not instead of it. Monitor suicidality, interactions, adverse effects, sodium risk, activation, and QT context according to patient and label.

  • Treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, substance use, trauma, dental injury, and gastrointestinal complications, and build relapse plans around early restriction, binge urges, purging, and missed follow-up.

Contraindications

  • Bupropion is contraindicated in a patient with a current or prior diagnosis of bulimia nervosa because seizure risk is higher.

  • Do not use laxatives, diuretics, emetics, thyroid hormone, insulin manipulation, or stimulant weight-loss products as treatment for bingeing or weight concern.

  • Review QT-prolonging and electrolyte-sensitive medicines carefully when purging, syncope, arrhythmia history, or electrolyte disturbance is present.

  • Do not advise abrupt cessation of heavy laxative or diuretic misuse without assessing fluid, electrolyte, bowel, renal, and edema consequences and arranging follow-up.

Safety Considerations

  • Potassium and ECG risk can change quickly with repeated purging. Reassess after new vomiting, diarrhea, medication misuse, fainting, weakness, palpitations, or reduced intake.

  • Vomiting with severe chest or upper abdominal pain, dyspnea, fever, crepitus, or blood requires urgent evaluation for serious esophageal or gastric injury rather than routine eating-disorder follow-up.

  • Ask directly about suicide, self-harm, alcohol or drug use, poisoning, insulin or medication manipulation, abuse, and access to lethal means.

  • Pregnancy changes fluid, nutrition, medication, and fetal risk; coordinate obstetric and eating-disorder care rather than assuming vomiting is pregnancy-related.

Nursing Priorities

  • Ask behavior-specific questions and document last binge and purge, method, amount or frequency, intake, fluid use, symptoms, medicines, supplements, weight trajectory, pregnancy possibility, suicide risk, and supports.

  • Trend vital signs, orthostatic response when safe, hydration, neurologic and cardiac status, intake and output, ECG, electrolytes, renal function, oral findings, abdominal symptoms, edema, and bowel pattern.

  • Administer prescribed fluids, electrolytes, meals, and medicines; observe for arrhythmia, seizure, continued purging, water loading, or medication adverse effects and escalate objective deterioration.

  • Use calm post-meal support and consistent bathroom or activity plans when ordered, explain their safety purpose, and avoid humiliation, confrontation, or surveillance beyond the least restrictive effective plan.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, marked weakness, reduced consciousness, or ECG abnormality

  • Severe dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, reduced urine output, uncontrolled purging, or acute kidney injury

  • Hematemesis, severe chest or abdominal pain after vomiting, dyspnea, fever, rigid abdomen, or signs of gastrointestinal perforation

  • Suicidal intent, recent serious self-harm, dangerous medication or insulin manipulation, poisoning, or inability to maintain safety

  • Pregnancy with repeated purging, dehydration, syncope, bleeding, reduced fetal movement, or other obstetric concern

Complications

  • Hypokalemia and other electrolyte or acid-base disorders, dehydration, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, syncope, seizure, and sudden death

  • Dental erosion, caries, salivary swelling, pharyngitis, reflux, esophagitis, Mallory-Weiss bleeding, rare esophageal rupture, gastric dilation, and bowel dysfunction

  • Edema during behavior change, menstrual or endocrine disturbance, nutritional deficiency, aspiration, and medication or substance toxicity

  • Depression, anxiety, substance use, self-harm, suicide, financial strain, isolation, and relapse

Prognosis

Many patients substantially improve or recover with specialized treatment, although relapse and crossover to another eating-disorder presentation can occur. Earlier reduction of restriction, bingeing, and purging lowers medical risk. Recovery includes normalized eating, reduced overvaluation and compensation, medical healing, and restored function rather than weight or abstinence for one brief interval alone.

Prevention

  • Identify dieting, bingeing, vomiting, laxative or diuretic use, driven exercise, and dental or electrolyte clues early without waiting for weight change.

  • Use regular adequate meals and snacks, body-neutral care, nonstigmatizing language, safe medication access, and rapid follow-up after relapse warning signs.

  • Teach that laxatives do not remove most absorbed calories and can cause dangerous fluid, electrolyte, renal, and bowel injury.

Patient Education

  • Bulimia nervosa can be medically serious at any weight. Tell the team honestly about vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, exercise, water loading, insulin or medicines because each method changes the risks and tests.

  • Fluoxetine can reduce adult bulimia symptoms when prescribed, but therapy and regular nutrition remain central. Do not start, stop, or borrow medicine without the prescriber.

  • Bupropion should not be used if you currently have or previously had bulimia nervosa or anorexia nervosa because seizure risk is higher.

  • Seek emergency help for fainting, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, vomiting blood, severe chest or abdominal pain after vomiting, suicidal intent, or inability to keep fluids down.

Special Populations

  • Adolescents need growth, puberty, school, family, confidentiality, and safeguarding assessment. Involved caregivers can support family-based treatment without being blamed for the illness.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require obstetric coordination for hydration, electrolytes, nutrition, fetal growth, medication, vomiting differential, mood, and relapse risk.

  • Men, older adults, athletes, larger-bodied patients, and gender-diverse patients may be missed because weight and gender stereotypes obscure binge-purge behavior.

  • Diabetes requires urgent review of insulin restriction or overdose, glucose and ketones, and a coordinated diabetes-eating-disorder plan.

Nclex Traps

  • Average or high weight does not exclude bulimia nervosa or dangerous hypokalemia.

  • Bulimia includes recurrent compensation; binge-eating disorder does not include routine compensatory behavior.

  • Do not reassure from one normal potassium result when purging continues or symptoms change.

  • Fluoxetine is an evidence-supported adult medication adjunct; medication does not replace CBT and nutrition care.

  • Bupropion is contraindicated with current or prior bulimia or anorexia because of seizure risk.
