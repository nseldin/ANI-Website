Definition

Frostbite develops when tissue freezes and microvascular flow fails, causing direct ice injury, endothelial thrombosis, and reperfusion damage after thawing. The characteristic pattern is numb waxy or hard pale skin that becomes painful, swollen, blistered, or cyanotic after rapid rewarming. Confirm or stage it with clinical depth and perfusion reassessment after thawing; distinguish it from frostnip, immersion foot, arterial occlusion, compartment syndrome, and burn injury by exposure, tissue freezing, pulses, and evolution after thawing. The priority is to protect from refreezing and rapidly rewarm in circulating warm water because tissue necrosis can complicate Frostbite. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • clinical depth and perfusion reassessment after thawing

  • core temperature, trauma, glucose, and systemic exposure assessment

  • vascular imaging only for severe deep injury when thrombolysis or operative planning is considered

  • Distinguish Frostbite from frostnip, immersion foot, arterial occlusion, compartment syndrome, and burn injury by exposure, tissue freezing, pulses, and evolution after thawing.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • protect from refreezing and rapidly rewarm in circulating warm water

  • provide analgesia, wound care, tetanus care, and specialist consultation

  • consider time-sensitive reperfusion therapy for selected severe deep frostbite

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for concurrent hypothermic instability, compartment signs, absent perfusion after thaw, rapidly spreading infection, sepsis, hemorrhagic blistering with deep injury, or a severe recent freeze because specialty thrombolysis or surgical evaluation may be time sensitive.

  • For suspected frostbite, assess exposure duration, refreezing risk, hypothermia, hydration, sensation, color, capillary refill, blisters, tissue firmness, and associated trauma because systemic cold threats come before definitive limb treatment.

  • Protect the frozen part from pressure and rubbing, remove constricting items, pad and elevate it, and do not thaw when refreezing remains likely because repeated freeze-thaw cycles deepen microvascular injury.

  • When refreezing is no longer possible, begin prescribed frostbite rewarming rapidly in a controlled circulating water bath, provide strong analgesia, and monitor temperature and tissue response because thawing is painful and uncontrolled heat can burn insensate skin.

  • Document serial photographs or wound findings, distal perfusion, sensation, edema, blister character, infection signs, and ordered imaging because tissue demarcation evolves and early appearance does not reliably define final viability.

Complications

  • tissue necrosis can complicate Frostbite.

  • infection can complicate Frostbite.

  • compartment syndrome can complicate Frostbite.

  • amputation can complicate Frostbite.

  • chronic neuropathic pain can complicate Frostbite.

  • systemic hypothermia and arrhythmia

  • refreezing injury after premature thawing

Red Flags

  • Hypothermic shock, unstable rhythm, or declining consciousness

  • Absent perfusion after thaw or rapidly worsening cyanosis

  • Tense swelling, pain out of proportion, weakness, or sensory loss

  • Spreading erythema, purulence, crepitus, fever, or systemic toxicity

Patient Education

  • Teach prevention with dry loose layers, mittens, foot and buddy checks, food and hydration, and immediate shelter for numb or pale skin because early cold injury may be painless.

  • Explain why the care team may delay amputation decisions: viable and nonviable tissue often separates over time unless infection or compartment pressure demands earlier surgery.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not rub frozen tissue or use dry direct heat

  • Do not thaw until refreezing can be prevented
