Used To Treat

A choice framework for primary aldosteronism, HFrEF, post-MI left-ventricular dysfunction, resistant hypertension, cirrhotic edema, CKD associated with type 2 diabetes, and heart failure with LVEF at least 40 percent. The indication belongs to the individual agent and trial population.

Description

Spironolactone, eplerenone, and finerenone all block the intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor, reducing aldosterone-driven potassium secretion and maladaptive inflammatory or fibrotic signaling. Spironolactone and eplerenone are steroidal MRAs, whereas finerenone is structurally nonsteroidal. Spironolactone is potent and broadly used but also binds androgen and progesterone receptors, explaining gynecomastia, sexual effects, and menstrual changes. Eplerenone is more selective and causes fewer endocrine effects, but it is a CYP3A substrate with indication-specific renal and potassium restrictions. Finerenone has high receptor selectivity and a distinct tissue distribution and evidence base. Its current U.S. label includes reducing kidney and cardiovascular outcomes in adults with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes and reducing cardiovascular death and heart-failure events in adults with heart failure and LVEF at least 40 percent. These drugs share hyperkalemia risk, but they are not milligram-equivalent and should not be swapped based solely on the word MRA.

Mechanism

All three prevent aldosterone or cortisol from activating the mineralocorticoid receptor, a nuclear transcription factor that promotes sodium transport, potassium secretion, inflammation, oxidative stress, hypertrophy, and fibrosis. Spironolactone is converted to active sulfur-containing metabolites, giving effects that outlast the parent drug and making endocrine cross-reactivity clinically visible. Eplerenone is less avid at androgen and progesterone receptors and is cleared largely through CYP3A. Finerenone is nonsteroidal, has no active metabolites, and produces a different receptor-cofactor conformation and heart-kidney distribution; its clinical benefit reflects anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic signaling rather than strong acute diuresis. Potassium rises because distal sodium uptake and the electrical drive for potassium secretion fall. Benefit and toxicity therefore emerge from the same receptor blockade, which is why scheduled potassium and eGFR checks are part of treatment rather than administrative extras.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: No class-wide U.S. boxed warning. Hyperkalemia can be fatal, and strong CYP3A inhibition is contraindicated with eplerenone or finerenone under their labeling because exposure rises and magnifies potassium risk.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyperkalemia, kidney-function change, hypotension, dizziness, and hyponatremia can occur across the class; risk increases with CKD, diabetes, RAAS blockade, older age, and potassium products.

  • Spironolactone causes the most endocrine adverse effects. Eplerenone and finerenone reduce that receptor cross-reactivity but do not remove potassium or interaction risk.

Contraindications

  • Potassium above the drug- and indication-specific start threshold, severe renal impairment, anuria, adrenal insufficiency, and strong CYP3A inhibitors are key label-dependent barriers.

  • Do not combine MRAs without a specialist rationale. Duplicate receptor blockade adds hyperkalemia and kidney risk without established routine benefit.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact indication before selecting or judging an MRA: a drug chosen for cirrhotic ascites, HFrEF, diabetic CKD, or HF with LVEF at least 40 percent is being used against different evidence and dosing rules.

  • Check potassium and eGFR before start and at the label-specified early interval after initiation or dose change. Reassess during acute illness, dehydration, or addition of an interacting drug.

Interactions

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, potassium products, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, tacrolimus, and cyclosporine amplify hyperkalemia. Strong CYP3A inhibitors are contraindicated with eplerenone or finerenone.

  • Spironolactone has active metabolites and endocrine receptor interactions; eplerenone and finerenone have important CYP3A exposure interactions, so the cleanest endocrine profile is not the simplest medication reconciliation.

Key Labs

  • Potassium, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, bicarbonate, sodium, pressure, and indication-specific albuminuria or heart-failure trajectory.

  • A mild early eGFR change may accompany hemodynamic therapy, but progressive decline or rising potassium requires dose and contributing-factor review.

Nclex Traps

  • Nonsteroidal describes finerenone's chemistry and receptor pharmacology; it does not mean nonhormonal or potassium neutral.

  • The principal HFrEF benefit of an MRA is not simply extra urine. Blocking mineralocorticoid-driven remodeling and fibrosis changes outcomes.
