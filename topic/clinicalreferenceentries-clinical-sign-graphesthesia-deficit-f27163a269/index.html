Definition

Graphesthesia deficit is a bedside cortical sensory sign. It is a clue to contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction. A positive finding means the patient cannot identify the traced symbol.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Graphesthesia deficit means the patient cannot identify the traced symbol.

Why It Matters

The observed Graphesthesia deficit finding can signal contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction. Primary touch reaches cortex, but parietal association networks cannot integrate the spatial pattern. Graphesthesia deficit cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction

Assessment Method

With eyes closed, trace a familiar number or letter on the palm after confirming primary sensation.

Positive Finding

The patient cannot identify the traced symbol.

Mechanism

Primary touch reaches cortex, but parietal association networks cannot integrate the spatial pattern.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Graphesthesia deficit finding raises concern for contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction because primary touch reaches cortex, but parietal association networks cannot integrate the spatial pattern. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Graphesthesia deficit does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Graphesthesia deficit to focus evaluation of contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: peripheral neuropathy, aphasia, poor symbol knowledge. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: With eyes closed, trace a familiar number or letter on the palm after confirming primary sensation. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Graphesthesia deficit as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

The test is invalid when primary sensation, language, or familiarity with symbols is impaired. Therefore, Graphesthesia deficit cannot confirm or exclude contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • peripheral neuropathy

  • aphasia

  • poor symbol knowledge

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as peripheral neuropathy, aphasia, poor symbol knowledge.

False Negative Situations

Graphesthesia deficit may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction.

Modern Status

Current cortical sensory adjunct. When performed as described, Graphesthesia deficit can adjust concern for contralateral parietal cortical dysfunction, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Graphesthesia deficit: The test is invalid when primary sensation, language, or familiarity with symbols is impaired.

Safety Precautions

Observe Graphesthesia deficit with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: With eyes closed, trace a familiar number or letter on the palm after confirming primary sensation. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Confirm the patient can feel and name simple touch before testing cortical interpretation.
