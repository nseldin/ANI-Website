Definition

Stroke is sudden brain injury caused either by blocked arterial blood flow (ischemic stroke) or bleeding into or around the brain (hemorrhagic stroke). Both mechanisms interrupt neural networks and can cause abrupt focal neurologic deficits such as weakness, numbness, language change, visual loss, neglect, or imbalance; severe headache, seizure, or reduced consciousness may also occur. A transient ischemic attack produces a temporary focal ischemic syndrome without established infarction but remains an emergency warning because the responsible vascular source may still cause a completed stroke. Symptoms alone cannot safely identify the type, so emergency imaging must separate occlusion from bleeding before treatment paths diverge.

Pathology

In ischemia, an occluded vessel creates an irreversibly injured core surrounded by potentially salvageable penumbra supported by collateral flow. In hemorrhage, extravasated blood directly injures tissue, raises intracranial pressure, distorts anatomy, and can obstruct CSF or provoke vasospasm depending on location. Both pathways can produce edema, herniation, seizure, aspiration, immobility, and permanent network loss, but reperfusion therapy that helps occlusion can worsen active bleeding.

Pathophysiology

The brain depends on continuous oxygen and glucose because it has little energy reserve. With arterial occlusion, ATP failure disables ion pumps, causing depolarization, calcium influx, excitotoxicity, cytotoxic edema, and progressive recruitment of penumbra into infarct. With vessel rupture, blood dissects through tissue or enters subarachnoid spaces, creating mass effect, inflammation, hydrocephalus, and impaired perfusion. The first emergency goals are therefore shared - airway, oxygenation, glucose, time, focused neurologic examination, blood pressure, medication history, and rapid CT - but definitive care separates quickly: eligible ischemic stroke may need IV thrombolysis or thrombectomy, while hemorrhage may need anticoagulant reversal, pressure control, neurosurgery, aneurysm treatment, or CSF diversion. TIA still requires urgent source evaluation because temporary reperfusion can precede recurrence.

Etiology

Ischemic stroke arises from cardiac embolism, artery-to-artery embolism, local atherosclerotic thrombosis, penetrating small-vessel disease, dissection, hypercoagulability, or another arterial mechanism. Hemorrhagic stroke arises from hypertensive small-vessel rupture, aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, trauma, anticoagulation, tumor, venous disease, or coagulopathy. A TIA shares ischemic causes but improves before lasting infarction is demonstrated, so resolution does not make the source harmless.

Risk Factors

  • Hypertension injures small and large vessels, increasing both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke risk.

  • Atrial fibrillation, carotid or intracranial atherosclerosis, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, and prior TIA or stroke increase ischemic risk because they promote embolism or thrombosis.

  • Aneurysm, vascular malformation, amyloid angiopathy, anticoagulant exposure, stimulant use, and coagulopathy increase hemorrhagic risk because vessel rupture or uncontrolled bleeding becomes more likely.

Signs Symptoms

  • Use BE-FAST for sudden Balance loss, Eye or vision change, Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech trouble, and Time to call emergency services.

  • Neglect, numbness, dysphagia, severe ataxia, diplopia, abrupt confusion, seizure, or reduced consciousness can also be stroke because not every vascular territory produces classic face-arm-speech findings.

  • A thunderclap or worst sudden headache with vomiting or reduced consciousness raises concern for hemorrhage, but absence of headache does not prove ischemia.

Diagnostics

  • Document exact last-known-well, discovery time, baseline function, medications and anticoagulants, glucose, BP, and item-level neurologic deficits because treatment selection depends on time, disability, and bleeding context.

  • Obtain emergency noncontrast CT or appropriate MRI to identify hemorrhage and ischemic burden; add CTA or MRA when vessel occlusion, dissection, or aneurysm is possible.

  • Use perfusion or diffusion-mismatch imaging for selected unknown-onset or extended-window ischemic pathways, while ECG, telemetry, vascular imaging, echocardiography, and targeted labs investigate cause after immediate stabilization.

Labs

  • Bedside glucose is immediate because hypoglycemia can mimic focal stroke and injure brain, while persistent deficit after correction still requires imaging.

  • CBC, platelets, coagulation testing, electrolytes, renal function, oxygenation, and pregnancy context are selected for reperfusion, bleeding, contrast, and mimic safety without creating avoidable imaging delay.

  • Lipids, A1c, rhythm data, and targeted etiologic testing guide prevention because the mechanism, not the word stroke alone, determines long-term therapy.

Treatments

  • Activate emergency stroke systems and use ambulance transport because prearrival notification, destination choice, and parallel evaluation preserve time-sensitive options.

  • For ischemic stroke, restore flow with IV thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy when the current guideline, imaging, disability, label, and protocol establish benefit over bleeding risk.

  • For hemorrhagic stroke, stop the bleeding pathway, reverse relevant anticoagulation, control pressure through the specific protocol, manage ICP or hydrocephalus, and obtain neurosurgical or endovascular care because clot-dissolving treatment would worsen active bleeding.

Nursing Priorities

  • Use sudden balance, vision, face, arm, speech, sensory, coordination, and consciousness changes to recognize possible stroke because ischemia and hemorrhage both demand immediate imaging rather than bedside certainty.

  • Establish exact last-known-well, baseline function, glucose, anticoagulants, blood pressure, recent surgery or bleeding, and transport destination because these facts determine reperfusion, reversal, and neurosurgical pathways.

  • Activate emergency stroke care, maintain nothing-by-mouth status, protect the airway and weak limbs, and avoid empiric aspirin before imaging because aspiration and unrecognized intracranial bleeding can worsen outcomes.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, blood pressure, rhythm, oxygenation, glucose, temperature, swallowing, headache, and seizure activity because neurologic injury and treatment eligibility can change minute by minute.

  • Escalate immediately for any new focal deficit, NIH Stroke Scale increase, declining consciousness, unequal pupils, severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, aspiration, or airway failure because occlusion, hemorrhage, edema, and herniation are time-critical emergencies.

Complications

  • Ischemic core expansion, hemorrhagic transformation, cerebral edema, and herniation can enlarge neurologic injury.

  • Rebleeding, vasospasm, hydrocephalus, or mass effect can follow hemorrhagic stroke depending on location and cause.

  • Aspiration, pneumonia, DVT/PE, falls, pressure injury, depression, seizures, language loss, neglect, visual loss, cognitive impairment, and long-term disability can follow either type.

Contraindications

  • Do not give thrombolysis, antiplatelet therapy, food, drink, or oral medication before the required hemorrhage and swallowing safety decisions.

  • Do not wait for symptoms to resolve or drive privately because transient improvement can represent TIA, collateral fluctuation, or temporary recanalization.

  • Do not infer stroke type from blood pressure, headache, age, or symptom severity because ischemia and hemorrhage overlap clinically.

Red Flags

  • Sudden facial droop, unilateral weakness, aphasia, visual loss, neglect, or ataxia

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, recurrent focal deficit, or rapidly declining consciousness

  • Thunderclap headache, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, posturing, or seizure

  • Aspiration, inability to protect the airway, severe hypoxemia, or hemodynamic instability

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for any sudden stroke sign even if it disappears because transient symptoms may precede permanent disability.

  • Write down the last time the person was definitely normal and bring the medication list because time and anticoagulants guide emergency treatment.

Nclex Traps

  • Stroke is an emergency syndrome; imaging establishes ischemic versus hemorrhagic type.

  • TIA symptoms resolving is not reassurance or permission to delay.

  • Posterior stroke can present with balance, eye, swallowing, or consciousness findings without classic unilateral weakness.

Source Note

Educational umbrella synthesis grounded in the 2026 AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline (https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-early-management-of-patients-with-acute-ischemic-stroke/top-things-to-know), the 2022 AHA/ASA spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage guideline (https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2022-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-spontaneous-intracerebral-hemorrhage/top-things-to-know), the 2023 AHA/ASA aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage guideline (https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2023-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-aneurysmal-subarachnoid-hemorrhage/top-things-to-know), and NINDS stroke assessment resources (https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/stroke/assess-and-treat). It distinguishes occlusive, intraparenchymal-bleeding, subarachnoid-bleeding, and transient ischemic pathways for education; emergency imaging and the treating stroke team determine an individual's diagnosis and care.

Swallowing Connections

  • Stroke can cause dysphagia, aphasia, both, or neither. Complete a validated dysphagia screen before food, fluid, or oral medication, or keep the patient NPO until safety is established; formal evaluation determines individualized food texture, liquid consistency, route, and strategies.
