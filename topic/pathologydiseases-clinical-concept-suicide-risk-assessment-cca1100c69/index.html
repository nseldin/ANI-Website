Plain Language

A suicide risk assessment is a direct clinical conversation that examines suicidal thoughts, plans, intent, past and recent behavior, access to lethal means, mental and medical conditions, protective factors, and the person's current ability to stay safe. It guides immediate care, but it cannot predict with certainty who will or will not attempt suicide.

Why It Matters

A person may deny suicidal thoughts yet have recent preparatory behavior, a serious attempt, intoxication, psychosis, or collateral warning that changes urgency. A careful assessment links current evidence to observation, emergency treatment, lethal-means safety, safety planning, and follow-up instead of relying on a diagnosis, score, or promise alone.

Definition

Suicide risk assessment is an evidence-informed, person-specific formulation of current and longer-term suicide risk. It follows a positive screen, a disclosed concern, warning signs, self-directed violence, or clinical concern and includes ideation, plan, method access, intent, behavior, past attempts, psychiatric and medical state, substance use, stressors, protective factors, collateral information, and the care environment. It is not a diagnosis, a mathematical prediction, or a one-time clearance decision.

Pathology

The assessment has no disease mechanism. Suicide risk arises from changing interactions among acute symptoms, intent, behavior, access to lethal means, past history, substance use, medical illness, pain, losses, relationships, social conditions, and supports. Static risk factors inform context; current warning signs and dynamic changes guide immediate action. Protective factors can help but do not cancel a plan, intent, recent attempt, or lethal access.

Pathophysiology

  • Acute hopelessness, agitation, insomnia, intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis, mania, mixed mood states, severe anxiety, pain, and cognitive constriction can reduce flexible problem solving and increase short-term danger.

  • Access to a highly lethal method can shorten the time between thought and action, so means access and the person's ability to use the method are assessed directly and addressed collaboratively.

  • Risk changes with treatment, sobriety, sleep, interpersonal events, discharge, access to means, and new information; repeated assessment is more clinically useful than treating one score as permanent.

Etiology

Not applicable as a diagnosis. Assessment is indicated after suicidal ideation, an attempt or interrupted or aborted attempt, preparatory behavior, non-suicidal self-injury, severe depression, psychosis, mania, intoxication or withdrawal, major loss, a concerning screening result, collateral concern, or any clinician concern about self-directed harm.

Risk Factors

  • Previous suicide attempt or recent self-directed violence, especially with high medical lethality, persistent intent, concealment, rehearsal, or rescue avoidance

  • Current suicidal thoughts with plan, intent, access, preparatory acts, escalating frequency or intensity, inability to delay action, or a belief that the act will be lethal

  • Depression, bipolar mixed or manic state, psychosis, command hallucinations, severe anxiety or agitation, substance use, withdrawal, insomnia, chronic pain, traumatic loss, or serious medical illness

  • Firearm or other lethal-means access, recent discharge or care transition, social isolation, housing or legal crisis, abuse, discrimination, bereavement, financial loss, or treatment interruption

  • Protective factors that are weak, unavailable in the current crisis, or overwhelmed; protective factors reduce risk only when the person can actually use them

Signs Symptoms

  • Talking or writing about death, suicide, hopelessness, unbearable pain, being a burden, or having no reason to live

  • Seeking or arranging a method, giving away valued belongings, saying goodbye, making a will unexpectedly, rehearsing, or taking steps to avoid discovery or rescue

  • A recent attempt, interrupted or aborted attempt, escalating self-harm, abrupt withdrawal, severe agitation, intoxication, command hallucinations, or sudden behavioral change

  • A sudden calm or apparent improvement after intense distress, especially when access, intent, or a plan has not been reassessed

Diagnostics

  • Begin with direct, private, nonjudgmental questions about desire to die, suicidal thoughts, frequency, intensity, duration, controllability, plan, timing, place, method, access, preparatory behavior, intent, expected lethality, ambivalence, and reasons for living.

  • Clarify past attempts, interrupted and aborted attempts, rehearsals, medical lethality, rescue circumstances, non-suicidal self-injury, family suicide history, and what has changed since the most dangerous point.

  • Assess current depression, mania or mixed symptoms, psychosis, anxiety, agitation, sleep, cognition, pain, intoxication, withdrawal, medicines, medical illness, trauma, abuse, recent losses, legal or housing stress, supports, and treatment connection.

  • Use a validated screen or structured framework when appropriate, but determine overall risk through clinical formulation. There is no universal score that independently assigns discharge, admission, observation, or involuntary care.

  • Document acute and chronic risk, protective factors, rationale, interventions, lethal-means plan, observation, disposition, follow-up, and triggers for reassessment.

Labs

  • No laboratory, imaging, genetic, or questionnaire result rules suicide risk in or out.

  • Use targeted glucose, oxygenation, toxicology, alcohol level, medication level, metabolic, pregnancy, ECG, neurologic, or other studies when intoxication, overdose, withdrawal, delirium, medical illness, or treatment toxicity may impair safety or capacity.

  • Do not delay immediate protection while waiting for routine results when current intent, dangerous behavior, a recent attempt, or medical instability is present.

Assessment

Ask directly and listen without shock. Establish current location and immediate danger; ensure medical stabilization after any attempt; assess ideation, plan, method, access, intent, behavior and ability to delay action; review past attempts and recent changes; assess psychiatric, substance-related and medical contributors; identify usable protective factors and supports; obtain collateral when necessary; then document a risk formulation and matched intervention. Reassess after treatment, sobriety, sleep, new information, transfer, leave, or discharge planning.

Differential

  • Passive wish for death, active suicidal ideation, preparatory behavior, suicide attempt, interrupted or aborted attempt, and non-suicidal self-injury, which differ in intent but can coexist

  • Intrusive ego-dystonic harm thoughts in OCD, trauma images, psychotic commands or delusions, impulsive intoxicated behavior, and factitious or externally motivated presentation; none should be dismissed without safety assessment

  • Accidental overdose or injury, medication error, delirium, dementia, developmental or communication differences, and culturally shaped expressions of distress

  • Homicidal intent, coercion, abuse, trafficking, unsafe home environment, and danger to dependents that require parallel assessment

Treatments

  • For current intent, a feasible plan, recent serious behavior, inability to delay action, severe intoxication, psychosis, mania, or inability to maintain safety, provide emergency psychiatric treatment in a secure clinically appropriate setting and treat medical consequences immediately.

  • Use the least restrictive observation and environment that can reliably prevent access to lethal means and permit immediate intervention; reassess frequently and explain the plan.

  • Create a collaborative written safety or crisis response plan with warning signs, internal coping steps, people and places for distraction, supports to contact, professional resources, and a specific lethal-means safety plan; a plan supplements care and never proves low risk.

  • Treat the underlying disorder and contributors with evidence-based psychotherapy, substance-use treatment, sleep and pain care, and diagnosis-specific medication. No medication is selected solely from a suicide-risk label or screening score.

  • Use a warm handoff, rapid follow-up, caring contacts when supported by the care system, medication supply planning, and clear return instructions after discharge.

Contraindications

  • Do not use a no-suicide contract or verbal promise as a substitute for assessment, observation, safety planning, or treatment.

  • Do not classify risk or discharge a patient from one score, one denial, one protective factor, one diagnosis, or apparent calm.

  • Do not assume asking directly creates suicidal thoughts, and do not use euphemisms that leave plan, intent, behavior, or access unclear.

  • Do not count family presence as protection without confirming willingness, reliability, safe transport, lethal-means control, and ability to obtain urgent help.

  • Do not prescribe a large potentially lethal medication supply or return unrestricted lethal-means access without an individualized safety and follow-up plan.

Safety Considerations

  • Maintain continuous direct observation and emergency response capability when the person may act imminently or cannot reliably seek help; follow the setting's written policy rather than inventing an interval.

  • Use collaborative lethal-means safety counseling for firearms, medications, toxic substances, heights, vehicles, and other identified methods; temporary off-site secure storage may be appropriate where lawful and feasible.

  • Protect privacy but obtain necessary collateral or disclose the minimum necessary information under emergency safety and safeguarding rules when there is imminent danger.

  • Reassess before discharge, after a denied request or upsetting call, after visitors, after medication or sobriety changes, after attempted elopement, and at every handoff.

Nursing Priorities

  • Triage medical injury and immediate means access, stay with a patient at imminent risk, summon help, and initiate the ordered observation and environmental safety plan.

  • Document the patient's exact words, time frame, plan, method access, intent, preparatory and past behavior, deterrents, supports, collateral, clinical rationale, and actions taken.

  • Ask in plain language and remain calm; validate pain and hope for help without minimizing, debating, promising secrecy, or giving false reassurance.

  • Reconcile medicines and quantities, assess alcohol and drug use, withdrawal, psychosis, mania, agitation, insomnia, pain, abuse, housing and transport, and involve the interdisciplinary team.

  • Complete a closed-loop handoff that names current risk, observation, access restrictions, pending medical issues, legal status, safety-plan status, and accepting clinician or service.

Red Flags

  • Current intent with a feasible plan or method access, recent attempt or preparatory act, rehearsal, concealment, rescue avoidance, or inability to delay action

  • Command hallucination, delusional suicide belief, severe agitation, mania or mixed state, intoxication or withdrawal, rapidly escalating insomnia, or profound hopelessness

  • Firearm access, a large medication supply, toxic substance access, recent violent loss, impending discharge or eviction, or sudden loss of the only support

  • Refusal of needed medical care after an attempt, unstable vital signs, altered consciousness, overdose, injury, pregnancy or postpartum crisis, or danger to a child or dependent adult

Complications

  • Suicide, recurrent attempt, increasingly lethal behavior, accidental injury, overdose complications, and harm to others during a crisis

  • False reassurance from a negative screen or denial, or unnecessary coercion from a score treated as a prediction

  • Loss of trust, nondisclosure, stigma, unsafe discharge, failed follow-up, medication interruption, and repeat emergency care

  • Family trauma and unsafe transfer of monitoring responsibility without training, consent, resources, or a workable means-safety plan

Prognosis

Risk is dynamic, not destiny. Many people recover from suicidal crises, and direct disclosure creates an opportunity for effective protection and treatment. Past attempts and chronic vulnerabilities still matter, but the immediate plan should be updated as symptoms, access, supports, treatment engagement, and environment change.

Prevention

  • Use validated screening in appropriate settings with a defined response pathway, then complete an evidence-based clinical assessment when a screen is positive or concern persists.

  • Treat depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, substance use, trauma, insomnia, pain, and medical illness and address housing, food, discrimination, violence, and access barriers.

  • Develop a collaborative safety plan and lethal-means safety steps before crisis, especially after an attempt, hospitalization, emergency visit, major loss, or medication change.

  • Provide rapid follow-up and reliable transitions after emergency or inpatient care, when risk may change abruptly.

Patient Education

  • You will be asked direct questions about thoughts, plans, actions, and access because the safest plan depends on what is happening now; honest answers help clinicians match support to risk.

  • A screening score is not a diagnosis or a guarantee. Tell staff about a recent attempt, method access, worsening intent, command voices, intoxication, or a change after the screen.

  • A safety plan is a practical step-by-step guide, not a promise. Keep it accessible, practice how to use it, and include specific temporary steps for firearms, medicines, and other lethal means.

  • In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support. Call 911 or go to an emergency department for immediate danger, an attempt, overdose, serious injury, or inability to stay safe.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require developmentally appropriate private assessment, caregiver collateral, bullying and abuse review, school and home safety, and careful distinction between non-suicidal self-injury and suicidal behavior.

  • Older adults require assessment of pain, illness burden, bereavement, isolation, firearm access, medication quantities, cognitive change, delirium, and ability to use supports.

  • Pregnant and postpartum patients require direct suicide, psychosis, bipolar, infant-harm, intimate-partner violence, obstetric, medication, sleep, and support assessment.

  • People with intellectual, language, communication, hearing, vision, autism, or cognitive needs require accessible questions and collateral without treating limited speech as absence of risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Ask directly about suicide; do not avoid the word or assume the question causes risk.

  • A patient who denies ideation can still be high risk after a recent attempt, preparatory behavior, psychosis, intoxication, or credible collateral report.

  • Protective factors do not erase current plan, intent, behavior, or lethal access.

  • A no-suicide contract is not a safety plan and does not replace observation or assessment.

  • Document the clinical rationale and intervention, not only high, moderate, low, or a numeric score.

Source Note

Original ANI nursing synthesis. VA/DoD supports validated screening, comprehensive assessment across self-directed behavior, psychiatric, medical and social domains, clinical formulation, reassessment, matched care, and lethal-means safety. SAFE-T supports direct inquiry, risk and protective factors, clinical judgment, documentation, and safety planning. Columbia supports structured ideation and behavior inquiry. Joint Commission supports evidence-based assessment after a positive screen, documented risk rationale, environmental mitigation, monitoring, reassessment, and discharge follow-up.
