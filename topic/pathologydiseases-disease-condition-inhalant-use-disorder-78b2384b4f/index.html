Plain Language

Inhalant use disorder is a treatable illness in which volatile chemicals—such as solvents, fuels, glues, or aerosol propellants—are intentionally breathed for a mind-altering effect in a pattern that becomes hard to control and causes harm. Accidental workplace exposure or use of every drug taken by inhalation is not this diagnosis.

Why It Matters

Inhalants can cause a fatal abnormal heart rhythm, suffocation, seizure, coma, burns, and permanent brain or organ injury—even during a first episode. The exposure scene can also endanger rescuers, so fresh-air removal, ignition control, emergency response, and poison consultation come before a lengthy diagnostic interview.

Definition

Inhalant use disorder is a problematic pattern of intentional use of a volatile hydrocarbon-based inhalant causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR uses at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months and a 10-feature framework because it does not include an inhalant withdrawal criterion. The identity does not automatically cover nitrites, nitrous oxide, anesthetic gases, smoked drugs, or accidental environmental exposure.

Pathology

No single odor, residue, blood level, or toxicology test diagnoses the disorder. Inhaled solvents and propellants enter the bloodstream rapidly and can depress the brain, sensitize the heart to fatal rhythms, displace oxygen, and injure white matter, peripheral nerves, liver, kidney, blood cells, lungs, skin, and other organs depending on the chemical and exposure.

Pathophysiology

  • Different inhalants contain different hydrocarbons and additives, so toxicity is not one uniform syndrome. Rapid absorption produces brief intoxication and repeated dosing, while hypoxia and direct cellular toxicity can damage the brain and organs.

  • Intoxication may cause dizziness, slurred speech, unsteady gait, poor coordination, nystagmus, lethargy, impaired judgment, muscle weakness, tremor, or euphoria; severe exposure can cause arrhythmia, seizure, respiratory depression, coma, or death.

  • DSM-5-TR does not define an inhalant withdrawal syndrome. Heavy users may report nonspecific sleep, mood, nausea, or craving symptoms, but seizures or delirium after stopping require assessment for another substance, poisoning complication, or medical disorder rather than an invented standard withdrawal protocol.

Etiology

Risk reflects easy access to household or industrial products, rapid effects, adolescent experimentation, poverty and housing instability, trauma, peer context, other substance use, neurodevelopmental or psychiatric illness, and low awareness of immediate lethality. The product's ordinary household purpose does not make intentional inhalation safe, and accidental occupational exposure must be assessed as an exposure rather than labeled addiction.

Risk Factors

  • Adolescence, early substance exposure, repeated use alone or in enclosed spaces, bagging or huffing, and access to fuels, solvents, glues, correction fluids, cleaners, or aerosols

  • Prior inhalant intoxication, syncope, seizure, arrhythmia, burn, hypoxic event, or escalating frequency and product mixing

  • Trauma, depression, suicide risk, developmental or learning difficulty, other substance use, unstable housing, and poor supervision or treatment access

  • Cardiac, lung, neurologic, liver, kidney, or blood disease; pregnancy; malnutrition; and occupational or environmental exposure

Signs Symptoms

  • Impaired control, craving, unsuccessful attempts to stop, substantial time obtaining or recovering, role failure, risky use, and continued use despite cognitive, medical, relationship, school, or work harm

  • Chemical odor, paint or solvent stains, hidden cans or rags, perioral irritation, headache, nausea, dizziness, slurred speech, nystagmus, unsteady gait, poor coordination, lethargy, or disinhibition

  • Severe poisoning with palpitations, syncope, arrhythmia, chest pain, seizure, respiratory depression, cyanosis, coma, chemical burn, frostbite, aspiration, or trauma

  • Chronic cognitive decline, poor school or work function, personality or mood change, ataxia, weakness, numbness, tremor, vision or hearing change, weight loss, or organ dysfunction

Diagnostics

  • Identify the exact product, ingredients or safety-data information if available, route and method, location and ventilation, duration, last exposure, co-use, intent, fire or explosion risk, symptoms, prior episodes, and each use-disorder feature over the same 12 months.

  • Confirm at least 2 qualifying DSM-5-TR features from the 10-feature framework and intentional psychoactive use. Do not add a withdrawal criterion or label accidental occupational or household exposure as a use disorder.

  • Assess current inhalant intoxication, poisoning, hypoxia, arrhythmia, trauma, burns, aspiration, suicide attempt, and environmental exposure separately. Determine whether other people at the scene may also be exposed.

  • Routine urine drug screens usually do not identify common volatile inhalants. Use Poison Help or medical toxicology and product-specific testing when it will change management; a chemical odor or negative screen is not diagnostic.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses inhalant use disorder. In acute exposure, use continuous rhythm and respiratory monitoring and select glucose, electrolytes, renal and liver measures, blood gas, lactate, creatine kinase, urinalysis, carboxyhemoglobin or methemoglobin, chest imaging, and other tests from the product and presentation.

  • Evaluate unexplained acidosis, hypokalemia, renal tubular findings, hepatitis, bone-marrow suppression, neuropathy, or cerebellar findings with product-specific toxicology guidance because particular chemicals cause different organ patterns.

  • Pregnancy testing, trauma imaging, burn assessment, and occupational or environmental evaluation are added when relevant; do not delay stabilization while searching for a confirmatory level that may not exist.

Assessment

Protect the rescuer first: do not enter an unsafe vapor-filled area or create a flame or spark. Move the patient to fresh air only when safe, activate emergency services, assess airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, ventilation, glucose, rhythm, temperature, neurologic status, trauma, burns, and co-exposures, and contact Poison Help or toxicology with the product information. Assess diagnostic features only after immediate hazards are controlled.

Differential

Differentiate inhalant intoxication from alcohol or sedative intoxication, opioid overdose, hypoglycemia, carbon monoxide or cyanide exposure, seizure, stroke, head injury, infection, and metabolic disease. Distinguish intentional volatile-hydrocarbon use from accidental occupational exposure and from nitrite, nitrous oxide, anesthetic-gas, smoked, or other inhaled-drug disorders that may have different diagnoses and treatments.

Treatments

  • For acute exposure, stop ongoing exposure without endangering the rescuer, provide fresh air and airway or ventilation support, continuous rhythm and neurologic monitoring, decontamination appropriate to the product, and poison or toxicology-guided treatment of arrhythmia, seizure, burn, aspiration, and organ injury.

  • Do not induce vomiting after ingestion or mixed exposure. Remove contaminated clothing and irrigate exposed skin or eyes according to poison guidance while preventing secondary staff exposure.

  • No medication is FDA approved specifically for inhalant use disorder. Use behavioral treatment, motivational work, family-based care for many youths, treatment of co-occurring psychiatric and substance disorders, and environmental access planning.

  • Provide rehabilitation and specialist follow-up for cognitive, neurologic, cardiac, pulmonary, liver, kidney, hearing, vision, burn, or functional complications.

Contraindications

  • Do not enter a contaminated enclosed space without appropriate rescue training and protection, and do not use flames, smoke, switch electrical equipment unnecessarily, or create sparks near volatile products.

  • Do not induce vomiting or give food, drink, or an improvised antidote unless Poison Help or the responsible clinician directs it.

  • Do not rely on a routine urine drug screen or lack of odor to exclude inhalant exposure, and do not delay airway or rhythm care for laboratory confirmation.

  • Do not treat all inhaled substances as volatile-hydrocarbon inhalants; nitrites, nitrous oxide, anesthetic gases, and smoked drugs can require different diagnostic and emergency pathways.

Safety Considerations

  • Continuous ECG and respiratory observation may be needed because fatal rhythms, hypoxia, and recurrent neurologic deterioration can occur even after a brief exposure; disposition depends on the chemical, symptoms, ECG, organ findings, and toxicology guidance.

  • Minimize fear, exertion, and unnecessary struggle while maintaining safety, and use clinician-directed rhythm treatment because some hydrocarbon exposures alter cardiac electrical stability.

  • Assess suicide intent, coercion, assault, child safety, housing ventilation, workplace exposure, and whether contaminated clothing or the scene endangers others.

  • Secure fuels, solvents, and aerosols when possible without transferring the patient to more dangerous chemicals; involve caregivers, school, workplace, or housing services with consent and safeguarding rules.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm scene safety and ignition control, remove ongoing exposure only when safe, call emergency support, and bring the product container or ingredient information without contaminating the care area.

  • Support airway and ventilation, place on continuous cardiac monitoring when indicated, obtain glucose and temperature, assess trauma, skin and eye injury, neurologic status, and trend for arrhythmia, seizure, hypoxia, and aspiration.

  • Document exact product, method, place, ventilation, timing, duration, intentional versus accidental exposure, co-use, symptoms, response, suicide risk, and others potentially exposed.

  • Before discharge, confirm safe housing and product storage, poison and emergency instructions, cognitive and organ follow-up, adolescent and family support when relevant, and a behavioral treatment appointment.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, palpitations, chest pain, dangerous rhythm, seizure, cyanosis, slow or absent breathing, coma, or sudden collapse

  • Enclosed-space exposure, fire or explosion, multiple affected people, suspected suicide attempt, or a contaminated rescuer or care area

  • Severe burn or frostbite, aspiration, trauma, focal neurologic change, persistent ataxia, weakness, vision change, or hearing loss

  • Dark urine, oliguria, severe acidosis or electrolyte change, jaundice, bleeding, pregnancy with symptoms, or progressive cognitive decline

Complications

  • Sudden sniffing death from fatal arrhythmia, respiratory arrest, asphyxia, aspiration, seizure, coma, and hypoxic brain injury

  • Burns, frostbite, explosion injury, falls, motor-vehicle trauma, suffocation from bagging, and poisoning of bystanders or rescuers

  • Persistent cognitive impairment, white-matter and cerebellar injury, peripheral neuropathy, hearing or vision injury, and functional decline

  • Liver, kidney, lung, bone-marrow, muscle, and cardiac injury; school or work loss; depression, suicide, and other substance use

Prognosis

A single exposure can be fatal, but stopping exposure and engaging in treatment can prevent further injury. Some cognitive and organ effects improve over time, while severe hypoxic, neurologic, auditory, visual, renal, or hepatic injury may persist. Continued follow-up should assess function and return to use, not just whether an acute toxicology test is negative.

Prevention

  • Store fuels, solvents, glues, and aerosols securely; ensure ventilation and occupational controls; and teach that ordinary household products can be immediately lethal when intentionally inhaled.

  • Use developmentally appropriate early screening and address trauma, mood, learning, family, school, housing, and other substance-use needs without shaming.

  • Post Poison Help and emergency information and teach families and workers not to create ignition sources or enter unsafe enclosed spaces during a rescue.

Patient Education

  • Inhalants can stop the heart or breathing even the first time; a familiar household product is not safe to breathe for an effect.

  • Call emergency services for fainting, palpitations, seizure, breathing trouble, confusion, or collapse. Do not create a spark or enter a vapor-filled area unless trained and protected.

  • Do not make someone vomit after an exposure. Contact Poison Help with the product name or container if it can be obtained safely.

  • A routine drug screen may be negative even after a serious inhalant exposure, so symptoms and the product history matter.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need confidential, developmentally appropriate assessment, caregiver involvement when safe, school and learning evaluation, product-access controls, and trauma and suicide screening.

  • Pregnancy requires emergency stabilization and obstetric assessment when symptomatic; possible fetal exposure does not change the need to treat maternal hypoxia, arrhythmia, or poisoning promptly.

  • Workers and people in poorly ventilated housing need environmental and occupational evaluation so accidental exposure and intentional use are not confused and ongoing exposure is stopped.

  • Patients with cardiac, pulmonary, neurologic, liver, kidney, or blood disease and those with prior hypoxic injury need a lower threshold for monitoring and specialist follow-up.

Nclex Traps

  • Scene safety comes first: volatile fumes can poison rescuers and ignite.

  • Sudden sniffing death can occur without a long history of use, so first use is not low risk.

  • DSM-5-TR does not include an inhalant withdrawal criterion; seizure or delirium after stopping requires another explanation until assessed.

  • Routine urine drug screens commonly miss volatile inhalants and cannot rule out poisoning or the disorder.
