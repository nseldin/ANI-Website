Definition

Virchow triad is a pathophysiologic framework, not a bedside sign. It groups three processes that promote thrombosis: abnormal or stagnant blood flow, injury or dysfunction of the vessel lining, and hypercoagulability (blood that clots more readily).

Plain Language

Blood clots are more likely when blood is not moving normally, a blood vessel's inner lining is damaged, or the blood is unusually ready to clot.

Why It Matters

Virchow triad explains why conditions such as immobility, surgery, vascular injury, malignancy, pregnancy, and inherited clotting disorders can increase thrombosis risk. It helps clinicians connect risk factors to prevention and evaluation for deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, but it does not diagnose or exclude either condition.

Associated Conditions

  • risk of thrombosis, especially venous thromboembolism

Assessment Method

Review mobility, recent surgery or vascular injury, malignancy, pregnancy, medications, prior thrombosis, and inherited or acquired clotting risks; organize them under stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability rather than trying to elicit a bedside sign.

Positive Finding

One or more of the three thrombosis-promoting mechanisms are present; interacting components can increase risk, but there is no standard positive threshold.

Mechanism

Stasis lets activated clotting factors accumulate and reduces their clearance; endothelial injury exposes or activates procoagulant surfaces; hypercoagulability shifts hemostasis toward fibrin formation, so the mechanisms can work together to initiate and propagate an intravascular clot.

Clinical Interpretation

The Virchow triad pattern raises concern for risk of thrombosis, especially venous thromboembolism because stasis lets activated clotting factors accumulate and reduces their clearance; endothelial injury exposes or activates procoagulant surfaces; hypercoagulability shifts hemostasis toward fibrin formation, so the mechanisms can work together to initiate and propagate an intravascular clot. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Review mobility, recent surgery or vascular injury, malignancy, pregnancy, medications, prior thrombosis, and inherited or acquired clotting risks; organize them under stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability rather than trying to elicit a bedside sign. Evidence Limitations

The triad organizes mechanisms but does not assign a universal probability, and thrombosis can occur without all three components being obvious. Therefore, Virchow triad cannot confirm or exclude risk of thrombosis, especially venous thromboembolism by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive label can result from grouping nonspecific components caused by arterial thrombosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, superficial thrombophlebitis without confirming that the defined pattern is present.

False Negative Situations

Virchow triad may be absent even when risk of thrombosis, especially venous thromboembolism is present because the complete named pattern need not occur.
