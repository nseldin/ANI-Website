Name

aPTT

Range

Use the reporting laboratory's interval and, when unfractionated heparin is being titrated by aPTT, the current assay-calibrated protocol target. Mayo Clinic Laboratories reports 25-37 seconds for one method; newborn, pregnancy, reagent, specimen, and anticoagulant effects can change interpretation. A fixed multiple of control is not a universal heparin target, and there is no universal critical aPTT cutoff.

Why

aPTT measures clot formation through the intrinsic and common coagulation pathways and is used by many protocols to monitor unfractionated heparin. A prolonged aPTT signals slower in-vitro clot formation and raises concern for heparin exposure or line contamination, factor VIII, IX, or XI deficiency, another factor deficiency, a specific factor inhibitor, lupus anticoagulant, DIC, liver disease, or low or dysfunctional fibrinogen. Lupus anticoagulant can prolong aPTT while being associated with thrombosis rather than bleeding, so the number alone does not define risk. A short aPTT often reflects increased factor VIII during inflammation, pregnancy, or estrogen exposure or a collection artifact; it is not a stand-alone diagnosis of hypercoagulability. Assess bleeding and thrombosis findings, vital signs, heparin infusion rate and sample timing, all anticoagulants, the draw site and possible line contamination, CBC and platelets, PT/INR, fibrinogen, liver status, and the trend. Cause-directed evaluation includes a repeat peripheral specimen when appropriate and clinician-directed mixing study, thrombin time, anti-Xa assay, lupus-anticoagulant testing, or factor assays. Escalate immediately for major bleeding, shock, new neurologic change, suspected intracranial bleeding, or a symptomatic out-of-range result during heparin therapy; follow the ordered heparin protocol rather than independently selecting a target or reversal plan. A normal aPTT does not exclude every bleeding disorder, and aPTT does not reliably monitor every anticoagulant.

High Causes

  • Unfractionated heparin effect or heparin contamination from a vascular line

  • Factor VIII, IX, or XI deficiency or another factor deficiency

  • Specific factor inhibitor

  • Lupus anticoagulant or another nonspecific inhibitor

  • DIC, liver disease, or low or dysfunctional fibrinogen

  • Specimen collection or processing problem

Low Causes

  • Increased factor VIII with acute or chronic inflammation, pregnancy, or estrogen exposure

  • Suboptimal venipuncture, specimen collection, or processing

  • A short result does not diagnose thrombosis or exclude a bleeding disorder.

Critical Concerns

  • Major bleeding, hemodynamic instability, new neurologic findings, or a rapidly changing aPTT during unfractionated-heparin therapy requires urgent protocol-based escalation.

  • A prolonged aPTT with lupus anticoagulant does not automatically mean bleeding risk; interpret the clinical presentation and additional testing.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess bleeding, thrombosis, neurologic and hemodynamic status; verify heparin dose and timing, other anticoagulants, specimen source, platelet count, and coagulation trend.

  • When contamination is possible, notify the treating team and anticipate a correctly collected repeat specimen rather than adjusting therapy from a questionable sample.

  • Follow the ordered heparin titration, hold, notification, and reversal protocol; monitoring may use anti-Xa instead when specified by the assay or clinical context.

Nclex Traps

  • aPTT commonly monitors unfractionated heparin, but not low-molecular-weight heparin or direct oral anticoagulants in routine care.

  • A prolonged result can reflect an inhibitor linked to thrombosis, and a normal result can miss a mild factor deficiency.
