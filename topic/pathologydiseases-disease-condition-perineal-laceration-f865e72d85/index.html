Definition

Perineal laceration develops when vaginal birth stretches and may tear perineal skin, muscle, anal sphincter, or rectal mucosa, with morbidity determined by depth and repair. The characteristic pattern is postpartum perineal pain, bleeding, swelling, wound separation, urinary difficulty, or fecal urgency interpreted by first- through fourth-degree anatomy. Confirm or stage it with systematic inspection with adequate lighting and rectal examination when sphincter injury is possible; distinguish it from vulvar hematoma, uterine bleeding, hemorrhoids, episiotomy pain, and neuropathic injury by examination and bleeding source. The priority is to repair accurately with adequate analgesia and appropriate expertise because postpartum hemorrhage or expanding hematoma can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Age- or pregnancy-specific cues: feeding change, weak cry, tone change, respiratory effort, poor perfusion, abnormal fetal tracing, bleeding, fever or low temperature, dehydration, or caregiver concern.

  • Infants and pregnant/postpartum clients may deteriorate with subtle symptoms, so trend and context matter.

Diagnostics

  • systematic inspection with adequate lighting and rectal examination when sphincter injury is possible

  • serial pain, bleeding, voiding, bowel, infection, and wound assessment

  • specialist review for suspected missed sphincter injury, hematoma, or breakdown

  • Distinguish Perineal laceration from vulvar hematoma, uterine bleeding, hemorrhoids, episiotomy pain, and neuropathic injury by examination and bleeding source.

Labs

  • platelets and LFTs matter in HELLP

  • urine protein supports preeclampsia but severe features can diagnose without it

  • glucose matters in gestational diabetes and newborn hypoglycemia

Treatments

  • repair accurately with adequate analgesia and appropriate expertise

  • use ice, hygiene, analgesia, stool-softening, and pelvic-floor follow-up according to degree

  • provide antibiotics and specialist follow-up for obstetric anal-sphincter injury according to protocol

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for rapidly increasing pain or swelling, hemodynamic change, urinary retention, fever, foul drainage, wound separation, fecal urgency or incontinence, or passage of stool through the vagina because hematoma, infection, breakdown, or fistula requires prompt evaluation.

  • Confirm laceration degree and repair details, inspect bleeding and approximation with consent and privacy, and assess rectal or anal-sphincter involvement because missed deeper injury can cause hemorrhage, infection, and long-term continence problems.

  • Trend pain, pulse, blood pressure, quantified lochia, perineal swelling, firmness, and bruising because a concealed expanding hematoma may cause severe pressure pain and shock without heavy external bleeding.

  • Assess ability to void, bladder volume when indicated, bowel function, flatus and stool continence, and wound pain during movement because edema, nerve injury, and sphincter damage can impair elimination.

  • Provide ice in the early period, prescribed analgesia, gentle hygiene, pelvic-floor and mobility guidance, hydration, and a stool-softening regimen for severe tears because pain control and soft stool protect the repair without promoting constipation.

Complications

  • postpartum hemorrhage or expanding hematoma

  • wound infection can complicate Perineal laceration.

  • dehiscence can complicate Perineal laceration.

  • dyspareunia can complicate Perineal laceration.

  • chronic pain can complicate Perineal laceration.

  • fecal incontinence and fistula after missed sphincter injury

Red Flags

  • Rapidly enlarging tense swelling, severe pressure pain, tachycardia, hypotension, or syncope

  • Fever, spreading erythema, purulent or foul drainage, or wound separation

  • Inability to void, painful bladder distention, or substantially reduced urine output

  • New fecal incontinence, inability to control gas, rectovaginal stool passage, or severe rectal pain

Patient Education

  • Rinse gently after toileting, pat dry, change pads often, use the prescribed pain and bowel medicines, and avoid straining because protecting clean, well-approximated tissue supports healing.

  • Seek care for worsening pressure pain, a growing lump, fever, foul drainage, inability to urinate, wound opening, or loss of bowel control; these are treatable complications, not something to endure silently.

Nclex Traps

  • A small skin opening can conceal deeper sphincter disruption

  • Disproportionate pain or pressure warrants examination for hematoma rather than reassurance alone
