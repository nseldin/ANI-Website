Definition

Crepitus is a grinding, grating, crackling, or coarse vibration that can be heard or felt when a joint or other musculoskeletal tissue moves. It is a finding, not a diagnosis, and is distinct from lung crackles.

Plain Language

Crepitus means you can feel or hear an unusual grinding or crackling as a joint or nearby tissue moves.

Why It Matters

Painless joint noise can be harmless, but new painful crepitus can reflect cartilage damage, tendon movement, or injury. Crepitus after trauma with deformity or poor circulation needs urgent assessment, and crackling under rapidly swollen, very painful skin with fever or toxicity can signal gas in soft tissue from a life-threatening infection.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

Inspect and compare sides, then place fingertips lightly over the involved structure while the patient performs safe active motion; use gentle passive motion only when appropriate, and document location, pain, swelling, warmth, range, trauma, and whether the sensation is articular, tendon-related, or subcutaneous.

Positive Finding

A reproducible grinding, grating, crackling, or coarse vibration is heard or felt during movement or gentle palpation.

Mechanism

Irregular surfaces, moving tendons or ligaments, fracture fragments, or gas bubbles can create vibration and sound as tissues move against one another.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Crepitus finding raises concern for joint-surface irregularity, cartilage degeneration, tendon or ligament movement, fracture, or gas within soft tissue because irregular surfaces, moving tendons or ligaments, fracture fragments, or gas bubbles can create vibration and sound as tissues move against one another. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Crepitus does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Crepitus to focus evaluation of joint-surface irregularity, cartilage degeneration, tendon or ligament movement, fracture, or gas within soft tissue and the reviewed alternatives: normal tendon movement, benign joint clicking, subcutaneous emphysema, fracture movement. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect and compare sides, then place fingertips lightly over the involved structure while the patient performs safe active motion; use gentle passive motion only when appropriate, and document location, pain, swelling, warmth, range, trauma, and whether the sensation is articular, tendon-related, or subcutaneous. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Crepitus as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Examiner perception, body habitus, normal tendon movement, and intermittent noise affect detection; crepitus identifies a mechanical or gas-producing phenomenon but not its cause. Therefore, Crepitus cannot confirm or exclude joint-surface irregularity, cartilage degeneration, tendon or ligament movement, fracture, or gas within soft tissue by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as normal tendon movement, benign joint clicking, subcutaneous emphysema, fracture movement.

False Negative Situations

Crepitus may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of joint-surface irregularity, cartilage degeneration, tendon or ligament movement, fracture, or gas within soft tissue.
