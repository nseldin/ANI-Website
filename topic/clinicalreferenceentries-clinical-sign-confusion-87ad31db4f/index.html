Definition

Confusion means that attention, orientation, understanding, memory, or organized thinking is not working as expected. It is an observed finding, not a diagnosis.

Plain Language

A confused person may not know where they are or what is happening, may give disorganized answers, or may have trouble following simple directions.

Why It Matters

Sudden confusion can be the first sign of stroke, low oxygen, low blood glucose, infection, medication or substance toxicity, seizure, or another emergency. Compare with the person's usual baseline and promptly check oxygenation, glucose, vital signs, and focal neurologic findings.

Associated Conditions

  • impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier

Assessment Method

Compare with the patient's cognitive and functional baseline; assess attention, orientation, ability to follow simple commands, language, hearing and vision, then check airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, bedside glucose, vital signs, pupils, and focal neurologic findings when the change is new.

Positive Finding

The patient is newly disoriented, inattentive, unable to organize answers or actions, or meaningfully less able to understand and respond than at baseline.

Mechanism

Normal cognition depends on intact attention and language networks plus adequate oxygen, glucose, perfusion, temperature, and metabolic balance; disruption can make thinking and behavior disorganized.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Confusion finding raises concern for impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier because normal cognition depends on intact attention and language networks plus adequate oxygen, glucose, perfusion, temperature, and metabolic balance; disruption can make thinking and behavior disorganized. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Confusion does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Confusion to focus evaluation of impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier and the reviewed alternatives: aphasia, delirium, dementia, sensory or language barrier. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compare with the patient's cognitive and functional baseline; assess attention, orientation, ability to follow simple commands, language, hearing and vision, then check airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, bedside glucose, vital signs, pupils, and focal neurologic findings when the change is new. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Confusion as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Baseline cognitive impairment, language, hearing, vision, education, fatigue, sleep loss, distress, medication, and an unfamiliar environment can alter performance; one wrong orientation answer does not define the cause. Therefore, Confusion cannot confirm or exclude impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • aphasia

  • delirium

  • dementia

  • sensory or language barrier

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as aphasia, delirium, dementia, sensory or language barrier.

False Negative Situations

Confusion may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier.

Modern Status

Current descriptive finding and a component of altered mental status; it is not by itself a diagnosis of delirium, dementia, or a psychiatric disorder. Interpret Confusion in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Baseline cognitive impairment, language, hearing, vision, education, fatigue, sleep loss, distress, medication, and an unfamiliar environment can alter performance; one wrong orientation answer does not define the cause.

Safety Precautions

Observe Confusion with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Compare with the patient's cognitive and functional baseline; assess attention, orientation, ability to follow simple commands, language, hearing and vision, then check airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, bedside glucose, vital signs, pupils, and focal neurologic findings when the change is new. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not dismiss sudden confusion as aging or behavior; establish last known baseline, check glucose and oxygenation, assess for stroke, sepsis, toxicity, and trauma, and escalate rapid or severe change.

Communicate the finding with its suspected association, impaired attention, orientation, memory, comprehension, or organized thinking from delirium, neurologic injury, infection, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication or substance effect, or a sensory or communication barrier, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."
