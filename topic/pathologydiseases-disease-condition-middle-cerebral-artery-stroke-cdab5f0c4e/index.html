Risk Factors

  • Atrial fibrillation, recent cardiac thrombus, carotid stenosis, and vascular risk factors raise embolic or thrombotic risk.

  • A tandem cervical ICA and intracranial MCA lesion can reduce both primary inflow and collateral support.

  • Poor collateral circulation and delayed reperfusion allow a proximal occlusion to produce a larger infarct.

Signs Symptoms

  • Contralateral face and arm weakness or numbness greater than leg involvement reflects lateral motor-sensory cortex injury.

  • Dominant MCA stroke can cause aphasia, while nondominant MCA stroke can cause neglect, poor insight, and visuospatial disorganization.

  • Gaze preference, homonymous hemianopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, and a severe combined cortical syndrome increase concern for a proximal large-vessel occlusion.

Diagnostics

  • NIHSS and a focused cortical exam quantify language, gaze, field, motor, sensory, and neglect deficits, but a low total cannot dismiss a disabling focal deficit.

  • Noncontrast CT excludes hemorrhage and estimates early ischemic change, while CTA identifies M1 or M2 occlusion and tandem carotid disease.

  • CT perfusion or MRI diffusion/perfusion may define core and threatened tissue when extended-window or uncertain-onset selection requires it.

Labs

  • Check glucose immediately because hypoglycemia can imitate hemiparesis or language change.

  • CBC, platelets, coagulation studies, renal function, and medication history support reperfusion safety without delaying time-critical imaging when protocol permits.

  • ECG, telemetry, and later rhythm monitoring matter because atrial fibrillation is a common embolic source.

Treatments

  • Evaluate eligible disabling deficits rapidly for IV thrombolysis because benefit falls as threatened tissue becomes infarcted.

  • Activate endovascular evaluation for eligible proximal LVO because mechanical thrombectomy can restore flow when a large clot is unlikely to dissolve quickly.

  • Begin cause-specific secondary prevention after hemorrhage and reperfusion issues are resolved because cardioembolic, large-artery, and small-vessel mechanisms require different prevention.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess contralateral face and arm greater than leg weakness or sensory loss, gaze preference, aphasia, neglect, and homonymous field loss because the middle cerebral artery supplies lateral motor, sensory, language, and attention networks.

  • Establish last-known-well, baseline function, glucose, anticoagulant exposure, and stroke severity while activating brain and vascular imaging because proximal MCA occlusion may benefit from thrombolysis, thrombectomy, or both.

  • Use supported communication for aphasia, structured scanning for neglect, shoulder protection, fall precautions, and nothing-by-mouth status until screening because cortical deficits create immediate communication, injury, and aspiration risks.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, gaze, language, neglect, strength, swallowing, blood pressure, oxygenation, and headache because large MCA infarction can develop hemorrhagic transformation or malignant edema.

  • Escalate for NIH Stroke Scale increase, increasing somnolence, unequal pupils, repeated vomiting, severe headache, seizure, posturing, or airway compromise because malignant hemispheric swelling and hemorrhage may require urgent neurosurgical and critical-care treatment.

Complications

  • Large MCA infarction can cause malignant hemispheric edema and herniation because swollen tissue has little room inside the skull.

  • Aphasia, neglect, hemianopia, dysphagia, depression, shoulder injury, falls, aspiration, DVT, and loss of independence can persist.

  • Hemorrhagic transformation can occur spontaneously or after reperfusion because ischemic vessels and blood-brain barrier are injured.

Contraindications

  • Do not label fluent nonsense speech as confusion without testing comprehension because it may be Wernicke aphasia.

  • Do not assume right-sided weakness means right MCA stroke because most corticospinal fibers cross.

  • Do not use symptom improvement to cancel emergency evaluation because recanalization may be incomplete or re-occlusion may occur.

Red Flags

  • Sudden face-arm predominant weakness with aphasia, neglect, gaze deviation, or field loss

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, recurrent deficit, or rapid decline in language or attention

  • Increasing somnolence, unequal pupils, posturing, severe headache, or repeated vomiting

  • Seizure, aspiration, inability to protect the airway, or severe oxygen desaturation

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for sudden speech, gaze, face, or arm changes because MCA occlusion can destroy a large brain territory quickly.

  • Do not assume neglect or aphasia is confusion or unwillingness because targeted communication and scanning support safer recovery.

Nclex Traps

  • MCA usually affects face and arm more than leg, but a proximal occlusion can be much broader.

  • Aphasia suggests the language-dominant hemisphere; neglect more often suggests the nondominant hemisphere.

  • A low NIHSS can still represent disabling aphasia or hemianopia and should not be equated with a harmless stroke.

Name

Middle cerebral artery stroke

Category

Neurovascular localization

Definition

Middle cerebral artery stroke is ischemia or infarction within the vascular territory supplying much of the lateral cerebral hemisphere and deep perforator structures. A proximal MCA occlusion can impair contralateral face and arm movement and sensation, gaze control, language in the dominant hemisphere, spatial attention in the nondominant hemisphere, and vision. The exact pattern varies because an M1 trunk occlusion, superior-division branch, inferior-division branch, or deep lenticulostriate lesion injures different networks.

Etiology

Common mechanisms include cardiac embolism, carotid artery-to-artery embolism, intracranial atherosclerotic thrombosis, dissection, and less often vasculopathy or hypercoagulability. A sudden maximal deficit favors embolic occlusion, while fluctuating or stepwise symptoms can occur with unstable thrombus, collateral failure, or evolving ischemia.

Pathology

The MCA supplies lateral frontal, parietal, and temporal cortex plus deep white matter and basal-ganglia regions through perforators. Because face and upper-limb motor and sensory representations lie laterally, those deficits often exceed leg involvement. Dominant perisylvian injury produces aphasia; nondominant parietal injury produces neglect; frontal eye-field dysfunction can produce gaze preference toward the injured hemisphere.

Pathophysiology

A proximal M1 occlusion can block cortical divisions and lenticulostriate perforators, creating dense weakness plus cortical signs. A superior-division infarct more often affects Broca-region language and face-arm motor networks, while an inferior-division infarct more often affects Wernicke-region comprehension, visual fields, and attention. Collateral flow from ACA and PCA border zones may preserve some cortex, which is why two patients with the same occlusion can have different NIHSS scores and core volumes. Reperfusion can save penumbra, but a large established core increases edema and hemorrhagic-transformation risk.
