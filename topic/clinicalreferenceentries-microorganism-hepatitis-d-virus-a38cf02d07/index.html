Definition

Hepatitis D virus is a clinically important virus associated with acute HBV-HDV coinfection, HDV superinfection, and rapidly progressive chronic hepatitis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Hepatitis D virus is an acellular, obligately intracellular virus with a negative sense ssrna genome and a enveloped particle. Envelope status helps explain environmental stability and susceptibility to routine controls, while genome type shapes replication and diagnostic targets. Capsid symmetry is left explicitly unknown in this bounded record rather than inferred without a reviewed taxonomic source.

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Hepatitis B vaccination prevents HDV in people not already infected with HBV; blood and body-fluid exposure prevention remains essential. The reviewed route description is body fluid transmission. The interval differs substantially between simultaneous HBV-HDV coinfection and HDV superinfection of established hepatitis B. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    HDV is a defective RNA agent that requires hepatitis B surface antigen for assembly; superinfection of chronic HBV can accelerate fibrosis and liver failure. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Hepatitis D virus is associated with acute HBV-HDV coinfection, HDV superinfection, and rapidly progressive chronic hepatitis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Anti-HDV identifies exposure and HDV RNA confirms active replication; results must be interpreted with hepatitis B markers and liver status. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
