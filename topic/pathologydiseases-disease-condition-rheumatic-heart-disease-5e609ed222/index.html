Definition

Rheumatic heart disease develops when an autoimmune response after group A streptococcal pharyngitis scars cardiac valves, most often the mitral valve, over years. The characteristic pattern is a history of rheumatic fever followed by mitral stenosis or regurgitation, murmur, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, or embolic symptoms. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for valve morphology, severity, chambers, and pulmonary pressure; distinguish it from degenerative valve disease, congenital valve disease, infective endocarditis, and nonrheumatic mitral disease by age, morphology, inflammation, and history. The priority is to maintain secondary antibiotic prophylaxis for the recommended duration because atrial fibrillation can complicate Rheumatic heart disease. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for valve morphology, severity, chambers, and pulmonary pressure

  • ECG and rhythm monitoring

  • assessment of prior rheumatic fever, secondary prophylaxis, heart-failure, pregnancy, and embolic risk

  • Distinguish Rheumatic heart disease from degenerative valve disease, congenital valve disease, infective endocarditis, and nonrheumatic mitral disease by age, morphology, inflammation, and history.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • maintain secondary antibiotic prophylaxis for the recommended duration

  • treat congestion, atrial fibrillation, and thromboembolic risk

  • perform catheter or surgical valve intervention when severe disease meets criteria

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for new focal deficit, rapid atrial fibrillation with instability, acute pulmonary edema, syncope, hemoptysis with hypoxemia, fever with embolic or heart-failure findings, or shock because embolism, decompensated valve disease, or endocarditis may be present.

  • Assess dyspnea, orthopnea, exercise tolerance, edema, palpitations, hemoptysis, fever, murmur history, prior rheumatic fever, and prophylaxis adherence because chronic scarring most often produces mitral stenosis or regurgitation with atrial and pulmonary consequences.

  • Trend rhythm, pulse, pressure, oxygenation, lung sounds, daily weight, edema, and signs of embolic neurologic injury because atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, congestion, and atrial thrombus are major routes to deterioration.

  • Administer prescribed diuretic, rate-control, anticoagulant, and streptococcal secondary-prophylaxis therapy while monitoring renal function, electrolytes, pulse, and bleeding because each treatment prevents a different valve-related complication.

  • Coordinate echocardiography, pregnancy planning, dental care, and valve-team follow-up because lesion severity, ventricular response, pulmonary pressure, and physiologic stress determine the safest timing of intervention.

Complications

  • atrial fibrillation can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

  • stroke can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

  • pulmonary hypertension can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

  • right-heart failure can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

  • pregnancy decompensation can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

  • infective endocarditis can complicate Rheumatic heart disease.

Red Flags

  • New unilateral weakness, speech change, vision loss, or acute confusion

  • Rapid irregular rhythm with hypotension, ischemic pain, or pulmonary edema

  • Fever with a new murmur, petechiae, embolic findings, or worsening heart failure

  • Syncope, severe hemoptysis, hypoxemia, or progressive right-heart congestion

Patient Education

  • Teach why scheduled antibiotic prophylaxis after rheumatic fever prevents new streptococcal-triggered immune injury rather than treating the existing scar.

  • Explain that increasing breathlessness, palpitations, swelling, pregnancy, or planned procedures should prompt early valve-team contact because hemodynamic demands can expose limited valve reserve.

Nclex Traps

  • Treating a current sore throat does not reverse established valve scarring

  • Anticoagulation decisions in rheumatic mitral stenosis differ from routine nonvalvular atrial fibrillation
