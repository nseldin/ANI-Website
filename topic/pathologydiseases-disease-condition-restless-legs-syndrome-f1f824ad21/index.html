Plain Language

Restless legs syndrome causes a powerful urge to move the legs, usually with crawling, pulling, aching, tingling, or other uncomfortable sensations. It begins or worsens while resting, is worse in the evening or night, and improves at least briefly with movement.

Why It Matters

RLS can severely disrupt sleep, mood, concentration, pregnancy wellbeing, and driving safety. It may be worsened by iron deficiency, kidney disease, pregnancy, sleep apnea, or medicines, and long-term dopamine treatment can paradoxically make symptoms start earlier, spread, and worsen through augmentation.

Definition

Restless legs syndrome is a sensorimotor sleep-related disorder defined clinically by an urge to move the legs, usually with unpleasant sensations, that begins or worsens during rest, is relieved by movement, is predominantly worse in the evening or night, and is not solely explained by another condition or behavior. Periodic limb movements during sleep may coexist but are neither required nor equivalent; a laboratory value or sleep study does not diagnose RLS by itself.

Pathology

RLS involves altered central iron handling and dopaminergic sensorimotor regulation in susceptible people, but serum iron can appear normal and no single biomarker confirms the disorder. Primary familial disease and secondary forms associated with iron deficiency, pregnancy, kidney disease, or other conditions share the same clinical pattern.

Pathophysiology

  • Rest increases the urge and sensations, movement temporarily suppresses them, and circadian biology makes symptoms more likely later in the day.

  • Brain iron status and dopamine signaling are relevant, but peripheral ferritin is an imperfect proxy and thresholds used for RLS treatment differ from general anemia thresholds.

  • Dopaminergic medicines can cause augmentation: earlier daily onset, faster symptoms at rest, greater intensity, or spread beyond the legs.

Etiology

RLS may be primary or familial, or associated with iron deficiency, pregnancy, chronic kidney disease, neurologic disease, and selected medicines. Antihistaminergic, serotonergic, and antidopaminergic drugs, alcohol, caffeine, and untreated sleep apnea can exacerbate symptoms. An association does not mean every patient should stop a necessary medicine or receive iron.

Risk Factors

  • Family history and increasing age, while children and young adults can also be affected

  • Iron deficiency from bleeding, low intake, malabsorption, pregnancy, or another cause

  • Pregnancy, chronic kidney disease including dialysis, and selected neurologic conditions

  • Caffeine, alcohol, antihistamines, serotonergic or antidopaminergic medicines, and untreated obstructive sleep apnea

Signs Symptoms

  • Urge to move with creeping, crawling, pulling, aching, burning, electric, or hard-to-describe sensations

  • Onset or worsening while sitting or lying still and temporary relief with walking, stretching, rubbing, or movement

  • Evening or nighttime predominance when not solely caused by daytime inactivity

  • Difficulty falling asleep, repeated waking, partner-observed periodic limb movements, daytime fatigue, mood change, and impaired concentration

Diagnostics

  • Confirm all core clinical features and ask the patient to distinguish urge, pain, cramp, numbness, anxiety, and involuntary movement.

  • Review timing, frequency, severity, sleep loss, pregnancy, bleeding, diet, kidney disease, neuropathy, venous symptoms, arthritis, medicines, alcohol, caffeine, apnea, and prior dopamine exposure.

  • Check iron studies including ferritin and transferrin saturation in clinically significant RLS under appropriate collection conditions; interpret them with the laboratory, inflammation, kidney disease, pregnancy, age, and the current sleep guideline.

  • Polysomnography is not required for a typical diagnosis but may evaluate sleep apnea, periodic limb movement disorder, or another uncertain sleep condition.

Labs

  • Obtain ferritin and transferrin saturation, calculated from iron and total iron-binding capacity, when clinically significant RLS is assessed; CBC alone can miss relevant iron status.

  • Do not apply one universal ferritin cutoff across adults, children, pregnancy, inflammation, kidney disease, laboratories, or routes of iron. RLS consensus treatment thresholds differ from general-population anemia thresholds and require current protocol review.

  • Use targeted renal, pregnancy, metabolic, or neuropathy evaluation when the history supports it; a normal ferritin does not independently exclude RLS.

Assessment

Assess sleep and driving impairment, falls from nighttime walking, depression and suicide risk, pregnancy, blood loss, kidney disease, apnea, medicines and supplements, alcohol and caffeine, opioid or sedative exposure, and prior dopamine-agent response. Ask specifically whether treated symptoms now start earlier, begin sooner when sitting, intensify, or spread to arms because patients may mistake augmentation for disease progression.

Differential

  • Nocturnal leg cramps, which are painful muscle contractions rather than an urge relieved by movement

  • Peripheral neuropathy, radiculopathy, arthritis, positional discomfort, venous disease, edema, akathisia, anxiety, or habitual fidgeting

  • Periodic limb movement disorder, which is measured during sleep and can occur without awake RLS symptoms

  • Medication-induced akathisia, especially with dopamine-blocking drugs, which may be more generalized and not show the classic evening-rest pattern

  • Sleep apnea, insomnia, pregnancy discomfort, chronic pain, and insufficient sleep that may coexist

Treatments

  • Address exacerbating factors first: review alcohol, caffeine, antihistaminergic, serotonergic and antidopaminergic medicines and treat obstructive sleep apnea. Change a necessary medicine only through shared prescriber review.

  • Use oral or intravenous iron only when iron studies and the current RLS guideline support it, with route chosen for iron status, absorption, pregnancy, kidney disease, prior response, and infusion risk. Ferrous sulfate and IV ferric carboxymaltose are representative evidence-supported generics in appropriate patients, not universal treatment.

  • AASM strongly recommends gabapentin, pregabalin, and gabapentin enacarbil for adults. Gabapentin enacarbil has a U.S. indication for moderate-to-severe primary RLS in adults; monitor dizziness, somnolence, renal function, falls, and respiratory-depressant combinations.

  • AASM suggests against standard long-term use of pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine, or levodopa because augmentation can outweigh short-term benefit. Do not abruptly stop an established dopamine agent; create a supervised transition because severe rebound can occur.

  • Specialist-supervised opioids have a conditional role in selected refractory or augmentation cases, but overdose, respiratory depression, sleep apnea, dependence, and interaction risks require strict safeguards.

Contraindications

  • Do not give iron solely because the patient reports RLS; verify iron indices and contraindications and monitor according to route and population.

  • Do not increase a dopamine agonist automatically when symptoms begin earlier or spread; first assess augmentation.

  • Do not abruptly discontinue a long-used dopamine agonist, gabapentinoid, benzodiazepine, or opioid without a safe plan.

  • Avoid combining gabapentinoids, opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other CNS depressants without explicit respiratory and sedation review.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess drowsy driving and falls from both sleep loss and sedating treatment.

  • Monitor gabapentinoids for dizziness, somnolence, edema, misuse, mood change, renal accumulation, and respiratory risk with opioids or lung disease.

  • Monitor iron for constipation and overdose risk orally and infusion reaction or iron overload risk intravenously under the exact protocol.

  • Track augmentation with onset time, latency at rest, intensity, body distribution, and dose history rather than symptom score alone.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm the four-part rest-movement-evening pattern and exclude common mimics.

  • Obtain and trend complete iron studies under the clinician's protocol, not ferritin alone.

  • Reconcile antihistamines, antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiemetics, dopamine medicines, caffeine, alcohol, and apnea treatment.

  • Teach augmentation early and document function, sleep, driving, falls, and adverse effects at follow-up.

Red Flags

  • Unilateral hot swollen leg, chest pain, or dyspnea suggesting thrombosis or embolism rather than RLS

  • New weakness, saddle anesthesia, bowel or bladder dysfunction, rapidly progressive neuropathy, or severe focal pain

  • Profound sedation, slow breathing, overdose, or unsafe CNS-depressant combination

  • Severe augmentation, suicidal distress, or inability to sleep for multiple nights with unsafe behavior

Complications

  • Chronic insomnia, daytime sleepiness, crashes, falls, mood symptoms, and impaired work or school function

  • Dopamine-agent augmentation and severe rebound during an unsafe withdrawal

  • Sedation, dizziness, edema, respiratory depression, misuse, or falls from treatment

  • Iron overload, infusion reaction, constipation, or accidental pediatric iron poisoning

Prognosis

RLS course varies. Pregnancy-associated symptoms often improve after delivery but may recur, while primary and kidney-associated disease can persist. Correcting a relevant iron problem or aggravating factor can substantially help. Long-term follow-up should identify augmentation and medication harm early rather than assuming every worsening is natural progression.

Prevention

  • Prevent compounding symptoms through adequate sleep, moderate activity, and treatment of apnea and iron deficiency when present.

  • Review aggravating medicines and substances before escalating therapy.

  • Avoid routine chronic dopamine-agent escalation and teach augmentation from the start.

Patient Education

  • RLS is an urge to move that starts or worsens at rest, improves with movement, and is worse later in the day; leg pain alone is not enough.

  • Periodic leg kicks during sleep are related but are not the same diagnosis.

  • Do not start iron or change antidepressants, antipsychotics, or dopamine medicines on your own.

  • If symptoms begin earlier, become stronger, or spread after dopamine treatment, report possible augmentation rather than taking extra doses.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy requires iron and medication decisions based on obstetric context; many usual medicines lack reassuring pregnancy evidence, so non-drug care and specialist guidance are emphasized.

  • Children may describe growing pains, bedtime resistance, or an urge poorly; use pediatric criteria, iron interpretation, and dosing rather than adult assumptions.

  • Chronic kidney disease and dialysis alter iron management, drug clearance, and symptom burden; coordinate nephrology and sleep care.

  • Older adults need fall, renal function, cognition, polypharmacy, apnea, and opioid or sedative risk review.

Nclex Traps

  • RLS is diagnosed clinically; periodic limb movements or a ferritin result alone do not diagnose it.

  • RLS iron thresholds are context-specific and differ from ordinary anemia thresholds.

  • Gabapentinoids are now strongly recommended adult options; routine dopamine agonists are discouraged because of augmentation.

  • Earlier and spreading symptoms on a dopamine agent suggest augmentation, not automatically undertreatment.
