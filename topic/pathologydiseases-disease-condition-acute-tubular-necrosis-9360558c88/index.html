Definition

Acute tubular necrosis, more precisely called acute tubular injury when frank necrosis is not demonstrated, is intrinsic AKI caused by ischemic, toxic, inflammatory, or pigment-related injury to tubular epithelial cells. Damaged tubules fail to reabsorb normally and also reduce GFR through obstruction, backleak, vasoconstriction, and inflammatory signaling.

Pathology

High-energy proximal tubular and thick-ascending-limb cells are vulnerable to oxygen and mitochondrial failure. Injured cells lose polarity and brush border, detach into the lumen, form granular casts, obstruct flow, and expose basement membrane; patchy injury can occur without diffuse histologic necrosis.

Pathophysiology

ATP depletion disrupts cytoskeleton and transporter localization, so sodium reclamation falls and distal NaCl delivery activates tubuloglomerular feedback. Cell debris and Tamm-Horsfall protein form granular casts that raise tubular pressure, while damaged epithelium permits filtrate to leak back into interstitium. Endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and intrarenal vasoconstriction further reduce perfusion. During recovery, regenerating epithelium may restore filtration before concentrating and electrolyte control fully recover, creating a polyuric phase with dehydration and potassium or magnesium loss.

Etiology

Common triggers include prolonged shock or hypoperfusion, sepsis, major surgery, nephrotoxic medications such as aminoglycosides, amphotericin, or cisplatin, pigments from rhabdomyolysis or hemolysis, severe hyperbilirubinemia, toxins, and multifactorial ICU exposures. Contrast-associated AKI requires contextual assessment because illness and hemodynamics often coexist with contrast timing.

Risk Factors

  • Prolonged hypotension, shock, sepsis, cardiac surgery, or major trauma

  • CKD, diabetes, older age, or reduced renal reserve

  • Multiple nephrotoxins or high exposure during impaired clearance

  • Rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, tumor lysis, severe jaundice, or toxic ingestion

Signs Symptoms

  • Oliguric or nonoliguric creatinine rise

  • Fluid overload, edema, hypertension, dyspnea, or uremic symptoms

  • Pigment-associated dark urine with muscle pain or hemolysis clues

  • Polyuria with electrolyte loss during recovery

Diagnostics

  • Urine microscopy for muddy brown granular casts and tubular epithelial cells

  • Review shock, sepsis, surgery, toxins, drug levels, CK, hemolysis, and bilirubin context

  • Exclude obstruction and evaluate glomerular, interstitial, or vascular alternatives

  • Trend filtration and urine output through injury and recovery phases

Labs

  • Creatinine and BUN rise as filtration falls

  • FeNa may be elevated after established tubular dysfunction but can remain low early, in sepsis, or in pigment-associated injury

  • Potassium, phosphorus, and acids accumulate during low clearance

  • Recovery diuresis can produce hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and volume depletion

Treatments

  • Restore hemodynamics and treat sepsis or shock without causing congestion

  • Stop, reduce, or substitute nephrotoxins and adjust all renally cleared medicines

  • Treat pigment release, electrolyte emergencies, acidosis, and fluid overload

  • Provide kidney replacement therapy when refractory complications or toxin removal require it

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess duration of hypotension, sepsis, surgery, pigment injury, contrast and toxin exposure, medications, and baseline kidney function because ischemic and nephrotoxic tubular injury often follows a recognizable insult.

  • Trend urine output, creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, phosphate, magnesium, fluid balance, weight, and granular casts because electrolyte and volume complications change across oliguric and recovery phases.

  • Maintain cause-directed perfusion and infection treatment while avoiding further nephrotoxins and adjusting medication doses because supportive care prevents additional tubular injury while epithelium recovers.

  • During diuretic recovery, replace fluid and electrolytes only to prescribed targets with frequent reassessment because rising urine output can cause hypovolemia, hypokalemia, and delayed renal recovery.

  • Escalate urgently for refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, anuria, uremic encephalopathy, or pericarditic pain because these are indications for immediate nephrology and possible kidney replacement.

Complications

  • Severe hyperkalemia, acidosis, and pulmonary edema

  • Uremia and dialysis requirement

  • Recovery-phase dehydration and electrolyte depletion

  • Incomplete repair, CKD, recurrent AKI, or kidney failure

Contraindications

  • Do not rule out ATN because FeNa is below a memorized cutoff

  • Do not assume rising urine output means filtration and tubular function have fully recovered

  • Avoid using 'contrast nephropathy' as the automatic explanation without evaluating competing causes

Red Flags

  • Hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram change or malignant dysrhythmia

  • Pulmonary edema with severe hypoxemia or respiratory distress

  • Anuria, severe persistent acidosis, or rapidly worsening azotemia

  • Recovery-phase polyuria with hypotension or major electrolyte depletion

Patient Education

  • Explain that damaged tubules may need time to repair even after blood pressure or infection improves, so creatinine can worsen before recovery begins.

  • Teach patients to report reduced urine, swelling, breathlessness, severe weakness, or later excessive urine because both oliguric and recovery phases require monitoring.

Nclex Traps

  • ATN can be nonoliguric

  • Muddy brown casts support tubular injury but their absence does not exclude it

  • FeNa is a clue, not a diagnosis

  • The diuretic recovery phase can still be dangerous
