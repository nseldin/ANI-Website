Used To Treat

Early treatment of influenza A or B and selected post-exposure prophylaxis. Oral oseltamivir is the default for many patients and is recommended as soon as possible for hospitalized or progressive disease; inhaled zanamivir, IV peramivir, and oral baloxavir have narrower route, age, disease-severity, pregnancy, or airway constraints.

Description

Influenza antivirals interrupt either release of newly formed virions or transcription of viral RNA. Oseltamivir, zanamivir, and peramivir inhibit neuraminidase, the surface enzyme that cleaves terminal sialic acid so progeny virions can escape an infected respiratory cell. Baloxavir inhibits the viral PA cap-dependent endonuclease, blocking cap snatching before viral mRNA can be transcribed. Treatment works best early because viral replication peaks near symptom onset; therefore preventing new rounds of infection has more leverage before inflammatory injury and complications dominate. High-risk, hospitalized, severe, or progressive illness should still be treated promptly even when more than 48 hours have passed because viral replication can remain clinically important.

Mechanism

Influenza hemagglutinin binds sialic acid to enter cells. After replication and budding, neuraminidase cleaves the same type of sialic-acid linkage, freeing virions from the host membrane and preventing aggregation. Oseltamivir carboxylate, zanamivir, and peramivir mimic the transition state of sialic acid and occupy the neuraminidase catalytic site, so spread to neighboring cells falls. Baloxavir marboxil is converted to baloxavir acid, which chelates divalent metal ions in the PA endonuclease active site. The endonuclease normally cuts capped fragments from host pre-mRNAs; influenza polymerase uses those caps as primers. Blocking this cap-snatching step suppresses viral mRNA and protein synthesis earlier in the life cycle. Route and pharmacokinetics then determine who can receive each drug.

Boxed Warning

None of the four has a current U.S. boxed warning, but selection errors can be consequential. Zanamivir inhalation can cause serious bronchospasm and is avoided in asthma or COPD. Baloxavir absorption falls with calcium, iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, antacids, or dairy taken together because metal chelation traps the drug in the gut. Oseltamivir and peramivir require renal adjustment. Serious skin reactions and transient neuropsychiatric events have been reported with influenza antivirals, while influenza itself can also cause delirium or abnormal behavior.

Adverse Effects

  • Oseltamivir: nausea, vomiting, headache; zanamivir: bronchospasm and airway irritation; peramivir: diarrhea

  • Baloxavir: generally mild adverse effects, but treatment-emergent resistance can occur, especially in some pediatric or immunocompromised contexts

Contraindications

  • Avoid inhaled zanamivir in underlying airway disease and in severe milk-protein allergy because the inhalation powder contains lactose with milk proteins.

  • Do not use route convenience alone: hospitalized influenza is generally treated with oral or enteric oseltamivir because outcome evidence is stronger than for routine baloxavir, inhaled zanamivir, or single-dose peramivir.

Nursing Essentials

  • Do not delay treatment in a high-risk or hospitalized patient while waiting for test confirmation when clinical suspicion is sufficient.

  • Teach the exact duration: uncomplicated outpatient oseltamivir or zanamivir is commonly five days, whereas uncomplicated peramivir and baloxavir are single-dose regimens. Severe disease may require a longer individualized course.

Interactions

  • Influenza antivirals can interfere with replication of live attenuated intranasal influenza vaccine; vaccine timing differs by drug half-life.

  • Polyvalent cations markedly reduce baloxavir exposure. Renally cleared drugs or kidney injury alter oseltamivir and peramivir dosing.

Key Labs

  • Renal function for oseltamivir/peramivir, oxygenation and severity assessment, pregnancy status, airway history, immune status, and ability to absorb or inhale medication

  • Symptom-onset time, exposure timing for prophylaxis, clinical progression, secondary bacterial infection, and resistance concern when virus persists during prolonged treatment

Nclex Traps

  • The 48-hour window describes greatest benefit, not a rule to withhold treatment from hospitalized, severe, progressive, or high-risk patients.

  • Baloxavir blocks cap-dependent endonuclease; it is not a neuraminidase inhibitor and should not be taken with polyvalent cations.
