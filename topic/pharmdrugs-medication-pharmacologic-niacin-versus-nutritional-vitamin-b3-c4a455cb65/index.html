Used To Treat

Prevention or treatment of niacin deficiency at nutritional doses and selected severe dyslipidemia at prescription pharmacologic doses. Modern routine addition to effective statin therapy is limited because favorable HDL or triglyceride changes did not reliably add cardiovascular benefit.

Description

Niacin is vitamin B3, but 'niacin' can refer to two clinically different exposures. Milligram nutritional replacement supports synthesis of NAD and NADP and prevents pellagra. Gram-range prescription nicotinic acid changes lipoprotein metabolism, lowers triglycerides and LDL to varying degrees, and raises HDL-C, but also causes flushing, hepatotoxicity, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia, hypotension, and GI toxicity. Nicotinamide can treat deficiency but does not reproduce the lipid effect. Immediate-release, sustained-release, and prescription extended-release nicotinic acid have different absorption and liver-to-flush tradeoffs and must not be exchanged milligram for milligram. Raising HDL-C does not guarantee fewer events; modern trials found no routine outcome advantage when niacin was added to well-treated statin patients, so laboratory improvement alone is not a sufficient reason for exposure.

Mechanism

At nutritional doses, niacin is converted to NAD/NADP for redox metabolism. At pharmacologic doses, nicotinic acid activates hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2, also called GPR109A, in adipocytes and immune or skin cells. Adipose signaling acutely reduces lipolysis and free-fatty-acid delivery to liver, while hepatic triglyceride synthesis and ApoB-containing VLDL secretion fall through additional mechanisms. Slower ApoA-I catabolism contributes to higher HDL-C. In skin, GPR109A activates prostaglandin D2 and E2 release, causing vasodilation, warmth, redness, and itching; aspirin before the dose can blunt that prostaglandin pathway. Sustained hepatic exposure explains greater hepatotoxicity with some slow-release products. Insulin resistance and reduced uric-acid excretion explain hyperglycemia and gout risk.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Prescription extended-release niacin can cause severe hepatotoxicity, especially if substituted for an equal dose of immediate- or sustained-release niacin. Confirm formulation and titrate; stop and evaluate jaundice, severe fatigue, dark urine, persistent GI symptoms, or marked glucose or uric-acid deterioration.

Adverse Effects

  • Flushing, warmth, pruritus, headache, dizziness, hypotension, nausea, diarrhea, dyspepsia, and rash are common dose-related effects.

  • Hepatotoxicity, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia or gout, myopathy with statins, reduced platelets, increased INR, and cystoid macular edema are serious concerns.

Contraindications

  • Active liver disease, unexplained persistent aminotransferase elevation, active peptic ulcer, and arterial bleeding are key prescription-label contraindications.

  • Do not use a supplement bottle as a substitute for prescription extended release; purity, release profile, and dose can change both efficacy and liver risk.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify exact molecule, dose, brand, and release formulation. Give prescription extended release at bedtime after a low-fat snack as labeled; avoid hot drinks, alcohol, or spicy food near dosing because vasodilation worsens flushing.

  • Aspirin premedication may reduce flushing when approved, but flushing can fade with tolerance while liver, glucose, uric-acid, and muscle risks remain. Never escalate solely to chase HDL.

Interactions

  • Statins add myopathy risk; alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs increase liver burden; antihypertensives add hypotension; anticoagulants require bleeding and INR review.

  • Bile-acid sequestrants can bind niacin and require spacing. Chromium or glucose-lowering therapy may need review because glycemia can worsen.

Key Labs

  • Lipid panel, ALT/AST, bilirubin, fasting glucose or A1c, uric acid, platelets, and INR when relevant; CK and creatinine for significant muscle symptoms.

  • Laboratory benefit should be linked to a clinical indication. An HDL rise alone does not prove cardiovascular benefit.

Nclex Traps

  • Nicotinamide treats vitamin deficiency but is not the lipid-lowering form. Pharmacologic nicotinic acid is not ordinary vitamin supplementation.

  • Flushing is prostaglandin mediated and can be reduced; lack of flushing does not prove the dose is safe or ineffective.
