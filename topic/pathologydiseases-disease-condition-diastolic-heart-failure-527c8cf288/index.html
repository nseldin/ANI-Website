Definition

Diastolic heart failure develops when a stiff or hypertrophied left ventricle fills at abnormally high pressure despite a preserved ejection fraction, transmitting congestion backward into lungs and atria. The characteristic pattern is exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, edema, elevated filling-pressure clues, left-atrial enlargement, and preserved ejection fraction in the appropriate syndrome. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for ejection fraction, structure, valves, and diastolic indices; distinguish it from pulmonary disease, obesity or deconditioning, constrictive pericarditis, and high-output states by imaging, hemodynamics, and cause testing. The priority is to relieve congestion with careful diuresis because pulmonary edema can complicate Diastolic heart failure. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for ejection fraction, structure, valves, and diastolic indices

  • natriuretic peptide interpreted with age, rhythm, obesity, and kidney function

  • evaluation for ischemia, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, infiltrative disease, and lung mimics

  • Distinguish Diastolic heart failure from pulmonary disease, obesity or deconditioning, constrictive pericarditis, and high-output states by imaging, hemodynamics, and cause testing.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • relieve congestion with careful diuresis

  • control blood pressure and treat ischemia, atrial fibrillation, obesity, sleep apnea, diabetes, and kidney disease

  • use evidence-based heart-failure therapy matched to current guideline indications

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for acute pulmonary edema, rapid atrial fibrillation with symptoms, chest pain, syncope, hypotension, confusion, or oliguria because a stiff or hypertrophied left ventricle fills at abnormally high pressure despite a preserved ejection fraction, transmitting congestion backward into lungs and atria.

  • Assess congestion and perfusion with breath sounds, orthopnea, edema, jugular venous pressure, blood pressure, mentation, and urine output because a stiff ventricle can produce high filling pressure despite a preserved ejection fraction.

  • Trend heart rate and rhythm continuously during decompensation because atrial fibrillation and tachycardia shorten filling time and can abruptly worsen pulmonary congestion.

  • Administer prescribed diuretics in measured steps and reassess symptoms, weight, creatinine, sodium, potassium, and orthostasis because these patients may be congested yet sensitive to excessive preload reduction.

  • Identify triggers such as hypertension, ischemia, infection, anemia, kidney dysfunction, medication changes, and dietary sodium because treating the trigger is central to restoring stable filling pressures.

  • Support ordered blood-pressure control and comorbidity treatment, pace activity with rest, and avoid unreviewed fluid boluses because normal ejection fraction does not mean the ventricle accepts volume normally.

Complications

  • pulmonary edema can complicate Diastolic heart failure.

  • atrial fibrillation can complicate Diastolic heart failure.

  • stroke can complicate Diastolic heart failure.

  • kidney injury can complicate Diastolic heart failure.

  • right-heart failure can complicate Diastolic heart failure.

  • hypotension or kidney decline from overdiuresis

  • recurrent hospitalization and frailty

Red Flags

  • Sudden severe dyspnea, diffuse crackles, frothy sputum, or escalating oxygen need

  • Rapid or irregular rhythm with chest discomfort, hypotension, or altered mentation

  • Syncope, cool extremities, falling urine output, or acute kidney-function decline

  • Severe hypertension with neurologic symptoms or pulmonary edema

Patient Education

  • Explain that the pumping percentage may look normal while the heart muscle is too stiff to fill without high pressure; this is why congestion symptoms are real despite a preserved ejection fraction.

  • Track weight, blood pressure, swelling, and breathing symptoms, take medicines consistently, and call early for rapid gain, increasing pillows, palpitations, dizziness, or reduced urine.

Nclex Traps

  • Preserved ejection fraction does not mean normal cardiac function

  • Do not diagnose diastolic heart failure from one echo descriptor without a clinical congestion syndrome
