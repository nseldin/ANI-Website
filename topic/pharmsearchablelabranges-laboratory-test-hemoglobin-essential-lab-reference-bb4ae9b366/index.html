Name

Hemoglobin

Range

Hemoglobin reference intervals vary by reporting laboratory, age, sex, pregnancy, altitude, and other patient factors; use the patient's printed interval and baseline. A low value supports anemia, but transfusion is not triggered by one universal number: the 2023 AABB guideline recommends considering red-cell transfusion below 7 g/dL for most hospitalized, hemodynamically stable adults and for specified stable pediatric populations, while allowing different thresholds for selected cardiac-surgery, orthopedic-surgery, cardiovascular-disease, and congenital-heart-disease contexts. Those thresholds do not replace immediate resuscitation and hemorrhage management in active bleeding or instability.

Why

An abnormal hemoglobin result signals altered red-cell oxygen-carrying capacity. A low result raises concern for blood loss, iron or vitamin B12 deficiency, hemolysis, kidney disease, inflammation, or bone-marrow failure; a high result can reflect hemoconcentration from dehydration, chronic hypoxemia, smoking or altitude, or polycythemia vera. Promptly assess bleeding, vital signs and hemodynamic stability, chest pain, dyspnea, oxygenation, mental status, fatigue or syncope, volume status, pregnancy status when relevant, and the prior trend. Cause-directed evaluation can include a repeat complete blood count, hematocrit and red-cell indices, reticulocyte count, blood smear, iron studies, vitamin B12 or folate testing, hemolysis studies, kidney evaluation, and testing for an identified bleeding source as ordered. Escalate immediately for major bleeding, shock or hemodynamic instability, chest pain, syncope, altered consciousness, respiratory distress, or rapid deterioration. One hemoglobin value does not diagnose the cause of anemia or establish a transfusion decision; interpret symptoms, acuity, active bleeding, comorbidities, population, baseline and trend, and the ordered transfusion protocol together.

High Causes

  • Hemoconcentration from dehydration

  • Chronic hypoxemia, smoking, or higher altitude

  • Polycythemia vera or another erythrocytosis

Low Causes

  • Acute or chronic blood loss

  • Iron, vitamin B12, or folate deficiency

  • Hemolysis

  • Kidney disease or inflammation

  • Bone-marrow failure or malignancy

  • Pregnancy-related hemodilution

Critical Concerns

  • Major bleeding, shock or hemodynamic instability, chest pain, syncope, altered consciousness, respiratory distress, or rapid deterioration requires immediate escalation regardless of a single hemoglobin value.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess bleeding, vital signs, perfusion, oxygenation, cardiopulmonary and neurologic symptoms, volume status, medications, pregnancy status when relevant, and the hemoglobin trend.

  • Verify that any transfusion plan follows the ordered, population-specific protocol and the patient's symptoms and clinical context rather than one automatic threshold.

Nclex Traps

  • Hemoglobin measures oxygen-carrying protein; it does not identify why it is abnormal.

  • A restrictive transfusion threshold for a stable studied population is not a universal trigger for every patient or an instruction to delay emergency hemorrhage care.
