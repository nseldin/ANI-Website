Definition

Escherichia coli is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic bacillus that normally inhabits the intestines of humans and other warm-blooded animals. Most strains are harmless members of the gut microbiota, but specific pathotypes and extraintestinal strains can cause diarrhea, urinary tract infection, bloodstream infection, pneumonia, neonatal meningitis, and sepsis. The species name alone therefore does not tell whether an isolate is harmless, virulent, or clinically significant.

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    E. coli is a species in the Enterobacteriaceae family. Terms such as STEC, EHEC, enterotoxigenic E. coli, and uropathogenic E. coli describe pathotypes or clinical groupings, not synonyms for every E. coli strain. This distinction is essential because pathotype determines mechanism, diagnostic testing, complications, and whether an antimicrobial may help or harm.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    E. coli is a non-spore-forming Gram-negative rod with an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide and a thin peptidoglycan layer. It can grow with or without oxygen. Many strains are motile by peritrichous flagella, but important nonmotile serotypes exist, so motility is a variable trait rather than a species-wide diagnostic rule. Gram-negative cell-envelope injury can contribute inflammatory endotoxin effects during invasive infection.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    The intestinal tract is the principal reservoir. Enteric pathogenic strains commonly spread through the fecal-oral route in contaminated food, water, hands, or animal contact. Extraintestinal disease often results from a person's own bowel flora entering a normally sterile site, as when periurethral contamination ascends into the bladder. These routes explain why food safety and hand hygiene matter for diarrheal disease while catheter care and urinary risk factors matter for UTI.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Different strains use different virulence programs. Uropathogenic strains attach to urinary epithelium and resist clearance by urine flow, whereas diarrheagenic pathotypes attach to or injure intestinal epithelium. Shiga toxin-producing E. coli can release toxin that damages microvascular endothelium, especially in the kidney, helping explain hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and hemolytic uremic syndrome. That mechanism also explains why suspected STEC requires special treatment caution.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    E. coli is a leading cause of uncomplicated cystitis and a common cause of invasive Gram-negative infection. Enteric disease ranges from watery diarrhea to severe abdominal cramping and bloody diarrhea. Warning findings include dehydration, reduced urine output, pallor, bruising, altered mental status, hypotension, or sepsis. A positive culture must be interpreted by specimen and symptoms because stool colonization and contaminated urine samples can mislead.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Testing must match the syndrome. Urinary symptoms call for urinalysis and, when indicated, urine culture; suspected bloodstream infection requires blood cultures. Diarrheal evaluation may use stool culture plus Shiga toxin or gene testing because culture alone can miss clinically important toxin information. Species identification does not replace susceptibility testing, and an E. coli result does not identify the pathotype unless the laboratory method specifically evaluates it.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Prevent enteric spread through hand hygiene, safe food handling, separation of raw and cooked foods, appropriate cooking, safe water, and avoidance of cross-contamination. Standard Precautions apply in healthcare, with additional precautions based on the patient's diarrheal syndrome and setting. Avoid reflexive antibiotics for suspected STEC: CDC warns that antimicrobials may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome. Therapy for UTI or invasive disease must follow site, host factors, and susceptibility results.
