Used To Treat

Symptomatic, severe, recurrent, or anticipated hypoglycemia and hypokalemia after excess injected, infused, or mistaken-concentration insulin, including large long-acting depots and pump errors.

Description

Insulin is a glucose-lowering hormone that activates its receptor to suppress hepatic glucose release and shift glucose and potassium into cells; in overdose, these actions cause recurrent hypoglycemia, seizure, dysrhythmia, or coma. Excess insulin continues moving glucose from blood into insulin-sensitive tissues and suppressing hepatic glucose release, so the brain can become energy deprived again after an initial dextrose bolus. Long-acting analogs, concentrated U-500 products, large subcutaneous depots, repeated injections, kidney or liver disease, and uncertain amounts can extend risk for many hours or longer than the usual therapeutic profile. Insulin also shifts potassium into cells. The serum potassium may fall even though total-body potassium is not depleted, so replacement must be careful and trend based; aggressive correction can cause rebound hyperkalemia when insulin action fades. Immediate treatment restores glucose to the brain, while a titrated dextrose infusion, safe oral complex carbohydrate, frequent glucose checks, and prolonged observation match ongoing substrate delivery to the persistent insulin effect. Glucagon is a bridge when IV access is unavailable, not proof that recurrence is impossible.

Mechanism

Insulin activates its receptor tyrosine kinase, moves GLUT4 transporters to skeletal-muscle and adipose-cell membranes, promotes glycogen and fat storage, and suppresses hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. In excess, these normal actions rapidly lower circulating glucose, depriving the brain of its principal fuel and causing confusion, seizure, coma, dysrhythmia, and injury. Dextrose provides immediate circulating substrate; continuous dextrose and oral complex carbohydrate replace glucose at the rate insulin keeps removing it. Glucagon mobilizes hepatic glycogen, but fasting, alcohol use, malnutrition, or liver disease can make that store inadequate. The same receptor signaling activates sodium-potassium ATPase and drives potassium intracellularly, which explains hypokalemia without automatic total-body potassium loss. C-peptide can help distinguish endogenous insulin secretion from injected insulin, although some insulin-analog assays do not detect every product reliably.

Administration Timing

  • For seizure, unconsciousness, inability to swallow, or severe confusion, call 911, position and ventilate as needed, give labeled emergency glucagon when available, and use IV dextrose under protocol. Never force oral sugar into an unprotected airway.

  • After initial correction, start measured, titrated carbohydrate replacement appropriate to severity: frequent oral complex carbohydrate when awake and safe, and a dextrose infusion when recurrent or severe hypoglycemia continues. Use a central line for highly concentrated or prolonged infusions when local policy and tissue risk warrant it.

  • Continue frequent glucose monitoring and sustained carbohydrate until the patient remains stable after dextrose is weaned and the longest plausible insulin effect has passed. Long-acting, concentrated, depot, pump, kidney-disease, and uncertain-intent exposures require individualized monitored observation with Poison Help, endocrinology, and toxicology.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact insulin name, concentration, device, prescribed dose, actual units and volume, injection sites, time, repeated injections, pump history, remaining cartridge amount, co-ingested glucose-lowering drugs, meals, exercise, alcohol, and kidney or liver disease.

  • Check bedside glucose at short protocol-defined intervals during rescue and after every infusion change because recurrent neuroglycopenia can develop rapidly as dextrose delivery changes. Independently verify dextrose concentration and rate, inspect the IV site for extravasation, record all carbohydrate intake, and trend neurologic status and fluid balance because concentration errors, tissue injury, osmotic diuresis, or fluid overload can complicate rescue.

  • Trend potassium, magnesium, phosphate, ECG, and urine output during prolonged high-dose carbohydrate replacement. Replace potassium cautiously to a clinical target rather than chasing a transient intracellular shift, and anticipate electrolyte reversal as insulin clears.

Key Labs

  • Serial point-of-care and laboratory glucose, electrolytes including potassium, magnesium and phosphate, creatinine, hepatic studies, ECG, and blood gas or lactate when critically ill. Confirm discordant meter results because poor perfusion and assay limitations can mislead.

  • Obtain insulin, C-peptide, beta-hydroxybutyrate, and a sulfonylurea or meglitinide screen when the cause is unclear because their pattern can distinguish injected insulin from endogenous secretion or a secretagogue. Treatment must not wait for those results because ongoing neuroglycopenia requires immediate glucose correction, and exogenous analog cross-reactivity differs across assays even when insulin is high and C-peptide is suppressed.

Adverse Effects

  • Hypoglycemia causes sweating, tremor, palpitations, behavior change, focal deficits, seizure, coma, cerebral injury, dysrhythmia, and death; recurrence can occur after apparent recovery. Hypokalemia can cause weakness, ileus, respiratory compromise, and ventricular dysrhythmia.

  • Dextrose therapy can cause hyperglycemia, osmotic diuresis, fluid overload, phlebitis, extravasation injury, and electrolyte shifts. Glucagon can cause vomiting and aspiration and may fail when hepatic glycogen is depleted.

Contraindications

  • Do not give food or liquid to a patient who cannot protect the airway. Do not stop monitoring after one corrected glucose or because the usual duration of the named insulin has elapsed after a massive depot.

  • Do not replace potassium reflexively without serial values, ECG, kidney function, urine output, and awareness that the deficit may be redistribution. Do not remove an injection depot surgically except in rare, specialist-selected circumstances.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Recurrent hypoglycemia, need for increasing dextrose, seizure, coma, dysrhythmia, severe electrolyte disturbance, fluid intolerance, uncertain long-acting dose, or combined sulfonylurea exposure requires ICU and toxicology or endocrinology. Reconsider sepsis, liver failure, adrenal disease, secretagogues, or ongoing pump delivery when glucose demand does not fall as expected.

Interactions

  • Sulfonylureas and meglitinides drive endogenous insulin release and may require octreotide, while beta blockers can hide tremor and tachycardia. Alcohol, fasting, exercise, kidney failure, and liver disease reduce glucose reserve or insulin clearance.

  • Do not use corticosteroids as routine first-line antidotes for insulin overdose because their delayed metabolic effects do not neutralize circulating injected insulin. Octreotide should not substitute for sustained glucose replacement in pure exogenous insulin toxicity because it does not reliably switch off injected insulin.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Use measured weight, reconstruct the caregiver's device steps, and continue prolonged recurrence surveillance because a small absolute dosing error can be large per kilogram and a child may be unable to identify the product or amount.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal neuroglycopenia threatens both patients; treat immediately and add fetal assessment after maternal glucose, airway, and circulation are stable.

  • Population

    Kidney or liver disease

    Note

    Reduced insulin clearance or impaired glycogen and gluconeogenic reserve can prolong hypoglycemia and make glucagon less dependable.

Evidence Limitations

  • Large intentional insulin overdoses are described mainly in observational series and case reports; there is no single dose-to-duration formula. Product labels confirm recurrent hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, sustained carbohydrate, and observation needs, but infusion targets and discharge timing remain individualized.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal glucose after dextrose is a treatment response, not proof that excess insulin has cleared.

  • Insulin shifts potassium into cells, so a low serum value does not automatically equal a large total-body deficit.

  • Glucagon depends on hepatic glycogen and is a bridge when IV glucose is not yet available.

Regulatory Status

Dextrose and glucagon products have labeled severe-hypoglycemia uses. Prolonged infusion, electrolyte strategy, and observation after overdose are individualized emergency and toxicology care rather than a single FDA-labeled overdose regimen.

Boxed Warning

INSULIN-OVERDOSE WARNING: seizure, unconsciousness, or inability to swallow requires 911 and parenteral glucose rescue; never force oral sugar because aspiration can occur without airway protection. Sustain carbohydrate and monitor glucose frequently because hypoglycemia can recur long after apparent recovery, especially with long-acting or concentrated insulin; correct potassium cautiously because the low serum value may reflect an intracellular shift rather than total-body depletion.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
