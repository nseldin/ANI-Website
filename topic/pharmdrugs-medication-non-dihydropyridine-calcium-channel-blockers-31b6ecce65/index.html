Class

Cardiac-active L-type calcium-channel antagonists and Class IV antiarrhythmics

Used To Treat

Temporary ventricular-rate control in selected atrial fibrillation/flutter, AV-node-dependent paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, chronic hypertension, and angina depending on agent, route, formulation, ventricular function, and rhythm diagnosis.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not treat a wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain origin, ventricular tachycardia, or pre-excited atrial fibrillation/flutter with diltiazem or verapamil. Avoid or use only under expert direction in acute decompensated systolic heart failure, cardiogenic shock, severe hypotension, unpaced sick sinus syndrome, or second-/third-degree AV block. IV beta blockers and IV diltiazem/verapamil should not be administered together or in close proximity because profound bradycardia, AV block, myocardial depression, hypotension, and cardiac arrest can occur.

Mechanism

SA- and AV-nodal action potentials rely heavily on inward L-type calcium current rather than the fast sodium current used by atrial and ventricular working myocardium. Diltiazem and verapamil bind open and inactivated L-type channels with use dependence, so repeated rapid depolarization produces more block. Slower phase-0 nodal upstroke reduces conduction velocity; longer AV-node refractoriness interrupts reentry and filters atrial impulses; reduced phase-4 automaticity slows pacemaker firing. In ventricular myocytes, less trigger calcium means less calcium-induced calcium release and weaker contraction. Adding a beta blocker removes adrenergic cAMP support at the same time, so the combination can produce more than a simple additive slowing of rate. It can collapse conduction and contractility in a vulnerable patient.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify the rhythm rather than treating the monitor's rate alone. Review QRS width, evidence of pre-excitation, ventricular function, blood pressure, heart rate, PR interval, and whether the patient already received a beta blocker, digoxin, amiodarone, or another conduction-slowing drug.

  • Use continuous ECG and blood-pressure monitoring for IV therapy. Hold/escalate according to the actual order parameters and clinical state; do not invent one universal pulse cutoff for every indication.

Interactions

  • Beta blockers, digoxin, ivabradine, clonidine, antiarrhythmics, anesthetics, and neuromuscular blockers can add conduction or contractility depression.

  • Both drugs participate in CYP3A/P-glycoprotein interactions. They can raise selected statin, digoxin, ranolazine, carbamazepine, cyclosporine, or other substrate exposure; exact limits differ between diltiazem and verapamil.

Key Labs

  • Continuous ECG and pressure during IV use; heart rate, PR interval, QRS/rhythm identity, symptoms, ventricular function, liver/renal context, and relevant digoxin or interacting-drug levels.

  • In toxicity, serial glucose, potassium, calcium, magnesium, acid-base status, lactate, urine output, and bedside echocardiography guide high-dose insulin, calcium, vasopressor, pacing, and mechanical-support decisions.

Nclex Traps

  • Diltiazem and verapamil slow the AV node; amlodipine and other dihydropyridines do not provide equivalent rate control.

  • An AV-node blocker can be lifesaving in ordinary AV-node-dependent SVT and dangerous in pre-excited atrial fibrillation. Rhythm mechanism changes the answer.

Contraindications

  • Unpaced sick sinus syndrome or second-/third-degree AV block, severe hypotension or cardiogenic shock, acute decompensated heart failure, and serious hypersensitivity.

  • Atrial fibrillation/flutter with an accessory pathway, ventricular tachycardia, and close IV coadministration with a beta blocker; product-specific restrictions also apply.

Description

Diltiazem and verapamil are the cardiac branch of the calcium channel blocker family and the major Class IV antiarrhythmics. They slow SA-node automaticity, prolong AV-node conduction and refractoriness, and reduce myocardial contractility while also dilating arteries. Diltiazem is generally less negatively inotropic than verapamil, whereas verapamil has stronger cardiac and constipation effects. Their ability to slow the AV node makes them useful for selected supraventricular rhythms, but the same mechanism can cause bradycardia, high-grade heart block, hypotension, or decompensated heart failure. They are especially dangerous when the atria are fibrillating through an accessory pathway because blocking the AV node can leave the bypass tract as the dominant route to the ventricles and precipitate an extremely rapid ventricular rhythm.

Adverse Effects

  • Bradycardia, PR prolongation, first- through third-degree AV block, hypotension, dizziness, edema, fatigue, and worsening heart failure; constipation is especially characteristic of verapamil.

  • Asystole, ventricular fibrillation after rhythm misdiagnosis, severe cardiogenic shock, hepatic injury, and prolonged toxic conduction/myocardial depression in overdose.
