Definition

Hematuria means red blood cells are present in the urine. Gross hematuria is visible blood, while microscopic hematuria is found only when a urine sample is examined; the finding describes blood in urine but does not identify the cause.

Plain Language

Hematuria means blood is in the urine, even when the urine looks normal and the blood is visible only under a microscope.

Why It Matters

Hematuria can come from infection or a stone, but it can also signal glomerular kidney disease, trauma, urinary obstruction, or cancer. A positive dipstick must be confirmed with microscopy, and visible blood, clots, inability to urinate, major trauma, unstable vital signs, or persistent unexplained microscopic blood needs timely evaluation.

Assessment Method

Ask whether blood was visible and about clots, pain, fever, trauma, menstruation or vaginal bleeding, exercise, procedures, medications, smoking, and urinary symptoms; inspect a fresh specimen when available, obtain a correctly collected urinalysis with microscopy, and assess blood pressure, perfusion, flank and suprapubic findings, urine output, creatinine, protein, and casts.

Positive Finding

Urine visibly contains blood or a properly collected microscopic urinalysis confirms urinary red blood cells even when urine looks normal.

Mechanism

Bleeding, inflammation, or filtration-barrier injury allows red blood cells to enter urine anywhere from the glomerulus to the urethra; free hemoglobin, myoglobin, foods, or medications can change color or a dipstick result without intact urinary red blood cells.

Clinical Interpretation

The Hematuria laboratory finding raises concern for red blood cells entering urine from glomerular disease, urinary-tract inflammation or infection, stones, trauma, instrumentation, vascular injury, bleeding disorders, or kidney, ureter, bladder, or prostate tumors because bleeding, inflammation, or filtration-barrier injury allows red blood cells to enter urine anywhere from the glomerulus to the urethra; free hemoglobin, myoglobin, foods, or medications can change color or a dipstick result without intact urinary red blood cells. Evidence Limitations

A heme-positive dipstick does not prove red blood cells and must be confirmed microscopically; menstruation, vaginal bleeding, collection trauma, exercise, foods, and medications can confuse the source, while normal urine color does not exclude microscopic hematuria. Therefore, Hematuria cannot confirm or exclude red blood cells entering urine from glomerular disease, urinary-tract inflammation or infection, stones, trauma, instrumentation, vascular injury, bleeding disorders, or kidney, ureter, bladder, or prostate tumors by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • hemoglobinuria

  • myoglobinuria

  • beeturia

  • vaginal or menstrual blood contamination

False Positive Situations

A false-positive interpretation can result from specimen quality, preparation or staining artifact, and morphologic overlap with hemoglobinuria, myoglobinuria, beeturia, vaginal or menstrual blood contamination.

False Negative Situations

Hematuria may be missed when the specimen is delayed, diluted, poorly prepared, altered by treatment or transfusion, or the abnormal cells or material are sparse.

Modern Status

Current important urinary finding, not a diagnosis; classify visible versus microscopic and glomerular versus nonglomerular patterns, then use risk- and cause-directed evaluation. Safety Precautions

For Hematuria, verify the correct patient and the specimen, collection, handling, preparation, and laboratory method used to identify this finding. The reviewed precaution is: Do not dismiss hematuria as anticoagulation or a routine infection without follow-up; heavy bleeding, clots with inability to void, hypotension, syncope, major trauma, blood at the urethral opening, severe flank pain, fever with instability, pregnancy concern, rapidly falling output, or acute kidney injury requires urgent evaluation, and suspected urethral injury should not be blindly instrumented.
