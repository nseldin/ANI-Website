Risk Factors

  • Large infarct, uncontrolled pressure, anticoagulant exposure, severe hyperglycemia, and vessel injury increase hemorrhagic or edema risk.

  • Atrial fibrillation may be intermittent, so a normal first ECG does not exclude cardioembolism.

  • Prematurely labeling a stroke cryptogenic can miss dissection, cardiac thrombus, shunt, malignancy, or intracranial atherosclerosis because testing depth must match the patient.

Signs Symptoms

  • New headache, vomiting, acute hypertension, worsening NIHSS item, reduced consciousness, or seizure suggests intracranial complication until urgent imaging excludes it.

  • Tongue or lip swelling, stridor, or dyspnea after thrombolysis suggests orolingual angioedema and threatens airway.

  • Groin or radial swelling, back/flank pain, falling BP, anemia, cool limb, absent pulse, or severe access pain suggests procedure-site bleeding or ischemia.

Diagnostics

  • Repeat NCCT immediately for severe headache, acute hypertension, nausea or vomiting, or neurologic worsening after IVT, stopping an alteplase infusion still in progress, because symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage must be excluded urgently.

  • Obtain scheduled CT or MRI at about 24 hours before antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy under the post-IVT pathway because clinically silent hemorrhage may still change safety.

  • Use telemetry, ECG, echocardiography when indicated, and longer rhythm monitoring when occult atrial fibrillation remains plausible because one short tracing can miss paroxysmal disease.

  • Image extracranial and intracranial arteries and select dissection, shunt, inflammatory, malignancy, thrombophilia, or genetic testing based on age, pattern, history, and preliminary findings.

Labs

  • Trend glucose, CBC, platelets, coagulation, electrolytes, renal function, and other labs according to therapy and symptoms because bleeding, contrast, metabolic change, and medication safety differ.

  • A falling hemoglobin with hypotension or access pain can indicate occult procedural bleeding even without visible blood.

  • Lipids and A1c help quantify modifiable vascular risk, while specialized coagulation tests are reserved for contexts where results will change management.

Treatments

  • Activate hemorrhage, airway, seizure, edema, re-occlusion, or access-complication pathways immediately when triggered because post-reperfusion decline is not routine recovery.

  • Use protocol BP, glucose, temperature, oxygenation, antithrombotic timing, mobility, and swallowing precautions because treatment-related and infarct-related risks overlap.

  • Match secondary prevention to cause - anticoagulation for appropriate cardioembolic risk, antiplatelet and vascular-risk therapy for noncardioembolic mechanisms, and selected carotid or other intervention - because one prevention plan does not fit every stroke.

Nursing Priorities

  • Monitor neurologic findings and blood pressure at protocol intervals after thrombolysis or thrombectomy because early NIH Stroke Scale worsening may indicate hemorrhage, reocclusion, edema, or reperfusion injury before other signs appear.

  • Assess airway, oxygenation, temperature, glucose, swallowing, urine, headache, nausea, puncture sites, distal circulation, and visible bleeding because post-reperfusion complications involve both brain and treatment access.

  • Maintain the ordered treatment-specific blood-pressure range and avoid unapproved antithrombotic or invasive procedures because excessive pressure and premature hemostatic interference increase hemorrhage risk while hypotension can threaten reperfused tissue.

  • Coordinate rhythm monitoring, vascular imaging, cardiac evaluation, lipids, glycated hemoglobin, and selected thrombophilia or structural studies because identifying atrial, arterial, cardiac, and systemic sources determines secondary prevention.

  • Escalate immediately for NIH Stroke Scale increase, severe headache, vomiting, acute hypertension, reduced consciousness, tongue swelling, expanding access hematoma, absent distal pulse, or hypotension because intracranial hemorrhage, angioedema, and access bleeding require emergency treatment.

Complications

  • Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, cerebral edema, herniation, seizure, re-occlusion, distal embolization, and no-reflow can worsen brain injury.

  • Angioedema, aspiration, DVT/PE, infection, falls, pressure injury, access-site bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, and limb ischemia can threaten recovery.

  • Wrong mechanism classification can cause recurrent stroke when the prevention strategy fails to address the source.

Contraindications

  • Do not assume successful reperfusion means the patient is stable.

  • Do not intensively lower systolic BP below 140 after successful EVT as a routine strategy because the 2026 guideline identifies harm/no benefit; follow the current protocol.

  • Do not call a stroke cryptogenic before a workup appropriate to age, infarct pattern, vascular findings, rhythm risk, and clinical context.

Red Flags

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, new deficit, severe headache, vomiting, or acute hypertension

  • Reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, seizure, posturing, aspiration, or airway swelling

  • Expanding puncture-site hematoma, uncontrolled bleeding, hypotension, or falling hemoglobin

  • Absent distal pulse, cool painful access limb, recurrent focal deficit, or new dysrhythmia

Patient Education

  • Report any new weakness, speech change, headache, nausea, mouth swelling, or access-site swelling immediately because complications are most treatable when recognized early.

  • Complete the heart, vessel, and risk-factor workup even after full recovery because preventing another stroke depends on finding its mechanism.

Nclex Traps

  • Reperfusion success is a new monitoring phase, not the end of stroke care.

  • More aggressive BP lowering is not automatically better after EVT.

  • A normal admission ECG does not exclude paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and cryptogenic means unexplained after adequate evaluation, not no cause exists.

Name

Post-reperfusion stroke monitoring and cause workup

Category

Stroke critical care and secondary prevention

Definition

Post-reperfusion stroke care has two linked goals: detect early complications after IV thrombolysis or thrombectomy, and identify why the stroke occurred so recurrence prevention targets the mechanism. Reopening an artery does not end the emergency because hemorrhage, edema, re-occlusion, angioedema, reperfusion injury, access-site bleeding, and systemic instability can evolve. Cause workup distinguishes cardioembolism, large-artery atherosclerosis, small-vessel disease, other determined causes, and events that remain cryptogenic after adequate evaluation.

Etiology

Early deterioration can result from intracranial hemorrhage, failed or lost reperfusion, distal embolization, cerebral edema, seizure, aspiration, hypoxemia, hypotension, fever, or glucose disturbance. Recurrence risk may arise from atrial fibrillation, ventricular or valvular thrombus, carotid or intracranial plaque, dissection, penetrating-artery disease, cancer-associated thrombosis, inflammatory vasculopathy, or another specific mechanism.

Pathology

Ischemic microvessels and the blood-brain barrier are fragile after infarction and reperfusion. Fibrinolysis weakens hemostatic plugs, while endovascular access adds arterial injury and bleeding risk. Even complete large-vessel reperfusion may leave established core, microvascular no-reflow, edema, or emboli in new territories. At the same time, the original embolic source may still be active.

Pathophysiology

Frequent neurologic and BP checks look for change before secondary injury becomes irreversible. The 2025 TNKase label requires frequent BP monitoring and warns that bleeding can be serious or fatal. The 2026 guideline pathway keeps BP below 180/105 mm Hg for at least 24 hours after IVT and at or below that level during and for 24 hours after EVT because post-reperfusion hypertension increases hemorrhage risk. It also advises against intensive systolic lowering below 140 mm Hg after successful EVT because trials showed no functional benefit and possible harm; reperfusion-stage targets therefore follow the current stroke protocol rather than ordinary outpatient goals. Glucose below 60 mg/dL is treated, while intensive 80-to-130 control is avoided because it increases severe hypoglycemia without improving outcome. Cause testing links pattern to prevention: cortical multifocal infarcts and atrial fibrillation suggest embolism, territorial disease suggests large artery, and small deep lacunes suggest penetrating-vessel disease, but overlap requires evidence.
