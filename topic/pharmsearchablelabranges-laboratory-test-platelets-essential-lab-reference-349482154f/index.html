Name

Platelets

Range

A common adult platelet-count interval is about 150,000-400,000/µL (150-400 × 10^9/L), but the reporting laboratory, age, pregnancy, specimen quality, clinical context, and local critical-value and procedure policies govern interpretation. A platelet count measures number, not function; bleeding risk and decisions about procedures or platelet transfusion are not determined by one universal count alone.

Why

An abnormal platelet-count result signals altered primary hemostasis, the platelet-plug phase of clotting. A low count raises concern for impaired production, immune destruction such as ITP, consumption in DIC or sepsis, pregnancy-related disorders, medicine effect, or splenic sequestration; a new fall after heparin exposure can raise concern for HIT, a prothrombotic reaction in which thrombosis may be limb- or life-threatening. A high count can be reactive to infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, blood loss or splenectomy, or arise from a myeloproliferative disorder. Promptly assess petechiae, purpura, bruising, mucosal or menstrual bleeding, hematuria or gastrointestinal bleeding, neurologic change, signs of thrombosis, recent platelet trend, medicines and heparin exposure, infection or pregnancy context, and planned invasive procedures. Cause-directed evaluation can include repeat complete blood count, blood smear to confirm the count and morphology, coagulation and hemolysis studies, liver and kidney tests, HIT probability and testing when clinically indicated, and bone-marrow or other specialist evaluation as ordered. Escalate immediately for major bleeding, acute neurologic change, shock or hemodynamic instability, or limb ischemia or other suspected thrombosis. One platelet count does not diagnose ITP, HIT, DIC, HELLP syndrome, marrow disease, or platelet dysfunction; interpret the count and trend with symptoms, specimen quality, other cell lines, coagulation findings, medicines, pregnancy status, and the full clinical context.

High Causes

  • Reactive infection or inflammation

  • Iron deficiency or blood loss

  • After splenectomy

  • Myeloproliferative neoplasm

Low Causes

  • Immune thrombocytopenia

  • DIC or sepsis

  • Pregnancy-related thrombocytopenia, preeclampsia, or HELLP syndrome

  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia or another medicine effect

  • Bone-marrow suppression or failure

  • Splenic sequestration or dilution after massive transfusion

Critical Concerns

  • Major bleeding, acute neurologic change, shock or hemodynamic instability requires immediate escalation.

  • A new platelet fall after heparin exposure plus limb ischemia or another suspected thrombosis raises concern for HIT; follow the facility's urgent HIT pathway rather than assuming a low count protects against clotting.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess skin and mucosa, urine and stool, neurologic status, vital signs, thrombosis signs, recent procedures, medicines and heparin exposure, pregnancy context, and the platelet trend; apply bleeding precautions according to severity and policy.

  • Verify an unexpected or clinically discordant result and anticipate repeat CBC, smear, coagulation or hemolysis studies, and cause-directed testing as ordered.

Nclex Traps

  • A low platelet count increases bleeding concern but does not prove the cause; HIT is especially important because it causes thrombosis despite thrombocytopenia.

  • A platelet count does not show whether platelets function normally, and no one count is an automatic procedure or transfusion rule across all settings.
