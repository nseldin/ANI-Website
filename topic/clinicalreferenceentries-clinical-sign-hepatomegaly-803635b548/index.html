Definition

Hepatomegaly means true enlargement of the liver. It can result from congestion, inflammation, fat accumulation, infiltrative disease, cysts, or cancer, but a liver edge that can be felt does not always mean the liver is enlarged.

Plain Language

Hepatomegaly means an enlarged liver.

Why It Matters

An enlarged liver is a clue to an underlying hepatic, cardiac, hematologic, infectious, metabolic, or malignant disorder. Tender enlargement with jaundice, fever, severe right-upper-abdominal pain, low blood pressure, bleeding, confusion, or worsening heart failure needs prompt escalation and cause-directed testing.

Associated Conditions

  • true liver enlargement from congestion, inflammation, fatty infiltration, infiltrative or hematologic disease, cysts, or primary or metastatic malignancy

Assessment Method

Inspect the right upper abdomen, estimate the upper and lower liver borders by careful percussion, palpate gently during inspiration for the edge, tenderness, consistency, and nodularity, compare with the clinical context, and use ultrasound or other ordered imaging when size or cause matters.

Positive Finding

The estimated liver span is larger than expected for the patient's body size or imaging confirms enlargement; a palpable lower edge alone does not prove hepatomegaly.

Mechanism

Hepatocytes, blood, fat, inflammatory tissue, tumor, cysts, or infiltrative material increase liver volume, while venous congestion can distend the organ with blood.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Hepatomegaly finding raises concern for true liver enlargement from congestion, inflammation, fatty infiltration, infiltrative or hematologic disease, cysts, or primary or metastatic malignancy because hepatocytes, blood, fat, inflammatory tissue, tumor, cysts, or infiltrative material increase liver volume, while venous congestion can distend the organ with blood. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Hepatomegaly does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Hepatomegaly to focus evaluation of true liver enlargement from congestion, inflammation, fatty infiltration, infiltrative or hematologic disease, cysts, or primary or metastatic malignancy and the reviewed alternatives: low diaphragm, Riedel lobe, right upper quadrant mass, distended gallbladder. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect the right upper abdomen, estimate the upper and lower liver borders by careful percussion, palpate gently during inspiration for the edge, tenderness, consistency, and nodularity, compare with the clinical context, and use ultrasound or other ordered imaging when size or cause matters. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Hepatomegaly as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Body habitus, examiner technique, bowel gas, a low diaphragm, emphysema, pleural effusion, and a normal anatomic variant such as Riedel lobe can make a normal-sized liver seem palpable or alter percussion. Therefore, Hepatomegaly cannot confirm or exclude true liver enlargement from congestion, inflammation, fatty infiltration, infiltrative or hematologic disease, cysts, or primary or metastatic malignancy by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as low diaphragm, Riedel lobe, right upper quadrant mass, distended gallbladder.

False Negative Situations

Hepatomegaly may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of true liver enlargement from congestion, inflammation, fatty infiltration, infiltrative or hematologic disease, cysts, or primary or metastatic malignancy.

Modern Status

Current clinically important organ-enlargement finding; it is not a diagnosis, and bedside estimation should be confirmed when the result changes care. Observe Hepatomegaly with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect the right upper abdomen, estimate the upper and lower liver borders by careful percussion, palpate gently during inspiration for the edge, tenderness, consistency, and nodularity, compare with the clinical context, and use ultrasound or other ordered imaging when size or cause matters.
