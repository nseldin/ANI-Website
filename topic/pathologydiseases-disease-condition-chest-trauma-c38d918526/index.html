Definition

Chest trauma develops when blunt or penetrating force injures the chest wall, pleura, lung, heart, great vessels, or diaphragm and can rapidly impair ventilation or circulation. The characteristic pattern is pain, bruising, crepitus, asymmetric movement, hypoxemia, absent breath sounds, shock, or a concerning mechanism. Confirm or stage it with primary trauma survey with bedside ultrasound and immediate portable radiography; distinguish it from isolated chest-wall pain from pneumothorax, hemothorax, tamponade, pulmonary contusion, aortic injury, and abdominal diaphragmatic injury. Signs Symptoms

  • Pain, swelling, deformity, wound depth, drainage, odor, tissue color, blistering, numbness/tingling, pulses, movement, sensation, capillary refill, fever, or mechanism of injury shapes tissue-risk assessment.

  • Escalate absent pulses, compartment syndrome signs, evisceration, circumferential burn compromise, spreading infection, necrosis, or pain out of proportion.

Diagnostics

  • primary trauma survey with bedside ultrasound and immediate portable radiography

  • CT angiography or chest CT in stable patients with suspected deep injury

  • serial respiratory, hemodynamic, hemoglobin, electrocardiographic, and imaging reassessment

  • Distinguish Chest trauma from isolated chest-wall pain from pneumothorax, hemothorax, tamponade, pulmonary contusion, aortic injury, and abdominal diaphragmatic injury.

Labs

  • CK and potassium may rise with muscle breakdown

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • albumin/prealbumin may reflect nutrition risk

Treatments

  • treat tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, and tamponade during the primary survey

  • provide analgesia and lung-expansion support

  • coordinate chest drainage, blood products, airway care, or surgery according to injury

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for absent unilateral breath sounds, worsening hypoxemia, hypotension, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation, increasing subcutaneous air, large bloody output, or respiratory fatigue because emergent thoracic intervention may be required.

  • Perform a trauma primary survey with cervical protection and inspect chest symmetry, wounds, paradoxical movement, tracheal position, breath sounds, oxygenation, and perfusion because tension pneumothorax and massive hemothorax can kill before imaging.

  • Apply oxygen and prepare immediate decompression or chest-tube support when ordered because trapped air or blood can collapse a lung and obstruct venous return.

  • Provide multimodal analgesia and reassess sedation, respiratory rate, cough, and inspiratory capacity because untreated rib pain causes splinting while excessive opioids worsen ventilation.

  • Encourage ordered pulmonary hygiene, supported coughing, incentive spirometry, turning, and early mobility because atelectasis and retained secretions can progress to pneumonia and respiratory failure.

Complications

  • tension physiology can complicate Chest trauma.

  • hemorrhagic shock can complicate Chest trauma.

  • cardiac arrest can complicate Chest trauma.

  • delayed pulmonary contusion or respiratory failure

  • pneumonia can complicate Chest trauma.

  • retained hemothorax can complicate Chest trauma.

  • venous thrombosis can complicate Chest trauma.

Red Flags

  • Absent unilateral breath sounds with hypotension or severe distress

  • Rapidly increasing oxygen need, respiratory fatigue, or cyanosis

  • Large or suddenly increased bloody chest-tube output

  • New tracheal deviation, distended neck veins, or expanding subcutaneous emphysema

Patient Education

  • Explain that effective pain control and deep breathing protect the lungs; shallow breathing from pain can cause pneumonia even when the fractures themselves are stable.

  • Seek urgent care after discharge for worsening breathlessness, fever, coughing blood, fainting, increasing chest pain, or new crackling swelling under the skin.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not wait for imaging before treating a clinically obvious tension pneumothorax

  • Pain control is a respiratory intervention because splinting promotes collapse and secretion retention
