Definition

Varus stress test is a bedside knee-stability maneuver. It is a clue to lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury. A positive finding means lateral joint opening or pain exceeds the other side.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Varus stress test means lateral joint opening or pain exceeds the other side.

Why It Matters

A positive Varus stress test response can signal lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury. The LCL and posterolateral structures normally resist varus angulation. Varus stress test cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury

Assessment Method

Apply controlled varus force at about 30 degrees and near extension.

Positive Finding

Lateral joint opening or pain exceeds the other side.

Mechanism

The LCL and posterolateral structures normally resist varus angulation.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Varus stress test response raises concern for lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury because the LCL and posterolateral structures normally resist varus angulation. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Varus stress test does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Varus stress test to focus evaluation of lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury and the reviewed alternatives: lateral meniscal pain, arthritis, fracture. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Apply controlled varus force at about 30 degrees and near extension. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Varus stress test as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Pain without gapping is nonspecific. Therefore, Varus stress test cannot confirm or exclude lateral collateral ligament or posterolateral injury by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • lateral meniscal pain

  • arthritis

  • fracture

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from lateral meniscal pain, arthritis, fracture, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Varus stress test may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Varus stress test, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Apply controlled varus force at about 30 degrees and near extension. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Assess fibular nerve function and avoid stress with fracture concern.
