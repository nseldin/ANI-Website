Used To Treat

The first minutes of a suspected poisoning or unknown ingestion, when airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, seizures, contamination, and a small set of time-critical antidotes matter more than naming every substance immediately.

Description

Poisoning is a dose-dependent drug or toxin injury that disrupts vital physiology—especially ventilation, perfusion, temperature, glucose, and cardiac conduction—and can kill before the substance is identified. First protect rescuers from fumes, powders, liquids, needles, or contaminated clothing, then assess airway protection, ventilation, oxygenation, perfusion, mental status, bedside glucose, temperature, and the ECG. This order matters because hypoventilation, shock, dysrhythmia, seizure, severe hypoglycemia, and hyperthermia can kill before a comprehensive toxicology result returns. A toxidrome is a pattern, not a diagnosis: small pupils with respiratory depression suggests opioid effect; dry hot skin, mydriasis, urinary retention, and delirium suggests antimuscarinic effect; sweating, mydriasis, agitation, hypertension, and hyperthermia suggests sympathomimetic effect; secretions, diarrhea, miosis, and weakness suggests cholinergic effect. Mixed drugs, hypoxia, infection, trauma, metabolic illness, and treatment can blur every pattern, so the pathway repeatedly checks physiology while the history is reconstructed.

Mechanism

Poisoning creates failure at different control points: central depressants reduce respiratory drive and airway reflexes; membrane-active drugs slow cardiac conduction; adrenergic drugs increase heat production and oxygen demand; insulin and secretagogues deprive the brain of glucose; cholinergic agents flood muscarinic and nicotinic synapses; and corrosives or gases injure tissue directly. Airway support, ventilation, circulation, glucose correction, seizure control, cooling, and external decontamination interrupt these final common pathways even before the agent is known. Serial ECGs, vital signs, examinations, and targeted concentrations are more useful than a one-time broad screen because absorption, redistribution, active metabolites, and delayed-release products change toxicity over time. A negative urine immunoassay cannot exclude many important poisons and usually cannot prove present impairment, so treatment follows the clinical syndrome rather than the screen alone.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for collapse, breathing difficulty, seizure, severe confusion, chest pain, shock, or intentional overdose; call Poison Help immediately for any possible poisoning and do not wait for symptoms. Remove the patient from an unsafe source only when rescuers can do so safely.

  • Perform a poisoning-focused primary survey: airway and aspiration risk; respiratory rate and effective ventilation; pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, and ECG; neurologic status and pupils; bedside glucose; core temperature; and complete skin exposure. Give cause-directed emergency treatment such as ventilatory support, glucose, naloxone for suspected opioid respiratory depression, benzodiazepines for toxicologic seizures, or rapid cooling when indicated.

  • After immediate threats are controlled, identify every prescription, nonprescription product, patch, plant, chemical, container, formulation, amount, time, route, access by other people, and co-exposure. Reassess serially because a normal first examination does not clear delayed-release, long-acting, body-packet, caustic, battery, or toxic-alcohol exposure.

Nursing Essentials

  • Use gloves and route-specific PPE, isolate contaminated belongings, remove external contamination under the correct chemical protocol, and prevent secondary exposure of staff. Never enter a toxic atmosphere without trained hazmat protection.

  • Place high-risk patients on continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring because respiratory depression, conduction toxicity, and shock can emerge abruptly; obtain IV or intraosseous access when needed, record glucose and temperature early, preserve medication packages, and document exact treatment times so that changing physiology can be linked to each intervention.

  • Use a calm, nonjudgmental history and assess self-harm, coercion, substance use, access by children, and safeguarding only after physiologic stabilization because immediate airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, and temperature threats take priority. Maintain observation appropriate to the product because delayed-release or long-acting toxicity can develop after one normal vital-sign set.

Key Labs

  • Bedside glucose and a 12-lead ECG are near-universal early tests in significant unknown poisoning. Add electrolytes, bicarbonate, creatinine, liver studies, blood gas, lactate, acetaminophen and salicylate concentrations, pregnancy testing, osmolality, co-oximetry, CK, and agent-specific studies according to history and physiology.

  • Interpret every concentration by sampling time, formulation, units, kidney or liver function, and treatment already given because these factors change whether the result represents an early peak, delayed absorption, or impaired clearance. A routine urine drug screen cannot replace serial examination or targeted quantitative testing because its false negatives, false positives, incomplete panels, and long detection windows do not reliably identify current toxicity.

Adverse Effects

  • Unrecognized poisoning can progress to aspiration, respiratory arrest, shock, dysrhythmia, seizure, cerebral injury, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, kidney or liver failure, and cardiac arrest after an initially nonspecific presentation.

  • Rescue can also cause harm: excessive oxygen in a few specific exposures, antidote-triggered withdrawal or seizure, concentrated dextrose extravasation, fluid overload, alkalemia, hypothermia from cooling, or aspiration during unselected charcoal. Each intervention therefore needs a defined physiologic target and reassessment.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce vomiting, give home charcoal, force oral fluids, or try chemical neutralization. Do not put anything in the mouth of a drowsy, seizing, or vomiting patient because aspiration can become the immediate lethal event.

  • Do not delay ventilation, chest compressions, defibrillation, glucose, cooling, or a clearly indicated antidote while waiting for a toxicology screen or exact product identification because lethal physiologic failure can progress before those answers return. Conversely, do not give every antidote empirically because an antidote's own mechanism could worsen a mixed overdose.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Refractory shock, recurrent seizure, QRS or QT abnormality, severe acidosis, hyperthermia, hypoventilation, coma, progressive organ injury, or a plausible long-acting exposure requires ICU, medical-toxicology and poison-center input, and early transfer planning for dialysis, hyperbaric therapy, endoscopy, burn care, or ECMO when the specific mechanism warrants it.

Interactions

  • Mixed sedatives can produce greater respiratory depression than any single reported dose, while stimulant-plus-depressant combinations can hide toxicity until one component wears off. Treat the observed physiology and anticipate the next phase.

  • Therapeutic medicines can change interpretation: beta blockers may hide adrenergic warning signs, anticholinergics can delay gastric emptying, extended-release products delay peaks, and kidney failure can turn an ordinary maintenance dose into prolonged toxicity.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A single adult tablet, concentrated liquid, patch, or button battery can be catastrophic; use measured weight and child-specific observation thresholds.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Stabilize maternal oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, glucose, and temperature first because fetal rescue depends on maternal physiology, then add obstetric assessment.

  • Population

    Older adults and chronic disease

    Note

    Polypharmacy, reduced clearance, baseline ECG abnormalities, cognitive impairment, and limited cardiopulmonary reserve make history and apparent dose less reliable.

Evidence Limitations

  • Critical-poisoning evidence often comes from observational cohorts, case series, pharmacology, and expert consensus. This pathway organizes early priorities but cannot replace product-specific Poison Help or medical-toxicology guidance, local resuscitation protocols, or serial bedside judgment.

Nclex Traps

  • Treat airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, and seizures before chasing a complete toxin name.

  • A toxidrome is a working pattern that mixed ingestion and ordinary medical disease can distort.

  • A negative routine urine drug screen does not rule out clinically important poisoning because many dangerous agents are absent from routine panels or fall below immunoassay detection.

Regulatory Status

This is an emergency decision pathway, not a drug indication. Antidotes and procedures within it have product-specific labels, off-label toxicology uses, and local protocols; Poison Help and a medical toxicologist individualize selection.

Boxed Warning

UNKNOWN-POISONING WARNING: call 911 for breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure, severe confusion, chest pain, or shock, and call Poison Help immediately. Support airway, ventilation, circulation, glucose, temperature, and rhythm before waiting for a drug screen because lethal physiology can progress before screening identifies an agent; never induce vomiting or give oral treatment to an unprotected airway because aspiration can become the immediate lethal event.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
