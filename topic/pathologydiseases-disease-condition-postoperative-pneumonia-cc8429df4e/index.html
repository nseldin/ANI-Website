Definition

Postoperative pneumonia develops when anesthesia, pain, aspiration, immobility, impaired cough, and devices allow lower-respiratory infection after surgery. The characteristic pattern is new infiltrate with fever, leukocytosis, purulent secretions, worsening oxygenation, or respiratory decline after the immediate atelectasis period. Confirm or stage it with chest imaging plus oxygenation and respiratory assessment; distinguish it from atelectasis, aspiration pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, embolism, and ARDS by timing, imaging, cultures, secretions, and hemodynamics. The priority is to give timely empiric antibiotics matched to setting and resistance risk, then narrow because sepsis can complicate Postoperative pneumonia. can follow if the process advances.

Mechanistically, anesthesia, pain, aspiration, immobility, impaired cough, and devices allow lower-respiratory infection after surgery.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • chest imaging plus oxygenation and respiratory assessment

  • blood and good-quality respiratory cultures for severe or hospital-acquired disease without delaying antibiotics

  • evaluation for aspiration, resistant organisms, fluid overload, embolism, and source control

  • Distinguish Postoperative pneumonia from atelectasis, aspiration pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, embolism, and ARDS by timing, imaging, cultures, secretions, and hemodynamics.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • give timely empiric antibiotics matched to setting and resistance risk, then narrow

  • support oxygenation, secretion clearance, analgesia, mobility, and swallowing safety

  • drain empyema or address aspiration and device sources

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for rapidly increasing oxygen need, respiratory fatigue, hypotension, confusion, rising lactate, absent breath sounds, or failure to improve because empyema, obstruction, embolism, or resistant infection may be present.

  • Assess cough strength, breath sounds, work of breathing, oxygen requirement, temperature, swallowing safety, sedation, and mobility because atelectasis, aspiration, and infection often coexist after surgery.

  • Position upright, mobilize as soon as safe, coach deep breathing and supported coughing, and provide oral care because lung expansion and reduced oropharyngeal burden lower secretion retention and aspiration risk.

  • Obtain ordered respiratory and blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible without delaying therapy because microbiology allows narrowing and distinguishes resistant hospital pathogens.

  • Administer prescribed antibiotics and analgesia while avoiding unnecessary oversedation because pain limits ventilation but excessive sedation suppresses cough and airway protection.

  • Trend oxygen need, respiratory rate, secretions, temperature, white count, imaging, hemodynamics, and response at 48 to 72 hours to detect respiratory failure, abscess, or sepsis.

Complications

  • sepsis can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

  • respiratory failure can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

  • empyema can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

  • abscess can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

  • delirium can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

  • prolonged hospitalization can complicate Postoperative pneumonia.

Red Flags

  • Rapidly increasing oxygen requirement or respiratory fatigue

  • Hypotension, new confusion, rising lactate, or oliguria

  • Sudden unilateral absent breath sounds or acute pleuritic deterioration

  • Persistent fever and worsening infiltrates despite appropriate therapy

Patient Education

  • Explain that walking, upright meals, mouth care, and supported coughing help prevent secretions and food material from settling in the lungs.

  • Teach the patient to splint the incision, use prescribed breathing equipment correctly, and report worsening breathlessness, fever, confusion, or bloody sputum.

Nclex Traps

  • A postoperative infiltrate is not automatically bacterial pneumonia

  • Respiratory culture colonization should not override the clinical syndrome
