Used To Treat

Life-threatening agitation, hyperthermia, hypertension, coronary vasospasm, seizure, dysrhythmia, or shock caused by cocaine, methamphetamine, amphetamines, MDMA, synthetic cathinones, or another sympathomimetic.

Description

Sympathomimetic stimulant poisoning amplifies catecholamine signaling and produces a hyperadrenergic emergency. Agitation, paranoia, sweating, dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension, chest pain, hyperreflexia, and hyperthermia occur because norepinephrine, dopamine, and sometimes serotonin signaling overwhelms normal control. The dangerous heat is generated by relentless muscle activity and adrenergic metabolism, not by a reset fever thermostat; sedation and rapid external cooling therefore matter more than antipyretics. Prolonged physical struggle or restraint without sedation increases muscle work, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, and sudden cardiovascular collapse. Cocaine adds coronary vasoconstriction and fast sodium-channel blockade, so ischemia, wide QRS, ventricular dysrhythmia, and seizure can coexist. MDMA can also produce hyponatremia from water excess and antidiuretic effects. A quiet patient after severe agitation is not automatically recovered; exhaustion, intracranial hemorrhage, hyperthermic organ injury, or mixed depressants may be emerging.

Mechanism

Cocaine blocks reuptake of norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin and also blocks fast cardiac sodium channels. Amphetamines and methamphetamine promote monoamine release and reverse transport, while synthetic cathinones vary across these actions. The resulting alpha-mediated vasoconstriction raises afterload and limits coronary flow, beta stimulation raises heart rate and oxygen demand, and central activation drives agitation, seizure, and muscle-generated heat. Sedation reduces central catecholamine output and muscle work, which lowers temperature, blood pressure, acidosis, and oxygen demand at the same time. Rapid external cooling removes accumulated heat; vasodilators relieve severe vasoconstriction or coronary spasm; and sodium bicarbonate can address cocaine-associated sodium-channel blockade when QRS widening or ventricular dysrhythmia indicates that distinct mechanism. No single antidote removes all stimulant effects.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for severe agitation, chest pain, seizure, temperature elevation, neurologic deficit, collapse, or suspected body packing. Reduce stimulation, protect staff, check glucose and core temperature, obtain an ECG, and use protocol-directed sedation promptly rather than relying on prolonged physical restraint.

  • Treat life-threatening hyperthermia with rapid active external cooling while sedation stops further heat production. Support airway and ventilation, correct hypoglycemia and major electrolyte abnormalities, and use benzodiazepines for seizures; antipyretics do not correct muscle-generated heat.

  • For persistent severe hypertension, coronary vasospasm, wide-QRS toxicity, dysrhythmia, or shock, involve Poison Help and medical toxicology early because vasodilator, bicarbonate, antiarrhythmic, vasopressor, lipid, or ECMO choices depend on the dominant mechanism rather than a single stimulant label.

Nursing Essentials

  • Measure core temperature rather than relying on a peripheral reading, minimize struggle and sensory stimulation, use the least restrictive safe approach, and reassess respiratory effort after sedation because exhaustion or co-ingested opioids can appear once agitation resolves.

  • Monitor ECG, blood pressure, oxygenation, end-tidal carbon dioxide when available, urine output, mental status, skin perfusion, and limb or chest pain because ischemia, dysrhythmia, hypoventilation, shock, and rhabdomyolysis can evolve after agitation improves. Record cooling start, temperature trajectory, sedative response, and restraint duration so that recurrent heat or hyperadrenergic activity is detected promptly.

  • Ask about route, amount, time, repeated dosing, body stuffing or packing, sexual exposure context, adulterants, prescription stimulants, antidepressants, and fentanyl contamination. Offer substance-use treatment and naloxone access after the emergency without allowing counseling to delay resuscitation.

Key Labs

  • Obtain glucose, electrolytes including sodium and potassium, bicarbonate, creatinine, CK, urinalysis, blood gas and lactate, troponin when ischemia is possible, pregnancy testing when relevant, and serial ECGs because hyperthermia and catecholamine excess can produce electrolyte shifts, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, kidney injury, ischemia, and conduction instability. Use CT or other imaging for focal deficits, severe headache, trauma, or suspected packets because these clues raise concern for hemorrhage, occult injury, or retained drug burden.

  • A urine stimulant result neither measures severity nor excludes a different stimulant, and cocaine metabolites can persist after acute effects. Clinical temperature, neurologic, cardiovascular, renal, and acid-base trends guide treatment.

Adverse Effects

  • Complications include myocardial ischemia, aortic catastrophe, intracranial hemorrhage, seizure, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, kidney failure, hepatic injury, disseminated coagulopathy, pulmonary edema, dysrhythmia, shock, and death.

  • Sedatives can cause hypoventilation or hypotension; aggressive cooling can cause shivering and overshoot hypothermia; and poorly matched antihypertensive or antiarrhythmic therapy can worsen shock or conduction. Continuous reassessment keeps rescue aligned with the active mechanism.

Contraindications

  • Do not use prolonged physical restraint without effective sedation in severe stimulant agitation because struggle increases catecholamine release, acidosis, heat generation, and sudden-collapse risk. Do not use acetaminophen or ibuprofen as the primary treatment for toxic hyperthermia.

  • In life-threatening acute cocaine toxicity with coronary vasospasm or hypertensive emergency, prioritize sedation and short-acting vasodilators; do not reflexively give a pure beta blocker. AHA 2025 reports no supporting adult evidence, describes possible worsened vasoconstriction, and recommends against beta-adrenergic antagonists in children. ASAM treats the area as controversial and says that if a specialist still considers a beta blocker for stimulant intoxication, a combined alpha-1 and beta agent such as labetalol is preferred. That conditional guidance does not make beta blockade routine rescue therapy.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Core hyperthermia, refractory agitation or seizure, ischemic ECG or troponin, focal neurologic deficit, severe acidosis, rising CK or potassium, wide QRS, ventricular dysrhythmia, shock, or cardiac arrest requires ICU and toxicology; refractory cardiogenic shock or arrest may warrant early ECMO-center consultation.

Interactions

  • Serotonergic drugs can shift an MDMA or amphetamine presentation toward serotonin toxicity with clonus and hyperreflexia, while antipsychotics or anticholinergics can alter thermoregulation and the examination.

  • Fentanyl, alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other depressants can mask or follow stimulant toxicity; naloxone treats opioid respiratory depression but does not reverse stimulant hyperthermia, ischemia, or sodium-channel blockade.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Exploratory ingestion and prescription-dose errors can cause severe hypertension, agitation, or hyperthermia at small absolute amounts; use measured weight and pediatric sedation protocols.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal hyperthermia, hypertension, ischemia, and placental vasoconstriction threaten both patients; stabilize immediately and add fetal and obstetric assessment when viable.

  • Population

    Cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease

    Note

    Reduced vascular reserve increases ischemia, hemorrhage, and dysrhythmia risk even when the reported dose seems modest.

Evidence Limitations

  • Randomized evidence in life-threatening stimulant poisoning is limited, and street products have uncertain identity and dose. AHA and ASAM/AAAP guidance supports syndrome-based sedation, cooling, and complication treatment, with specialist selection of cardiovascular rescue.

Nclex Traps

  • Sweating helps distinguish a sympathomimetic pattern from the typically dry antimuscarinic pattern, but mixed exposure can blur both.

  • Toxic hyperthermia requires sedation and rapid cooling because antipyretics do not stop muscle heat production.

  • Cocaine can cause both catecholamine excess and sodium-channel blockade, so a wide QRS changes treatment priorities.

Regulatory Status

This is a syndrome pathway. Sedatives, vasodilators, bicarbonate, and cooling are used under emergency protocols; none is a universal FDA-approved antidote for all sympathomimetics.

Boxed Warning

STIMULANT-TOXICITY WARNING: call 911 for severe agitation, chest pain, seizure, neurologic deficit, collapse, or hyperthermia. Sedate and cool a dangerously hot patient promptly; prolonged struggle or restraint without sedation increases acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and sudden cardiovascular collapse.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
