Used To Treat

Serious susceptible lower-respiratory, urinary, intra-abdominal, gynecologic, skin/soft-tissue, bone/joint, endocardial, bloodstream, and polymicrobial infections. It covers susceptible Pseudomonas and many anaerobes but not MRSA, atypicals, Stenotrophomonas, or every carbapenem-resistant organism; it is not the routine meningitis carbapenem.

Mechanism

Imipenem binds several PBPs and stops peptidoglycan transpeptidation. Its broad stability does not include KPC, MBL, and other carbapenemases or all porin/efflux combinations. Cilastatin competitively inhibits brush-border DHP-I, a human enzyme, and therefore solves drug metabolism rather than microbial resistance. Renal clearance determines both components' exposure. Imipenem can antagonize GABA-mediated inhibition at high CNS concentrations, explaining myoclonus and seizure. Like other carbapenems, it rapidly lowers valproate concentrations through disrupted valproate-glucuronide recycling, creating a second seizure pathway.

Contraindications

  • Serious hypersensitivity to imipenem, cilastatin, or related beta-lactams requires avoidance; the IM lidocaine formulation has additional lidocaine contraindications.

  • Do not use as routine meningitis therapy because seizure risk and product indication make meropenem or another regimen more appropriate when a carbapenem is needed.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check current creatinine clearance, CNS/seizure history, valproate use, culture/MIC, and exact formulation before dosing because the largest preventable harms arise from accumulation and interaction.

  • For new confusion, aphasia, myoclonus, or reduced responsiveness, hold/escalate per protocol and consider EEG because nonconvulsive status can appear before a dramatic convulsion.

Interactions

  • Valproic acid/divalproex concentration can fall rapidly and profoundly; avoid coadministration when possible because increasing valproate often fails to overcome the interaction.

  • Ganciclovir and other proconvulsant drugs can compound seizure risk; probenecid increases carbapenem exposure and is generally avoided.

Key Labs

  • Culture/MIC and carbapenemase data, creatinine/eGFR, urine output, CBC, liver tests, mental status, and valproate level when exposure occurs.

  • Cilastatin does not need a bacterial susceptibility result because its target is human DHP-I; imipenem susceptibility determines antibacterial fit.

Nclex Traps

  • Cilastatin protects imipenem from a human renal enzyme, not from bacterial beta-lactamase.

  • Imipenem covers susceptible Pseudomonas; ertapenem does not.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Seizures and other CNS adverse effects occur especially with renal impairment, CNS disease, or excessive dose. Carbapenems markedly lower valproate concentrations and can precipitate breakthrough seizures.

Class Pathway

  • Imipenem binds multiple PBPs

  • Cilastatin blocks human renal DHP-I

  • Broad susceptible aerobic and anaerobic killing

Description

Imipenem/cilastatin, branded Primaxin, combines a broad PBP-binding carbapenem with a nonantibacterial inhibitor of a human kidney enzyme. Imipenem penetrates many Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria and resists numerous older beta-lactamases. In the proximal tubule, human renal dehydropeptidase I would hydrolyze imipenem into an inactive, nephrotoxic metabolite; cilastatin blocks that enzyme, preserves urinary/systemic imipenem, and reduces renal metabolite toxicity. Cilastatin does not inhibit bacterial beta-lactamases or broaden the organism spectrum. Imipenem has a prominent seizure risk, especially with CNS disease, renal impairment, or excessive exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, rash, fever, phlebitis, liver-test elevation, eosinophilia, cytopenias, allergy, and CDI.

  • Confusion, hallucinations, tremor, myoclonus, encephalopathy, nonconvulsive status, and generalized seizure; renal injury and superinfection can occur.
