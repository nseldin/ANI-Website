Definition

Neutropenia develops when the absolute neutrophil count falls from marrow suppression, destruction, sequestration, congenital disease, or drug effect, weakening early control of bacterial and fungal invasion. Signs Symptoms

  • Fatigue, dyspnea, pallor, bruising, petechiae, mucosal bleeding, fever, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, weight loss, neurologic change, or transfusion symptoms guide hematology/oncology priority.

  • Escalate neutropenic fever, active bleeding, severe anemia symptoms, tumor lysis signs, hypercalcemia crisis, or transfusion reaction.

Diagnostics

  • CBC with differential and absolute neutrophil count trend

  • blood cultures and site-directed cultures before antibiotics when this causes no delay

  • medication, chemotherapy, viral, nutritional, autoimmune, marrow, and splenic evaluation

  • Distinguish Neutropenia from isolated low count from normal variation, cyclic neutropenia, leukemia, aplasia, and sepsis consumption by trend, other cell lines, smear, and marrow context.

Labs

  • hemoglobin/hematocrit may fall with anemia or bleeding

  • platelets may fall with bleeding risk

  • ANC below 500 means severe neutropenia risk

  • calcium, potassium, phosphate, and uric acid matter in tumor lysis

Treatments

  • give immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics for febrile high-risk neutropenia

  • use hygiene, line, oral, food, and exposure precautions appropriate to risk

  • remove the cause and use growth-factor support when indicated

Nursing Priorities

  • Check temperature promptly and treat fever with severe neutropenia as an emergency because a limited inflammatory response can hide rapidly spreading infection.

  • Obtain ordered blood cultures from peripheral and central sites and administer prescribed broad antibiotic therapy promptly because delay increases sepsis risk.

  • Use meticulous hand hygiene, screen ill visitors, and avoid rectal temperatures, suppositories, and unnecessary invasive procedures because mucosal injury creates an infection portal.

  • Monitor absolute neutrophil count, blood pressure, oxygen need, mental status, lactate, urine output, oral mucosa, skin, lungs, lines, and perineum to detect subtle infection.

  • Escalate immediately for fever, hypothermia, hypotension, rigors, new cough, abdominal pain, confusion, or rapidly progressive skin change because neutropenic sepsis can deteriorate within hours.

Complications

  • septic shock can complicate Neutropenia.

  • invasive fungal infection can complicate Neutropenia.

  • mucositis can complicate Neutropenia.

  • enterocolitis can complicate Neutropenia.

  • abscess without pus can complicate Neutropenia.

  • death can complicate Neutropenia.

Red Flags

  • Fever or hypothermia with severe neutropenia

  • Hypotension, rising lactate, or altered mental status

  • New respiratory distress or focal pulmonary findings

  • Rapidly progressive abdominal, perineal, line, or skin findings

Patient Education

  • Teach daily temperature awareness, hand and mouth care, food and exposure guidance, and the oncology team's exact fever threshold.

  • Explain that infection may cause only fatigue, chills, cough, diarrhea, or confusion, so symptoms should be reported before taking fever-reducing medicine.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal-looking infection site does not exclude severe infection during neutropenia

  • Do not delay antibiotics for imaging in unstable febrile neutropenia
