Definition

Organophosphate poisoning develops when irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibition causes persistent muscarinic secretions, nicotinic weakness, and central nervous system toxicity. The characteristic pattern is pinpoint pupils, copious bronchial secretions, wheeze, vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, fasciculations, weakness, confusion, and seizures after pesticide exposure. Confirm or stage it with toxidrome and exposure recognition with immediate airway assessment; distinguish it from carbamate, opioid, nicotine, mushroom, and nerve-agent exposure by history, duration, fasciculation, secretion burden, and toxicology guidance. Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • toxidrome and exposure recognition with immediate airway assessment

  • serial oxygenation, ventilation, secretions, strength, ECG, temperature, and acid-base status

  • cholinesterase testing for support, not to delay antidotes

  • Distinguish Organophosphate poisoning from carbamate, opioid, nicotine, mushroom, and nerve-agent exposure by history, duration, fasciculation, secretion burden, and toxicology guidance.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • protect rescuers and remove contaminated clothing with skin decontamination

  • give escalating atropine until pulmonary secretions and bronchospasm improve

  • give pralidoxime early and support ventilation and seizures

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for copious secretions, severe bronchospasm, fasciculations with weakness, bradycardia, seizure, hypoxemia, or respiratory fatigue because repeated antidote and advanced airway support may be required.

  • Use appropriate personal protective equipment, remove contaminated clothing, and irrigate exposed skin because residue can continue absorption and secondarily poison staff.

  • Suction secretions, support ventilation, and administer atropine as ordered until bronchial secretions and oxygenation improve because respiratory failure is driven by secretions, bronchospasm, and weakness.

  • Administer ordered oxime therapy promptly when appropriate and monitor pupils, secretions, muscle strength, oxygen saturation, rhythm, and cholinergic recurrence to detect persistent acetylcholinesterase inhibition.

Complications

  • respiratory arrest can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

  • aspiration can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

  • seizure can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

  • dysrhythmia can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

  • intermediate syndrome can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

  • delayed neuropathy can complicate Organophosphate poisoning.

Red Flags

  • Copious bronchial secretions with hypoxemia

  • Severe bronchospasm or respiratory muscle weakness

  • Bradycardia, seizure, or rapidly declining consciousness

  • Recurrent cholinergic findings after initial improvement

Patient Education

  • Teach that contaminated clothing should be handled cautiously and placed according to hazardous-material instructions because residue can expose other people.

  • Explain that breathing weakness or recurrent secretions can occur after initial improvement, so the observation period should not be shortened without toxicology guidance.

Nclex Traps

  • Atropine treats muscarinic lung findings but does not reverse nicotinic paralysis

  • Contaminated clothing can poison healthcare workers
