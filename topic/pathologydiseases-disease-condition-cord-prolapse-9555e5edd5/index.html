Definition

Cord prolapse develops when the umbilical cord descends beside or beyond the presenting part and becomes compressed, abruptly reducing fetal oxygen delivery. The characteristic pattern is visible or palpable cord after membrane rupture with sudden prolonged fetal bradycardia or recurrent variable decelerations. Confirm or stage it with immediate fetal-heart-rate assessment and sterile examination when prolapse is suspected; distinguish it from cord compression without prolapse, placental abruption, uterine rupture, and maternal hypotension by examination and obstetric context. The priority is to manually elevate the presenting part and position the patient to reduce compression because fetal hypoxic-ischemic brain injury can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Age- or pregnancy-specific cues: feeding change, weak cry, tone change, respiratory effort, poor perfusion, abnormal fetal tracing, bleeding, fever or low temperature, dehydration, or caregiver concern.

  • Infants and pregnant/postpartum clients may deteriorate with subtle symptoms, so trend and context matter.

Diagnostics

  • immediate fetal-heart-rate assessment and sterile examination when prolapse is suspected

  • rapid confirmation of presentation and cervical status without delaying decompression

  • continuous fetal monitoring during preparation for birth

  • Distinguish Cord prolapse from cord compression without prolapse, placental abruption, uterine rupture, and maternal hypotension by examination and obstetric context.

Labs

  • trend abnormal values against baseline

  • watch electrolytes, CBC, renal function, liver function, and oxygenation when relevant

Treatments

  • manually elevate the presenting part and position the patient to reduce compression

  • avoid handling the exposed cord and keep it moist with warm saline dressing

  • perform immediate operative delivery unless vaginal birth is faster and imminent

Nursing Priorities

  • Call the obstetric emergency team and prepare immediate operative delivery because cord compression can abruptly interrupt fetal oxygen delivery.

  • Use a gloved hand to elevate the presenting part off the cord and maintain that relief until delivery because removing pressure preserves umbilical blood flow.

  • Position the patient knee-chest or steep head-down as directed and avoid handling an exposed cord because gravity may reduce compression while manipulation can cause vasospasm.

  • Escalate immediately for persistent fetal bradycardia, recurrent prolonged decelerations, a palpable cord after membrane rupture, or loss of cord pulsation while monitoring fetal heart rate because definitive delivery cannot be delayed.

  • Trend the disease-specific findings of visible or palpable cord after membrane rupture with sudden prolonged fetal bradycardia or recurrent variable decelerations and escalate immediately for fetal hypoxic-ischemic brain injury because the umbilical cord descends beside or beyond the presenting part and becomes compressed, abruptly reducing fetal oxygen delivery.

Complications

  • fetal hypoxic-ischemic brain injury

  • acidosis can complicate Cord prolapse.

  • stillbirth can complicate Cord prolapse.

  • maternal operative complications can complicate Cord prolapse.

Red Flags

  • Palpable or visible cord after rupture of membranes

  • Persistent fetal bradycardia or prolonged deceleration

  • Loss of cord pulsation

  • Fetal heart-rate abnormality after sudden fluid release with a high presenting part

Patient Education

  • Explain during the emergency that the team is relieving pressure from the umbilical cord while preparing rapid delivery, which helps the patient understand the unusual position and continuous examination.

  • After delivery, review what occurred, the newborn assessment, maternal recovery, and emotional support needs.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not try to push the cord back into the uterus

  • Relieving compression is a bridge to delivery, not definitive treatment
