Definition

Hemiplegia means paralysis, or complete to nearly complete loss of voluntary movement, on one side of the body. Partial one-sided weakness is hemiparesis.

Plain Language

The face, arm, leg, or the entire side cannot move voluntarily or has almost no movement.

Why It Matters

New hemiplegia usually signals a major neurologic emergency such as stroke. Immediate priorities include emergency response, last-known-well documentation, airway and swallowing assessment, and protection from falls, pressure injury, contracture, and blood clots.

Associated Conditions

  • complete or near-complete loss of voluntary movement on one side from major cerebral, brainstem, or corticospinal injury, most often stroke but also trauma, tumor, infection, demyelination, or other structural disease

Assessment Method

Assess and document voluntary movement in the face, arm, and leg separately, compare tone, reflexes, sensation and neglect, and evaluate speech, swallowing, level of consciousness, gait or transfer safety, skin protection, and the timing of onset.

Positive Finding

Voluntary movement is absent or nearly absent on one side of the body, rather than merely reduced as in hemiparesis.

Mechanism

Severe interruption of descending motor pathways prevents effective motor signals from reaching muscles on one side; associated findings and the side relative to cranial deficits help localize the lesion.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Hemiplegia finding raises concern for complete or near-complete loss of voluntary movement on one side from major cerebral, brainstem, or corticospinal injury, most often stroke but also trauma, tumor, infection, demyelination, or other structural disease because severe interruption of descending motor pathways prevents effective motor signals from reaching muscles on one side; associated findings and the side relative to cranial deficits help localize the lesion. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Hemiplegia does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Hemiplegia to focus evaluation of complete or near-complete loss of voluntary movement on one side from major cerebral, brainstem, or corticospinal injury, most often stroke but also trauma, tumor, infection, demyelination, or other structural disease and the reviewed alternatives: hemiparesis, postictal weakness, spinal cord lesion, functional paralysis. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Assess and document voluntary movement in the face, arm, and leg separately, compare tone, reflexes, sensation and neglect, and evaluate speech, swallowing, level of consciousness, gait or transfer safety, skin protection, and the timing of onset. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Hemiplegia as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Sedation, coma, pain, joint restriction, severe neglect, language impairment, and inability to understand commands can make voluntary movement difficult to judge; paralysis does not identify the cause by itself. Therefore, Hemiplegia cannot confirm or exclude complete or near-complete loss of voluntary movement on one side from major cerebral, brainstem, or corticospinal injury, most often stroke but also trauma, tumor, infection, demyelination, or other structural disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • hemiparesis

  • postictal weakness

  • spinal cord lesion

  • functional paralysis

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as hemiparesis, postictal weakness, spinal cord lesion, functional paralysis.

False Negative Situations

Hemiplegia may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of complete or near-complete loss of voluntary movement on one side from major cerebral, brainstem, or corticospinal injury, most often stroke but also trauma, tumor, infection, demyelination, or other structural disease.

Modern Status

Current major motor sign; the term is sometimes used loosely, so exact strength and distribution must be documented and partial weakness should be called hemiparesis. Safety Precautions

Observe Hemiplegia with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Assess and document voluntary movement in the face, arm, and leg separately, compare tone, reflexes, sensation and neglect, and evaluate speech, swallowing, level of consciousness, gait or transfer safety, skin protection, and the timing of onset. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: New hemiplegia is an emergency; activate stroke or neurologic response, document last known well, protect the airway and weak side, and immediately address swallowing, falls, skin pressure, and venous-thromboembolism risk.
