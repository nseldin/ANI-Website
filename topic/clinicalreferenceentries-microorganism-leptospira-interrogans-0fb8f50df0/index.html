Definition

Leptospira interrogans is a clinically important bacterium associated with leptospirosis, aseptic meningitis, Weil disease, and pulmonary hemorrhagic syndrome. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Leptospira interrogans is a Gram-reaction indeterminate spiral with a obligate aerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, motile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Avoid contaminated water, control rodents, cover skin breaks, and use occupational protective measures around animal urine or floodwater. The reviewed route description is the reviewed zoonotic transmission and waterborne transmission route categories. The reviewed incubation estimate is 2 to 30 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Motile spirochetes enter through abraded skin or mucosa after animal-urine exposure, disseminate hematogenously, and injure endothelium, liver, kidney, and lung. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Leptospira interrogans is associated with leptospirosis, aseptic meningitis, Weil disease, and pulmonary hemorrhagic syndrome. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
