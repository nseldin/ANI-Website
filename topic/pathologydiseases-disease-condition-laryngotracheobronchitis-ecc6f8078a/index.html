Definition

Laryngotracheobronchitis develops when viral inflammation narrows the subglottic airway in a young child, making inspiratory resistance rise sharply. The characteristic pattern is barking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor, and symptoms worse at night, with retractions or stridor at rest indicating greater severity. Confirm or stage it with clinical severity assessment based on stridor at rest, retractions, air entry, color, hydration, and alertness; distinguish it from epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body, anaphylaxis, retropharyngeal abscess, and spasmodic croup by drooling, toxicity, onset, voice, and airway findings. The priority is to minimize agitation and give corticosteroid therapy because complete obstruction can complicate Laryngotracheobronchitis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • clinical severity assessment based on stridor at rest, retractions, air entry, color, hydration, and alertness

  • pulse oximetry for significant distress

  • imaging only when an atypical diagnosis is suspected and the airway is stable

  • Distinguish Laryngotracheobronchitis from epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body, anaphylaxis, retropharyngeal abscess, and spasmodic croup by drooling, toxicity, onset, voice, and airway findings.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • minimize agitation and give corticosteroid therapy

  • give nebulized epinephrine for moderate or severe obstruction with observation for recurrence

  • prepare expert airway support for exhaustion, cyanosis, or poor air entry

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for cyanosis, exhaustion, reduced air entry, altered consciousness, drooling, tripod posture, toxic appearance, or poor response because respiratory failure, epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, or foreign body requires emergency airway care.

  • Assess barky cough, stridor at rest, retractions, air entry, color, drooling, voice, fever, age, onset, and foreign-body risk because severity and dangerous alternative diagnoses determine airway urgency.

  • Keep the child calm with a caregiver and minimize upsetting examinations because crying increases turbulent airflow and can markedly worsen dynamic upper-airway obstruction.

  • Give prescribed dexamethasone and nebulized epinephrine for indicated severity while providing oxygen without forced separation because steroids reduce edema and epinephrine rapidly but temporarily narrows swollen mucosa.

  • Monitor work of breathing, stridor, fatigue, oxygenation, hydration, response, and recurrence through the observation period because symptoms can rebound after epinephrine wears off.

Complications

  • complete obstruction can complicate Laryngotracheobronchitis.

  • hypoxic arrest can complicate Laryngotracheobronchitis.

  • dehydration can complicate Laryngotracheobronchitis.

  • secondary bacterial tracheitis can complicate Laryngotracheobronchitis.

Red Flags

  • Cyanosis, apnea, exhaustion, reduced consciousness, or markedly diminished air entry

  • Stridor at rest with severe retractions or worsening despite nebulized epinephrine

  • Drooling, tripod position, muffled voice, inability to swallow, or toxic appearance

  • Sudden choking onset, asymmetric breath sounds, high fever, or suspected foreign body

Patient Education

  • Keep the child calm and seek emergency care for stridor at rest, blue color, drooling, or exhaustion because agitation can worsen obstruction.

  • Do not use steam burns, sedating cough medicines, or forced throat inspection because these do not treat swelling and may delay safe airway care.

Nclex Traps

  • Stridor at rest is more concerning than stridor only while crying

  • Do not force throat examination in a toxic drooling child with possible epiglottitis
