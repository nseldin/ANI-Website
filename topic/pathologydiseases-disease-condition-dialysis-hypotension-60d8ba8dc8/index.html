Definition

Dialysis hypotension is a clinically important blood-pressure fall during or immediately after dialysis that produces symptoms, requires intervention, or prevents safe delivery of treatment. It most often occurs when ultrafiltration exceeds plasma refill and cardiovascular compensation, but infection, bleeding, arrhythmia, ischemia, dialyzer reaction, or other emergencies can look similar.

Pathology

Rapid plasma-water removal lowers venous return. When interstitial refill, vasoconstriction, heart-rate response, and contractile reserve cannot compensate, cardiac output and organ perfusion fall.

Pathophysiology

Reduced preload lowers stroke volume; diabetic autonomic neuropathy or cardiac disease can blunt the compensatory response. Repeated episodes create myocardial stunning and can reduce cerebral, gut, residual-kidney, and access perfusion. The resulting cramps, nausea, and fatigue often shorten sessions, which leaves more salt, water, and uremic solute for the next treatment and creates a self-reinforcing cycle.

Etiology

Contributors include high ultrafiltration demand, an inaccurate low target weight, short treatment time, impaired cardiac output, autonomic dysfunction, vasodilating medicines, food-related splanchnic pooling, warm dialysate, low albumin, sepsis, anemia, and poor vascular tone.

Risk Factors

  • Large interdialytic gain, high ultrafiltration requirement, or shortened treatment

  • Diabetes with autonomic neuropathy, heart failure, ischemia, valvular disease, or arrhythmia

  • Low albumin, frailty, infection, anemia, or vasodilating medications

  • Inaccurate target weight or eating during treatment in a susceptible patient

Signs Symptoms

  • Dizziness, yawning, nausea, cramps, sweating, blurred vision, weakness, or syncope

  • Abrupt pressure fall or inability to obtain a pressure

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, arrhythmia, abdominal pain, or neurologic change when organ ischemia occurs

  • Slow post-dialysis recovery and falls

Diagnostics

  • Confirm pressure manually when needed and assess mentation, pulses, rhythm, oxygenation, bleeding, access, temperature, and ultrafiltration history

  • Compare pre-treatment volume findings and target-weight trend

  • Evaluate sepsis, hemolysis, air, reaction, arrhythmia, ischemia, tamponade, and bleeding when the pattern is atypical

  • Review treatment time, meals, dialysate, and medication timing

Labs

  • Glucose, hemoglobin, electrolytes, ECG, and infection studies are selected by the presentation

  • Albumin affects plasma refill but does not prove the cause

  • Troponin interpretation requires kidney and trend context

  • Delivered treatment can fall when sessions are stopped early

Treatments

  • Reduce or stop ultrafiltration, position and support circulation, and give protocol-directed fluid when appropriate

  • Treat bleeding, infection, arrhythmia, ischemia, or reaction immediately when suspected

  • Reassess target weight, treatment time/frequency, sodium and fluid burden, dialysate conditions, and medication timing

  • Use individualized preventive strategies rather than tolerating recurrent symptomatic episodes

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess predialysis weight, blood pressure, target weight, recent intake, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, bleeding, cardiac function, medications, and prior episodes because dialysis hypotension has multiple interacting causes.

  • Trend blood pressure, heart rate, rhythm, symptoms, mental status, ultrafiltration rate, and treatment progress because falling intravascular volume may exceed vascular and cardiac compensatory capacity.

  • Position the patient supine with legs elevated when appropriate, reduce or stop ultrafiltration, and give protocol saline because restoring effective circulating volume usually reverses symptomatic intradialytic hypotension.

  • Coordinate reassessment of target weight, interdialytic gain, treatment duration, dialysate settings, meal timing, and antihypertensive timing because recurrent episodes require prescription-level prevention rather than repeated rescue alone.

  • Escalate for persistent systolic pressure below 90 mmHg with symptoms, chest pain, sustained dysrhythmia, syncope, focal neurologic change, or shock because myocardial or cerebral ischemia and occult sepsis or bleeding may be present.

Complications

  • Myocardial stunning, infarction, arrhythmia, and heart failure

  • Cerebral or mesenteric ischemia, falls, and injury

  • Access thrombosis and loss of residual kidney function

  • Shortened treatments, underdialysis, recurrent overload, and hospitalization

Contraindications

  • Do not label every pressure fall as routine ultrafiltration intolerance

  • Do not keep removing fluid solely to reach target weight during organ hypoperfusion

  • Avoid reflex large-volume replacement without reassessing congestion and cause

Red Flags

  • Persistent symptomatic systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg despite initial measures

  • Chest pain, ischemic ECG change, sustained dysrhythmia, or severe dyspnea

  • Syncope, seizure, focal neurologic deficit, or prolonged altered mental status

  • Fever, bleeding, refractory shock, or new severe abdominal or back pain

Patient Education

  • Explain that dialysis removes fluid from blood faster than tissue can sometimes refill it, especially with large gains, short treatments, illness, meals, or limited cardiac reserve.

  • Teach patients to follow individualized sodium and fluid goals, report cramps or dizziness promptly, and confirm medication and meal timing with the dialysis team rather than changing them independently.

Nclex Traps

  • Place safety and perfusion before the target weight number

  • Recurrent hypotension is not benign

  • A dialysis patient can be overloaded overall and underfilled during rapid ultrafiltration

  • Always consider emergencies beyond fluid removal
