Definition

Stupor is a severe reduction in consciousness in which ordinary voice and touch do not wake the person; only vigorous or repeated stimulation produces a brief response.

Plain Language

The person is almost unresponsive, wakes only briefly with strong stimulation, and quickly becomes unresponsive again.

Why It Matters

New stupor is a medical emergency because it can signal major brain injury, seizure, severe infection, poisoning, respiratory failure, shock, or a dangerous metabolic problem. Protect the airway, support breathing and circulation, check glucose, and obtain emergency evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • severe dysfunction of the arousal system or both cerebral hemispheres from structural brain injury, seizure, infection, toxic or medication effect, metabolic failure, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or shock

Assessment Method

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, bedside glucose, vital signs, pupils, motor response, and focal asymmetry while documenting the least safe stimulus needed to produce any eye opening, sound, localization, withdrawal, or command following.

Positive Finding

The patient is unresponsive to ordinary voice and touch and can be aroused only briefly by vigorous or repeated stimulation, then quickly returns to an unresponsive sleep-like state.

Mechanism

Marked disruption of brainstem arousal pathways, diffuse hemispheric function, or their physiologic support permits only brief responses to strong stimulation.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Stupor finding raises concern for severe dysfunction of the arousal system or both cerebral hemispheres from structural brain injury, seizure, infection, toxic or medication effect, metabolic failure, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or shock because marked disruption of brainstem arousal pathways, diffuse hemispheric function, or their physiologic support permits only brief responses to strong stimulation. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Stupor does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Stupor as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Sedation, paralysis, severe hearing loss, language barriers, facial or limb injury, and the type of stimulus can alter the visible response; absent speech alone does not prove stupor. Therefore, Stupor cannot confirm or exclude severe dysfunction of the arousal system or both cerebral hemispheres from structural brain injury, seizure, infection, toxic or medication effect, metabolic failure, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or shock by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as coma, deep sedation, locked-in syndrome, severe aphasia, postictal state.

False Negative Situations

Stupor may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of severe dysfunction of the arousal system or both cerebral hemispheres from structural brain injury, seizure, infection, toxic or medication effect, metabolic failure, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or shock.

Modern Status

Current neurologic emergency descriptor between lesser reductions in alertness and coma; document the actual response because terminology is often used inconsistently.
