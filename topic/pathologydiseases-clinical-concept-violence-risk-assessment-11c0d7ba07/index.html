Plain Language

Violence risk assessment is a structured, repeated safety check of how likely a person may be to harm someone in the present context and what can reduce that risk. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis and cannot predict violence with certainty.

Why It Matters

Early recognition of escalating risk can prevent injury while avoiding unnecessary coercion, stigma, or restrictive care. Nurses must also identify delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, trauma, or another reversible cause that requires immediate treatment.

Definition

Violence risk assessment is a structured and repeatedly updated evaluation of the possibility that a person may harm another person in a particular setting and time period. It combines current behavior, threats, intent, access to means, prior events, acute medical or psychiatric contributors, triggers, protective factors, and feasible prevention measures. It is a clinical safety concept rather than a mental disorder, cannot determine future behavior with certainty, and must not be converted into a diagnosis or legal conclusion by a score alone.

Pathology

Violence risk assessment has no disease pathology because it is not a diagnosis. The assessed risk arises dynamically from interactions among recent behavior and history, specific threats or plans, access to possible weapons, acute medical or psychiatric symptoms, substance exposure or withdrawal, environmental stressors, available supports, and protective factors. A diagnosis, demographic characteristic, culture, ethnicity, religion, or appearance must never substitute for an individualized assessment.

Pathophysiology

  • There is no single biological mechanism that defines violence risk. Acute risk may rise through different pathways, including delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, intoxication, withdrawal, severe pain, mania, psychosis, fear, trauma-related arousal, medication toxicity, akathisia, neurologic illness, or escalating situational conflict.

  • Risk changes when symptoms, access to means, interpersonal context, setting, treatment, or protective supports change, so a prior conclusion or calm interval cannot establish current safety.

  • Mental illness alone does not establish imminent violence, and most people with mental illness are not violent. Assessment must focus on the person's current, case-specific evidence.

Etiology

This concept has no single etiology. Clinically important risk may reflect a combination of prior violence, recent escalation, a specific target or plan, immediate access to means, intoxication or withdrawal, delirium or neurologic disease, severe agitation, mania, psychosis, acute interpersonal conflict, treatment disruption, trauma-related fear, pain, communication failure, environmental stress, and limited protective support. These factors guide assessment but do not independently prove that violence will occur.

Risk Factors

  • Prior violence or aggression, especially when the circumstances resemble the current situation

  • A recent specific threat, identified target, expressed intent, preparatory behavior, or immediate access to a weapon or other means

  • Rapidly escalating agitation, fixation on a grievance or target, severe impulsivity, or inability to engage in de-escalation

  • Acute intoxication, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, stimulant effects, delirium, neurologic illness, severe pain, mania, psychosis, fear, or trauma-related arousal

  • Environmental conflict, treatment disruption, abrupt loss of support, or absence of a feasible safety plan

  • Risk factors must be balanced with protective factors such as engagement, help-seeking, willingness to separate from means or targets, reliable support, treatment response, and a workable follow-up plan

Signs Symptoms

  • Pacing, clenched posture, escalating voice, repeated threats, weapon-seeking behavior, fixation on a target, or rapidly worsening behavioral control

  • A sudden change in behavior, attention, orientation, perception, or motor activity that may indicate delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, head injury, or medication toxicity

  • Statements describing a target, plan, intent, access to means, rehearsal, or inability or unwillingness to maintain safety

  • No behavior or statement independently proves that violence will occur; findings must be interpreted in context and reassessed over time

Diagnostics

  • Violence risk assessment is not a diagnostic test and does not establish a psychiatric disorder or legal dangerousness. Document current behavior, threats, target, intent, means, preparatory actions, prior episodes, triggers, protective factors, and the patient's own explanation.

  • Obtain collateral information when safe, lawful, and clinically necessary, particularly during an emergency or when delirium, intoxication, cognitive impairment, or severe symptoms make the history unreliable.

  • A locally approved structured tool such as the Broset Violence Checklist or DASA-IV may support monitoring and care planning in an appropriate inpatient psychiatric setting, but it does not replace clinical assessment.

  • Do not use one score or cutoff as a universal trigger for medication, restraint, seclusion, admission, discharge, transfer, or law-enforcement involvement.

  • Reassess after any change in behavior, setting, medical condition, substance effect, access to means, treatment response, or protective support.

Labs

  • No laboratory test, imaging study, or biomarker diagnoses violence risk or predicts violence with certainty.

  • Check glucose, oxygenation, toxicology, electrolytes, infection studies, medication levels, neuroimaging, ECG, or other tests only when the history and examination support a medical, neurologic, substance-related, or treatment differential.

  • A positive toxicology result, abnormal laboratory result, or psychiatric diagnosis may identify a contributor but does not independently establish imminent violence.

Assessment

Maintain immediate safety while asking directly and respectfully about recent threats, a specific target, intent, plan, access to weapons or other means, preparatory behavior, prior violence, triggers, substance exposure or withdrawal, medications, pain, sleep, psychotic or manic symptoms, trauma-related fear, and willingness to accept help. Assess orientation, attention, glucose or oxygenation when indicated, neurologic findings, injury, suicidality, vulnerability to victimization, and protective factors. Separate direct observation, patient report, and collateral report in the record. Formulate why risk may be higher or lower now, what situations could change it, and which practical interventions may reduce harm.

Differential

  • Delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, infection, head injury, seizure, dementia, or another acute neurologic or metabolic illness

  • Alcohol or sedative withdrawal, stimulant intoxication, other intoxication, medication toxicity, akathisia, or adverse drug effects

  • Mania, psychosis, severe depression, trauma response, panic, severe pain, communication disability, fear, or situational conflict

  • Suicide risk, vulnerability to victimization, domestic or family violence, safeguarding concerns, and homicidal ideation, which may overlap but require their own assessments

  • Culturally or developmentally shaped communication that may appear unusual but does not establish violent intent

Treatments

  • Use calm, respectful, nonconfrontational communication; identify one primary communicator; reduce stimulation; offer realistic choices; address basic needs; and set clear, behavior-based limits.

  • Move to a safer and quieter area when feasible without isolating staff, blocking exits, or increasing the patient's fear.

  • Identify previously effective calming strategies and create an individualized prevention plan using known triggers, patient preferences, protective supports, and early warning signs.

  • Treat the underlying delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, withdrawal, intoxication, pain, medication reaction, mania, psychosis, or other identified condition.

  • No medication treats a violence-risk estimate. If a separate acute-agitation emergency protocol becomes necessary, representative medicines such as lorazepam or haloperidol may be used only according to the cause, patient factors, setting-specific protocol, and required monitoring; they must not be automatically triggered by a risk score.

  • Use restrictive interventions only when preventive and de-escalation measures have failed or cannot be used and there is an immediate potential for harm. Choose the least restrictive proportionate intervention for the shortest necessary time and never use it as punishment.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose violence risk from a structured tool, a psychiatric diagnosis, demographic characteristics, appearance, culture, ethnicity, religion, housing status, or substance-use history.

  • Do not treat a remote history of violence as proof of imminent risk or promise that a low score or calm interval guarantees safety.

  • Do not use medication, restraint, seclusion, observation, involuntary care, or police involvement solely because of a numerical score.

  • Do not use a restrictive intervention for convenience, retaliation, treatment refusal, verbal hostility alone, or punishment.

  • Do not overlook delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, injury, intoxication, withdrawal, pain, medication toxicity, or another reversible cause.

Safety Considerations

  • Maintain access to an exit, adequate trained staff, a clear team plan, and removal of immediate hazards while avoiding provocative crowding or unnecessary confrontation.

  • If an imminent threat, weapon, active assault, or loss of safe containment is present, activate the locally approved emergency response and communicate the known target, means, medical concerns, and safest approach.

  • During restrictive intervention or emergency medication use, protect airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness, positioning, circulation to restrained limbs, hydration, dignity, and ability to communicate; use the observation frequency required by law, policy, intervention, and patient condition.

  • After stabilization, reassess medical and psychiatric contributors, review whether restrictions remain necessary, and debrief with the patient and team.

  • Confidentiality, warning or protection duties, reporting, involuntary treatment, and law-enforcement procedures vary by jurisdiction and require current law and institutional policy.

Nursing Priorities

  • Perform immediate environmental and personal-safety checks, maintain a safe exit route, remove accessible hazards when possible, and obtain appropriate team support.

  • Use trauma-informed de-escalation with one lead communicator, respectful personal space, simple statements, active listening, and choices that the team can actually provide.

  • Document exact patient statements, observed behavior, named targets, expressed intent, access to means, protective factors, information sources, interventions, and the patient's response rather than using global labels such as dangerous or violent.

  • Assess and escalate reversible medical causes, including altered attention, abnormal glucose or oxygenation, fever, focal neurologic findings, injury, severe pain, intoxication, and withdrawal.

  • Monitor airway, respiratory rate and effort, oxygenation, blood pressure, pulse, consciousness, mobility, falls, aspiration risk, and medication-specific effects after emergency sedation.

  • Reassess risk and the need for observation or restriction after every meaningful clinical or environmental change and communicate the updated formulation during handoff.

Red Flags

  • Active assault, possession of a weapon, immediate access to a weapon, or a credible specific threat with target, intent, and means

  • Rapid escalation that cannot be safely de-escalated or inability to maintain safety in the current setting

  • Delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, seizure, head injury, focal neurologic findings, severe intoxication, or potentially life-threatening withdrawal

  • Respiratory depression, loss of consciousness, severe hypotension, aspiration, rigidity, hyperthermia, or another serious adverse effect after emergency medication or restriction

  • A proposed restraint, seclusion, involuntary intervention, or discharge based only on diagnosis, stigma, prior history, or a score rather than current evidence

Complications

  • Injury or death involving the patient, another patient, visitors, staff, or community members

  • Trauma, humiliation, discriminatory treatment, damaged therapeutic trust, and future avoidance of care

  • Aspiration, hypoxia, circulatory compromise, falls, medication toxicity, or cardiopulmonary arrest during an emergency intervention

  • Missed delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, head injury, infection, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, pain, or neurologic disease

  • Unnecessary coercion, prolonged restriction, legal harm, or stigmatizing documentation when uncertainty is converted into certainty

Prognosis

Violence risk is dynamic and context-dependent rather than a permanent trait. Risk may decrease when acute illness, intoxication, withdrawal, fear, pain, conflict, or access to means is addressed and protective supports become workable, but a calm interval does not guarantee future safety. A documented formulation, individualized prevention plan, consistent handoff, and timely reassessment can reduce harm while preserving dignity.

Prevention

  • Identify triggers, early warning signs, preferred calming approaches, trauma-related needs, communication supports, and effective prior interventions before a crisis when possible.

  • Treat pain, delirium, withdrawal, intoxication, sleep disruption, and unmet medical or basic needs promptly.

  • Use consistent team communication, safe environments, individualized care plans, access-to-means review, and reliable follow-up or crisis contacts.

  • Review each event with the patient and team to identify preventable contributors and update the plan without assigning blame.

Patient Education

  • Explain that violence risk assessment is a repeated safety conversation, not a psychiatric diagnosis or a prediction that the person will definitely become violent.

  • Invite the patient to describe triggers, fears, goals, warning signs, helpful staff approaches, calming strategies, and people who can provide support.

  • Explain observation, environmental limits, emergency medication, or restrictive measures in plain language, including what must improve before the restriction can end.

  • Discuss the limits of confidentiality when a serious and credible threat requires protective action under applicable law and policy.

  • Provide an accessible written crisis and prevention plan, follow-up instructions, and clear directions for obtaining urgent help.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require developmentally appropriate assessment, caregiver and safeguarding context, school or peer information when relevant, and interventions that account for size and developmental vulnerability.

  • Older adults with sudden behavioral change need urgent assessment for delirium, infection, medication effects, pain, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, sensory loss, or neurocognitive illness.

  • People with autism, intellectual disability, dementia, traumatic brain injury, language differences, hearing or vision loss, trauma histories, or other communication needs require interpreters, communication aids, lower-stimulation approaches, and individualized baseline comparison.

  • Pregnancy requires obstetric-aware positioning, medication review, and protection from hypoxia, hypotension, trauma, and prolonged restraint.

  • Cultural and spiritual context must be explored respectfully; unfamiliar beliefs, communication, ethnicity, religion, or identity must never be treated as proof of violence risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Violence risk assessment is a clinical safety concept, not a psychiatric diagnosis.

  • A structured tool supports monitoring and care planning but does not predict violence with certainty or replace clinical assessment.

  • No universal score independently determines restraint, seclusion, medication, admission, discharge, or transfer.

  • De-escalation and treatment of reversible causes come before restrictive intervention when the situation permits.

  • Restrictive interventions are never punishment and require continuous safety attention plus locally mandated monitoring.
