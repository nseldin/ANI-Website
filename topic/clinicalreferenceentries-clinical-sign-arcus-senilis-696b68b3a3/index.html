Definition

Arcus senilis is a gray-white, blue-white, or yellow-white ring of lipid deposited near the outer edge of the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. A small clear gap usually separates the ring from the limbus, where the cornea meets the white of the eye.

Plain Language

Arcus senilis is a pale ring of fatty material around the outside of the clear cornea.

Why It Matters

A typical ring in both eyes of an older adult is usually harmless and does not reduce vision. When it appears at a younger age or only on one side, it can justify evaluation for a lipid disorder or vascular disease rather than being dismissed as normal aging.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

Inspect both corneas in neutral light from the front and side and confirm uncertain findings with slit-lamp examination; document age at onset, bilateral or unilateral distribution, completeness, symptoms, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, smoking and vascular risks, and prior lipid results.

Positive Finding

A narrow gray-white, blue-white, or yellow-white opaque band appears in the peripheral cornea, often beginning superiorly and inferiorly before becoming circumferential, with a clear interval between the band and limbus.

Mechanism

Cholesterol, phospholipids, triglycerides, and lipoproteins deposit extracellularly in the peripheral corneal stroma after delivery from the more vascular limbal region.

Evidence Limitations

Typical bilateral arcus in an older adult does not impair vision and does not by itself diagnose dyslipidemia or predict cardiovascular disease; age, population, laterality, and systemic risk alter its meaning, and atypical opacities require eye examination.
