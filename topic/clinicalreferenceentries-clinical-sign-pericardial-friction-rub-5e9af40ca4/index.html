Definition

Pericardial friction rub is a scratchy or grating sound synchronized with the heartbeat that occurs when the pericardium, the sac around the heart, is inflamed. It is an auscultatory sign, not a measurement of fluid around the heart.

Plain Language

It sounds like a scratch, scrape, or creak over the heart because the tissue around the heart is inflamed.

Why It Matters

A true pericardial rub strongly supports pericarditis, but the sound may come and go and can be absent even when pericarditis is present. Chest pain still requires evaluation for heart attack and other emergencies, while low blood pressure, neck-vein distention, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or shock can signal cardiac tamponade.

Associated Conditions

  • acute or recurrent pericardial inflammation from infection, post-myocardial-infarction or post-cardiac-injury inflammation, autoimmune disease, uremia, malignancy, trauma, surgery, radiation, medication, or another pericardial process

Assessment Method

Auscultate repeatedly with the diaphragm along the left lower sternal border and adjacent precordium while the patient is positioned safely, including sitting and leaning forward when tolerated; document one, two, or three components and their cardiac-cycle timing, location, intensity, change with position and breathing, persistence during a comfortable brief breath hold, chest-pain features, temperature, vital signs, ECG findings, and signs of effusion or tamponade.

Positive Finding

A superficial scratchy, grating, rasping, or creaking sound with one to three components is synchronized with the cardiac cycle and may become clearer while leaning forward or during expiration.

Mechanism

Inflammation and fibrinous change involving pericardial surfaces generate cardiac-cycle vibrations; the exact sound mechanism is not fully explained and a rub can occur even when pericardial fluid separates the layers.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Pericardial friction rub response raises concern for acute or recurrent pericardial inflammation from infection, post-myocardial-infarction or post-cardiac-injury inflammation, autoimmune disease, uremia, malignancy, trauma, surgery, radiation, medication, or another pericardial process because inflammation and fibrinous change involving pericardial surfaces generate cardiac-cycle vibrations; the exact sound mechanism is not fully explained and a rub can occur even when pericardial fluid separates the layers. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Pericardial friction rub does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Pericardial friction rub to focus evaluation of acute or recurrent pericardial inflammation from infection, post-myocardial-infarction or post-cardiac-injury inflammation, autoimmune disease, uremia, malignancy, trauma, surgery, radiation, medication, or another pericardial process and the reviewed alternatives: pleural friction rub, heart murmur, stethoscope or clothing artifact, prosthetic valve sound, coarse crackles. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Pericardial friction rub as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

The rub can appear and disappear over hours, may have only one component, and can be difficult to hear with tachycardia, obesity, shallow breathing, effusion, or noise; its absence does not exclude pericarditis and its intensity does not measure effusion severity. Therefore, Pericardial friction rub cannot confirm or exclude acute or recurrent pericardial inflammation from infection, post-myocardial-infarction or post-cardiac-injury inflammation, autoimmune disease, uremia, malignancy, trauma, surgery, radiation, medication, or another pericardial process by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • pleural friction rub

  • heart murmur

  • stethoscope or clothing artifact

  • prosthetic valve sound

  • coarse crackles

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from pleural friction rub, heart murmur, stethoscope or clothing artifact, prosthetic valve sound, coarse crackles, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Pericardial friction rub may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current highly meaningful clue to acute pericarditis when correctly identified, but it is intermittent and insensitive; its presence does not determine the cause, effusion size, or tamponade physiology.
