Definition

Wheezing is an auscultatory respiratory sign. It is a clue to airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction. A positive finding means a continuous musical or whistling sound is heard, most often louder during expiration; it may be diffuse or localized.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Wheezing means a continuous musical or whistling sound is heard, most often louder during expiration; it may be diffuse or localized.

Why It Matters

A positive Wheezing response can signal airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction. Air forced through a narrowed airway makes airway walls and the air column oscillate, producing a sustained musical sound. Wheezing cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

  • airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction

Assessment Method

Auscultate all lung fields during quiet and deeper breathing, compare sides, note inspiratory versus expiratory timing and focal versus diffuse distribution, and correlate with work of breathing and air movement.

Positive Finding

A continuous musical or whistling sound is heard, most often louder during expiration; it may be diffuse or localized.

Mechanism

Air forced through a narrowed airway makes airway walls and the air column oscillate, producing a sustained musical sound.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Wheezing response raises concern for airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction because air forced through a narrowed airway makes airway walls and the air column oscillate, producing a sustained musical sound. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Wheezing does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Wheezing to focus evaluation of airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction and the reviewed alternatives: stridor, rhonchi, cardiac asthma, foreign-body obstruction. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Auscultate all lung fields during quiet and deeper breathing, compare sides, note inspiratory versus expiratory timing and focal versus diffuse distribution, and correlate with work of breathing and air movement. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Wheezing as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Wheezing is not specific for asthma and may disappear when airflow becomes critically low; upper-airway sounds, rhonchi, and transmitted noise can mimic it. Therefore, Wheezing cannot confirm or exclude airflow through narrowed intrathoracic airways from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, secretions, dynamic collapse, or focal obstruction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • stridor

  • rhonchi

  • cardiac asthma

  • foreign-body obstruction

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from stridor, rhonchi, cardiac asthma, foreign-body obstruction, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Wheezing may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current high-yield sign of airway narrowing; severity depends on airflow, so a quiet or silent chest in a struggling patient can be more dangerous than loud wheezing. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Wheezing, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Auscultate all lung fields during quiet and deeper breathing, compare sides, note inspiratory versus expiratory timing and focal versus diffuse distribution, and correlate with work of breathing and air movement. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Escalate immediately for severe work of breathing, exhaustion, cyanosis, altered mental status, poor air movement, hypotension, or suspected anaphylaxis; do not interpret reduced wheezing as improvement without confirming better ventilation.
