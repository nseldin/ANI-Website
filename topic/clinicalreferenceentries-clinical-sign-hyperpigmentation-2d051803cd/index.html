Definition

Hyperpigmentation means an area of skin or mucosa has become darker than the person's own baseline. It can result from extra melanin, inflammation, a medicine or deposit, hormonal signaling, or systemic disease.

Plain Language

Part of the skin or a moist lining such as the mouth has become darker than it used to be.

Why It Matters

Localized darkening is often postinflammatory or medication related, but new diffuse darkening of scars, creases, pressure points, lips, or oral mucosa with weakness, weight loss, vomiting, dizziness, syncope, or hypotension can signal primary adrenal insufficiency. That combination can precede adrenal crisis and requires prompt evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • increased melanin after inflammation, ultraviolet exposure, hormonal stimulation, genetic variation, medication or metal deposition, endocrine disease including primary adrenal insufficiency, or systemic metabolic disease

Assessment Method

Compare with the patient's baseline and prior images in neutral light; document location, symmetry, borders, texture, onset, progression, preceding inflammation, sun and chemical exposure, medications, scars, palmar creases, pressure points, nails, hair, and oral mucosa, then assess weight, blood pressure, gastrointestinal, and endocrine symptoms.

Positive Finding

One or more skin or mucosal areas are darker than the patient's baseline, either locally or diffusely; the surface may be otherwise normal or have a separate lesion texture.

Mechanism

Melanocytes may make or transfer more melanin, inflammation may leave pigment behind, or medicines, metals, blood products, and metabolic substances may deposit in tissue; primary adrenal insufficiency increases melanocortin stimulation.

Evidence Limitations

Baseline pigmentation, tanning, lighting, dryness, friction, and camera processing affect recognition; color alone cannot distinguish benign, inflammatory, endocrine, medication-related, or malignant causes. Therefore, Hyperpigmentation cannot confirm or exclude increased melanin after inflammation, ultraviolet exposure, hormonal stimulation, genetic variation, medication or metal deposition, endocrine disease including primary adrenal insufficiency, or systemic metabolic disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

  • normal pigmentation

  • postinflammatory change

  • medication-related pigmentation

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as normal pigmentation, melasma, postinflammatory change, medication-related pigmentation.

False Negative Situations

Hyperpigmentation may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased melanin after inflammation, ultraviolet exposure, hormonal stimulation, genetic variation, medication or metal deposition, endocrine disease including primary adrenal insufficiency, or systemic metabolic disease.
