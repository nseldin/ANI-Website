Name

PT/INR

Range

Use the reporting laboratory's PT interval and the patient's prescribed INR goal. Mayo Clinic Laboratories reports PT 9.4-12.5 seconds and INR 0.9-1.1 for one method, with common warfarin ranges of 2.0-3.0 or 2.5-3.5 in selected indications; these are examples, not universal targets. Mechanical-valve goals vary with valve type, characteristics, and thromboembolic risk. Use the current order, anticoagulation protocol, indication, trend, and laboratory method; there is no universal critical PT or INR cutoff.

Why

PT measures how long citrated plasma takes to clot through the tissue-factor and common coagulation pathways; INR standardizes PT mainly for vitamin K antagonist therapy such as warfarin. A prolonged PT/INR result signals slower clot formation and raises concern for warfarin effect, vitamin K deficiency, impaired liver synthesis, DIC or other factor consumption, massive transfusion or dilution, a factor deficiency, or an inhibitor. In a patient prescribed warfarin, an INR below the ordered target signals less anticoagulant effect and possible thromboembolic risk, while a short PT or low INR outside that context is usually not diagnostic of hypercoagulability. Assess active bleeding, bruising, stool or urine blood, new headache or neurologic change, recent fall or head injury, vital signs, medication and dose timing, adherence, diet or illness changes, liver status, CBC and platelets, and the patient's indication and target. An unexpected result may be repeated, and cause-directed evaluation may include a mixing study, aPTT, fibrinogen and D-dimer, liver and vitamin K assessment, or factor and inhibitor testing as ordered. Escalate immediately for major bleeding, hemodynamic instability, new neurologic findings, significant head injury, or a rapidly worsening result with symptoms; follow the prescribed hold or reversal pathway rather than changing therapy from the number alone. PT/INR does not identify the cause of an abnormal result or predict bleeding or clotting by itself.

High Causes

  • Warfarin or another medication effect

  • Vitamin K deficiency or impaired absorption

  • Liver synthetic dysfunction

  • DIC or other factor consumption

  • Massive transfusion or dilution

  • Congenital or acquired factor deficiency

  • Coagulation inhibitor or specimen interference

Low Causes

  • Below-target INR during prescribed warfarin therapy indicates less anticoagulant effect and may increase thromboembolic risk.

  • A short PT or low INR in a person not receiving warfarin is usually not a stand-alone diagnosis of increased clotting.

Critical Concerns

  • Major bleeding, shock, new neurologic change, or head injury with an elevated INR requires urgent escalation and the ordered anticoagulation-reversal pathway.

  • A mechanical-valve INR goal is not interchangeable with every other warfarin indication; verify the exact target and do not infer one from a generic range.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess bleeding and neurologic status, review the indication and ordered target, and verify medication dose and timing, adherence, interactions, recent illness, diet consistency, liver function, CBC, platelets, and result trend.

  • If the result is unexpected, verify specimen and timing and anticipate repeat testing or cause-directed coagulation studies as ordered.

  • Follow facility anticoagulation hold, notification, and reversal protocols; do not independently choose a target, hold a dose, or administer reversal from one result.

Nclex Traps

  • INR is primarily the monitoring anchor for warfarin, not a universal measure of every anticoagulant.

  • A high INR raises bleeding concern but does not prove active bleeding; a below-target INR during warfarin therapy raises clotting concern but does not prove a clot.
