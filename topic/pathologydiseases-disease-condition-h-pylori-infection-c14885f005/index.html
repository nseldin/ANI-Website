Definition

H. pylori infection develops when gastric colonization by Helicobacter pylori drives chronic gastritis, impairs mucosal defenses, and increases ulcer and gastric-cancer risk. The characteristic pattern is epigastric dyspepsia or peptic ulcer, sometimes with iron deficiency, but many infected people are asymptomatic. Confirm or stage it with urea breath test or stool antigen for active noninvasive diagnosis; distinguish it from functional dyspepsia, reflux, medication gastritis, biliary disease, and gastric malignancy by alarm features, testing, and endoscopy. The priority is to use a guideline-selected multidrug eradication regimen based on resistance and prior antibiotic exposure because bleeding or perforated ulcer can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • urea breath test or stool antigen for active noninvasive diagnosis

  • endoscopic biopsy when alarm features or endoscopy indications exist

  • post-treatment test of cure after an appropriate medication-free interval

  • Distinguish H. pylori infection from functional dyspepsia, reflux, medication gastritis, biliary disease, and gastric malignancy by alarm features, testing, and endoscopy.

Labs

  • hemoglobin may fall with bleeding

  • AST/ALT and bilirubin may rise with hepatic/biliary injury

  • lipase rises in pancreatitis

  • albumin and INR reflect liver synthetic function

Treatments

  • use a guideline-selected multidrug eradication regimen based on resistance and prior antibiotic exposure

  • support adherence and adverse-effect management

  • confirm eradication rather than assuming symptom relief equals cure

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for hematemesis, melena with weakness, syncope, hypotension, sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, persistent vomiting, progressive dysphagia, anemia, or weight loss because hemorrhage, perforation, obstruction, or malignancy needs urgent evaluation.

  • Assess dyspepsia, ulcer history, gastrointestinal bleeding, iron deficiency, prior gastric surgery, family gastric-cancer history, antibiotic and bismuth exposure, allergies, pregnancy, and prior eradication attempts because treatment choice and urgency depend on resistance and complication risk.

  • Collect urea breath, stool antigen, biopsy, or other indicated testing under the required proton-pump inhibitor, antibiotic, and bismuth hold conditions because suppressive medicines can produce a false-negative result.

  • Administer the complete prescribed multidrug regimen with a written schedule and monitor nausea, diarrhea, allergy, interactions, adherence, and dark stool or tongue from bismuth because regimen complexity is a major cause of eradication failure.

  • Arrange a test of cure at the guideline-specified interval after treatment and medication holds because symptom relief does not prove eradication and persistent infection continues ulcer and gastric-cancer risk.

Complications

  • bleeding or perforated ulcer

  • gastric adenocarcinoma can complicate H. pylori infection.

  • MALT lymphoma can complicate H. pylori infection.

  • recurrent ulcer after failed eradication

Red Flags

  • Hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, syncope, or hypotension

  • Sudden severe abdominal pain with guarding, rebound, or rigid abdomen

  • Persistent vomiting, early satiety, or inability to maintain hydration

  • Progressive dysphagia, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, or unintentional weight loss

Patient Education

  • Teach the exact dosing calendar and advise the patient to call about adverse effects rather than silently stopping one component because partial therapy promotes failure and resistance.

  • Explain why a post-treatment breath or stool test is still needed when symptoms disappear: ulcer symptoms can settle while the organism remains.

Nclex Traps

  • Serology cannot reliably prove active infection or cure

  • Acid suppression and recent antibiotics can cause false-negative breath or stool tests
