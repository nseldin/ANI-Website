Used To Treat

Short-term treatment of hypertension in adults when oral therapy is not feasible or desirable. Critical-care teams use it in condition-specific acute blood-pressure protocols, then transition to oral therapy as soon as the clinical situation permits.

Mechanism

Nicardipine inhibits Cav1.2 channels in arterial smooth muscle, reducing calcium-calmodulin activation of myosin light-chain kinase and relaxing arterioles. Systemic resistance and afterload fall, while baroreceptor compensation may raise heart rate. It does not therapeutically suppress AV-node conduction like diltiazem or verapamil. With a constant infusion, pressure begins to fall within minutes and approaches about half of its eventual decrease in roughly 45 minutes; after stopping, action falls by about half in approximately 30 minutes. Hepatic metabolism and reduced clearance in liver or renal impairment explain why gradual titration is required even though the drug is given intravenously.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Continuous blood-pressure and heart-rate monitoring is required. Stop the infusion for impending or unacceptable hypotension or tachycardia, then restart only after stabilization at a lower ordered rate. CARDENE IV is contraindicated in advanced aortic stenosis because lowering diastolic pressure and afterload across a fixed obstruction can worsen myocardial oxygen balance. Angina or heart failure can worsen. Use a central or large peripheral vein, avoid small veins and intra-arterial delivery, change peripheral sites every 12 hours, and respond immediately to pain, swelling, blanching, phlebitis, or extravasation.

Contraindications

  • Advanced aortic stenosis and serious hypersensitivity; do not use without a defined monitored pressure target and condition-specific plan.

  • Heart failure, significant LV dysfunction, hepatic impairment, or renal impairment require slower titration and close response monitoring rather than an automatic standard escalation.

Nursing Essentials

  • Use continuous BP/HR monitoring and titrate to the ordered target, not to a generic normal pressure. Reassess neurologic status, urine output, peripheral perfusion, and the disease-specific risk of lowering pressure too quickly.

  • Verify bag concentration because 0.1 and 0.2 mg/mL products convert to different mL/hour rates. Use dedicated compatible administration, a large vein or central line, inspect frequently, and document the 12-hour peripheral-site change.

Interactions

  • Nicardipine can raise cyclosporine and tacrolimus concentrations; monitor trough levels and toxicity. Cimetidine increases oral nicardipine exposure, while IV interaction significance follows the current label.

  • Beta blockers and other antihypertensives can add hypotension or myocardial depression. Avoid unreviewed same-line mixing; the ready-to-use CARDENE IV label says not to combine another product in the same line or bag.

Key Labs

  • Continuous pressure and heart rate, neurologic/perfusion checks, urine output, IV site, renal and hepatic function, and symptoms of ischemia or heart failure.

  • Cyclosporine/tacrolimus troughs when coadministered; monitor electrolytes, creatinine, lactate, and ECG according to the acute condition and degree of pressure change.

Nclex Traps

  • Nicardipine is the IV arterial vasodilator; it is not the oral nifedipine product and not the AV-node drug diltiazem.

  • The 12-hour rule is a peripheral infusion-site change to reduce local injury. It is not the clevidipine 12-hour vial-discard rule.

Description

Nicardipine is a titratable IV dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker that relaxes arterial resistance vessels, lowers systemic vascular resistance and afterload, and begins lowering pressure within minutes without being an AV-node rate-control drug. The current CARDENE IV label starts a patient not receiving oral nicardipine at 5 mg/hour, increases by 2.5 mg/hour every 5 to 15 minutes, and caps the infusion at 15 mg/hour. Because its effect carries over longer than clevidipine, repeated rapid titrations can stack and overshoot the target. It is also locally irritating: use a central line or large peripheral vein, avoid small hand/wrist veins and arterial administration, inspect for extravasation/phlebitis, and change a peripheral site every 12 hours.

Adverse Effects

  • Headache, hypotension, tachycardia, nausea/vomiting, flushing, dizziness, and infusion-site burning or irritation.

  • Severe hypotension, worsening angina, myocardial ischemia, acute heart-failure worsening, phlebitis, venous thrombosis, extravasation injury, and prolonged effect with impaired clearance.
