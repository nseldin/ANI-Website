Class

Carnitine replacement and metabolic adjunct in severe valproate toxicity

Used To Treat

Selected severe valproic-acid or divalproex toxicity with hyperammonemia, hepatotoxicity, encephalopathy, or a large overdose, as an OFF-LABEL adjunct to supportive care and extracorporeal-treatment assessment. It is not a proven stand-alone antidote and should not delay EXTRIP-directed dialysis in a qualifying patient.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): Known hypersensitivity is a contraindication. This does not remove the need to treat the underlying hyperammonemia, liver failure, shock, or cerebral edema by other means. Worsening coma, cerebral edema, shock, rising lactate, severe acidosis, refractory hyperammonemia, liver failure, or rising valproate despite therapy requires immediate ECTR and transplant/critical-care assessment as appropriate.

Mechanism

Carnitine shuttles fatty acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane, allowing beta oxidation and regeneration of free coenzyme A. Valproate and valproyl-CoA consume carnitine, inhibit parts of beta oxidation, and favor omega oxidation, which produces metabolites such as 4-en-valproate that can further impair mitochondria. Valproate-related mitochondrial dysfunction also reduces urea-cycle handling and can raise ammonia even before marked hepatitis appears. Levocarnitine replenishes the carrier pool, supports mitochondrial fatty-acid metabolism, and may reduce accumulation of toxic valproate metabolites, thereby improving ammonia handling. It does not bind valproate, rapidly lower a massive concentration, or directly reverse cerebral edema.

Nursing Essentials

  • Trend neurologic status and airway protection closely because valproate toxicity can progress from somnolence to coma while ammonia and measured valproate do not always move together.

  • Verify the exact valproate product, formulation, dose, ingestion time, coingestants, weight, renal/hepatic history, and levocarnitine concentration and calculation with an independent check because delayed-release products and unit errors can prolong or complicate treatment.

  • Monitor IV access and infusion tolerance, and record ammonia, mental status, and liver-test response with each dose so treatment is guided by a trend rather than by the label 'antidote.'

  • Maintain glucose, temperature, aspiration, seizure, and pressure-injury precautions and coordinate early dialysis-capable critical care when severe findings are present because metabolic support alone may not provide enough clearance.

Contraindications

  • Known hypersensitivity is a contraindication. This does not remove the need to treat the underlying hyperammonemia, liver failure, shock, or cerebral edema by other means.

  • Do not use levocarnitine merely because a patient takes valproate or a supplement seems harmless. Routine deficiency treatment, dialysis-related carnitine deficiency, and acute valproate poisoning are different indications and regimens.

Key Labs

  • Serial valproate concentrations until clearly falling after the expected absorption window; ammonia, AST/ALT, bilirubin, PT/INR, glucose, electrolytes, blood gas, lactate, creatinine, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, CBC, and lipase when pancreatitis is suspected.

  • Interpret total valproate cautiously in hypoalbuminemia, renal failure, critical illness, or very high concentrations because the unbound fraction rises as protein binding saturates. Obtain a free concentration when available without delaying treatment.

Nclex Traps

  • Ammonia can rise with valproate even when transaminases are not dramatically elevated because mitochondrial and urea-cycle effects can precede frank liver injury.

  • Levocarnitine supports metabolism; it does not directly bind or rapidly remove valproate.

  • In severe poisoning, evaluate dialysis while giving adjunctive carnitine rather than waiting to see whether carnitine alone works.

Description

Levocarnitine is the biologically active form of carnitine, a carrier required to move long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for beta oxidation. Valproate can deplete carnitine and impair mitochondrial metabolism, shifting metabolism toward pathways that generate toxic metabolites and interfere with the urea cycle. Replacing carnitine is biologically plausible and widely considered in severe toxicity, especially when ammonia is high or liver injury is developing. However, the 2026 evidence review found that a survival or toxin-clearance advantage remains uncertain, so it is an OFF-LABEL metabolic adjunct rather than a guaranteed lifesaving antidote.

Display Name

Levocarnitine

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and a fishy body odor can occur. Rapid IV delivery can be poorly tolerated, so use the protocol's dilution and rate.

  • Seizures have been reported in patients with or without a prior seizure disorder, and serious hypersensitivity reactions are possible. Distinguish a drug reaction from progression of valproate poisoning.

Administration Timing

  • Call Poison Help or medical toxicology early. Consider protocol-directed IV levocarnitine when severe valproate toxicity includes hyperammonemic encephalopathy, hepatotoxicity, coma, a very large exposure, or other high-risk findings; oral maintenance is not a substitute for acute IV rescue when gut absorption or consciousness is unreliable.

  • Use the poison-center or institutional loading, maintenance, and stopping protocol because published approaches vary. Continue serial clinical and biochemical reassessment rather than stopping solely because one ammonia value improves.

  • Evaluate EXTRIP criteria in parallel. Severe cerebral edema, shock, marked acidosis, extreme concentration, or ventilated coma may require extracorporeal treatment; levocarnitine must not delay dialysis access or transfer.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Worsening coma, cerebral edema, shock, rising lactate, severe acidosis, refractory hyperammonemia, liver failure, or rising valproate despite therapy requires immediate ECTR and transplant/critical-care assessment as appropriate.

  • A delayed or secondary rise can follow enteric-coated or extended-release ingestion. Continue concentrations and observation until absorption has ended and the patient is clinically improving.

Evidence Limitations

  • Valproate-poisoning treatment is OFF-LABEL. The 2026 review supports biological plausibility and consideration in severe hyperammonemia or hepatotoxicity but concludes that high-certainty survival and pharmacokinetic benefit are not established.

  • Dose, route, and stopping criteria vary among poison-center protocols. Levocarnitine cannot be presented as an alternative to ECTR when EXTRIP criteria are met.

Regulatory Status

OFF-LABEL for valproic-acid poisoning. Levocarnitine is FDA approved for specified carnitine-deficiency indications; toxicology use requires specialist protocol and candid discussion of uncertain outcome evidence.
