Definition

Influenza A virus is an enveloped, segmented negative-sense RNA virus that causes seasonal influenza and can infect humans and several animal species. It is one cause of influenza disease; influenza B is a separate virus. The segmented genome permits genetic reassortment, which helps explain why influenza A can produce novel subtypes and pandemics in addition to yearly seasonal epidemics.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    Influenza A viruses are classified by their hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins, producing subtype labels such as H1N1 or H3N2. Those labels are not aliases for every influenza A virus. Antigenic drift reflects gradual mutation and contributes to seasonal vaccine updates, while antigenic shift refers to major reassortment that can create a novel virus to which populations have little immunity.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The viral envelope contains hemagglutinin, which binds respiratory epithelial receptors and promotes entry, and neuraminidase, which helps new virions leave infected cells. Eight RNA segments reside in helical ribonucleoprotein complexes. Because RNA polymerase is error prone and the genome is segmented, the virus evolves through both mutation and reassortment. The lipid envelope also makes it vulnerable to detergents and appropriate disinfection.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Infected people spread seasonal influenza mainly through respiratory particles during close contact; contaminated hands can contribute when virus reaches the eyes, nose, or mouth. Viral shedding can begin before symptoms, so symptom-only control misses some transmission. Animal reservoirs are important to influenza A evolution, but ordinary seasonal human illness is usually maintained through human respiratory spread.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Hemagglutinin-mediated entry begins infection of respiratory epithelial cells. Viral replication kills or impairs those cells and disrupts mucociliary clearance, while innate cytokines produce fever, myalgia, and fatigue. Damaged epithelium and altered host defenses increase vulnerability to primary viral pneumonia and secondary bacterial pneumonia. This is why apparent improvement followed by renewed fever or respiratory decline is a meaningful warning pattern.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Influenza often begins abruptly with fever, cough, headache, myalgia, malaise, and sore throat, but presentations vary by age and immune status. Complications include pneumonia, worsening cardiopulmonary disease, encephalopathy, myositis, and dehydration. Respiratory distress, cyanosis, chest pain, confusion, severe weakness, poor intake, or clinical deterioration after initial improvement requires urgent assessment.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Molecular assays such as RT-PCR are preferred for hospitalized patients and provide greater sensitivity than many rapid antigen tests. A negative rapid antigen result does not rule out influenza when clinical suspicion is high. Test interpretation depends on specimen quality, site, timing, local circulation, and pretest probability. In priority patients, empiric antiviral treatment should not be delayed while waiting for a result.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Annual influenza vaccination reduces illness and severe outcomes even though circulating strains and vaccine composition change. Standard and syndrome-appropriate respiratory precautions, hand hygiene, source control, ventilation, and staying away from others when acutely ill reduce spread. Antiviral benefit is greatest when treatment starts early, but severe or hospitalized illness can still warrant treatment later under current guidance.
