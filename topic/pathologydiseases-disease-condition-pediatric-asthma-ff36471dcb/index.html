Definition

Pediatric asthma develops when episodic airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, mucus, and reversible bronchoconstriction narrow small airways in a developing child. The characteristic pattern is recurrent wheeze, cough, chest tightness, or exercise and nighttime symptoms that vary over time and improve with bronchodilation. Confirm or stage it with age-appropriate spirometry with bronchodilator response when feasible; distinguish it from viral bronchiolitis, foreign body, vocal-cord dysfunction, cystic fibrosis, and congenital airway disease by age, onset, focal findings, and reversibility. The priority is to give a rapid-acting bronchodilator for acute bronchospasm and systemic corticosteroid for significant exacerbation because status asthmaticus with respiratory failure can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • age-appropriate spirometry with bronchodilator response when feasible

  • symptom, trigger, exacerbation, inhaler-technique, and adherence assessment

  • alternative testing or specialist evaluation when diagnosis is uncertain in a young child

  • Distinguish Pediatric asthma from viral bronchiolitis, foreign body, vocal-cord dysfunction, cystic fibrosis, and congenital airway disease by age, onset, focal findings, and reversibility.

Labs

  • do not apply adult ranges without checking age

  • glucose, bilirubin, CBC, and electrolytes often require age-specific interpretation

Treatments

  • give a rapid-acting bronchodilator for acute bronchospasm and systemic corticosteroid for significant exacerbation

  • use an inhaled-corticosteroid-containing controller plan matched to age and severity

  • teach spacer technique, trigger reduction, and a written action plan

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for a silent chest, cyanosis, inability to speak or feed, exhaustion, altered consciousness, or rising carbon dioxide because intubation support may be required.

  • Assess speech or feeding, retractions, respiratory rate, air movement, oxygen saturation, and alertness because children may deteriorate before they can describe severe breathlessness.

  • Administer repeated inhaled bronchodilator and prescribed systemic corticosteroid therapy because bronchospasm and airway inflammation must both be treated.

  • Use a spacer or age-appropriate delivery device and reassess technique because poor delivery can look like medication failure.

  • Monitor heart rate, air entry, work of breathing, oxygen need, peak flow when feasible, and response after each treatment to detect fatigue.

Complications

  • status asthmaticus with respiratory failure

  • growth can complicate Pediatric asthma.

  • oral can complicate Pediatric asthma.

  • or adrenal effects from poorly supervised treatment

  • school absence can complicate Pediatric asthma.

  • sleep disruption can complicate Pediatric asthma.

  • airway remodeling from poor control

Red Flags

  • Silent chest or markedly decreasing air movement

  • Cyanosis or rapidly increasing oxygen requirement

  • Inability to speak, feed, or remain alert

  • Exhaustion or rising carbon dioxide

Patient Education

  • Teach child and caregiver inhaler-spacer technique, controller adherence, trigger reduction, and the written asthma action plan.

  • Explain that frequent rescue use, nighttime symptoms, or activity limitation means the controller plan needs prompt review.

Nclex Traps

  • A child with a quiet chest and fatigue may have less airflow, not improvement

  • Do not rely on symptoms alone when spirometry and exacerbation history show persistent risk
