Summary

The placenta is a temporary fetal organ attached to the maternal uterus that performs exchange, endocrine, immune, and anchoring functions. Maternal blood enters the intervillous space from remodeled uterine spiral arteries and bathes fetal chorionic villi; fetal blood remains inside villous capillaries and returns through the umbilical vein. The circulations come extremely close but normally do not flow through one continuous shared pipe. Exchange depends on surface area, membrane properties, concentration gradients, binding, transporters, and adequate flow on both sides.

Quick Answer

Two umbilical arteries carry fetal blood from the fetus to placental villi, and one umbilical vein returns more oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood. Maternal uterine arteries deliver blood into the intervillous space, and uterine veins drain it. Oxygen, carbon dioxide, water, glucose, amino acids, electrolytes, waste products, medicines, and antibodies cross by different mechanisms; the placenta is selective but not an absolute barrier. It produces hCG, progesterone, estrogens, human placental lactogen, and other signals that maintain pregnancy and alter maternal physiology. Poor implantation, inadequate spiral-artery remodeling, maternal hypotension, placental separation, abnormal location, thrombosis, or maternal disease can reduce exchange. Because uteroplacental flow depends strongly on maternal perfusion, position, oxygenation, circulating volume, and blood pressure directly affect fetal status.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The fetal side includes chorionic plate, branching chorionic villi, fetal capillaries, and umbilical cord; the maternal side interfaces with decidua of the uterine wall. Anchoring villi attach the placenta, while terminal villi provide thin, highly branched exchange surfaces in maternal blood-filled intervillous space. Maternal blood arrives from spiral arteries and leaves through uterine veins. Fetal blood enters through two umbilical arteries, traverses capillary networks, and exits through one umbilical vein. The cord is protected by connective tissue and usually contains these three vessels. Amnion covers the fetal surface. Placental location relative to the cervix and depth of attachment matter clinically.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: exchange without direct mixing

    Text

    Oxygen diffuses from maternal blood with higher effective partial pressure toward fetal blood, aided by fetal hemoglobin's oxygen affinity and continuous fetal flow. Carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction. Glucose uses facilitated transport, amino acids use active transport, and water and electrolytes follow several pathways. Lipids, hormones, antibodies, drugs, pathogens, and waste products cross to varying degrees based on size, charge, protein binding, lipid solubility, transporters, metabolism, and gestational age. Maternal and fetal red cells normally remain separated, yet small fetomaternal transfers can occur, especially with delivery, trauma, bleeding, or procedures. This is why Rh sensitization is possible despite separate circulations.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: endocrine and immune roles

    Text

    Early trophoblast hCG preserves the corpus luteum so progesterone continues until placental production can support the pregnancy. Progesterone helps maintain endometrium and uterine quiescence; estrogens support uterine and breast changes; human placental lactogen and other hormones shift maternal metabolism to improve nutrient availability. Placental enzymes and transporters modify some substances, but protection is incomplete. Maternal IgG crosses and provides temporary neonatal passive immunity, whereas antibody classes differ in transfer. The maternal immune system tolerates genetically distinct fetal tissue through local barrier and regulatory mechanisms without becoming globally inactive. Placental inflammation or immune dysfunction can therefore affect growth, membranes, labor, and maternal disease.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Normal placentation remodels spiral arteries into high-flow, low-resistance channels. Uteroplacental circulation lacks the strong autoregulation found in some vital organs, so maternal hypotension, severe vasoconstriction, hemorrhage, hypoxemia, or supine vena-caval compression can reduce delivery. Maternal cardiac output, hemoglobin, oxygen saturation, glucose, disease, smoking, and medicines influence the supply side; fetal cardiac output and placental vascular resistance shape the fetal side. Contractions transiently reduce uterine flow, which a healthy reserve usually tolerates. Positioning a hypotensive late-pregnancy patient laterally can relieve aortocaval compression, improve venous return, and improve placental perfusion while the underlying cause is treated.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Insufficient invasion or spiral-artery remodeling contributes to high-resistance flow associated with placental insufficiency, fetal growth restriction, and preeclampsia. Placental abruption separates a normally located placenta and interrupts exchange while causing maternal hemorrhage that may be concealed. Placenta previa places placental tissue over or near the cervical opening and classically causes painless bleeding; invasive attachment disorders can cause catastrophic hemorrhage at delivery. Infarction, thrombosis, infection, cord compression, or maternal shock reduce exchange. Excess transfer of glucose in diabetes drives fetal insulin and growth while increasing neonatal metabolic risk. Failure can appear first as altered fetal movement, growth, fluid, Doppler pattern, or fetal-heart response rather than maternal symptoms.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Many medicines cross the placenta, but crossing does not automatically mean harmful and absence of data does not prove safety. Decisions balance maternal benefit, fetal risk, dose, timing, route, disease danger, and alternatives; untreated maternal illness can be the greater threat. Antihypertensive choice in pregnancy is mechanism- and safety-specific. Antenatal corticosteroids accelerate selected fetal maturation when preterm birth risk fits guidance, distinct from chronic maternal steroid exposure. Rh immune globulin prevents sensitization by clearing susceptible fetal red cells before maternal immune memory forms; it does not treat established sensitization. Magnesium, anticoagulants, insulin, antimicrobials, and other therapies require pregnancy-specific indications and monitoring rather than blanket avoidance.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    Serial ultrasound evaluates fetal growth, anatomy, fluid, and placental location; Doppler studies assess selected maternal, placental, or fetal vessels. Nonstress testing and biophysical profile estimate current fetal well-being through heart-rate behavior and biophysical activity, not direct placental histology. Maternal blood pressure, urine protein assessment, CBC, type and antibody screen, glucose testing, liver and kidney markers, and hemolysis-related labs answer specific maternal risks. hCG supports early pregnancy evaluation but a single value neither proves normal location nor placental health. After bleeding or trauma, fetal monitoring, ultrasound, blood count, coagulation, Rh status, and fetomaternal-hemorrhage testing may be used according to gestation and clinical condition.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess gestational age, fetal movement, pain, contractions, bleeding amount and character, fluid leakage, trauma, headache or visual symptoms, edema in context, blood pressure, pulse, oxygenation, uterine tone and tenderness, and fetal heart pattern when appropriate. With instability, prioritize maternal airway, breathing, circulation, lateral displacement or positioning as indicated, vascular access, hemorrhage response, and rapid obstetric escalation because fetal perfusion depends on maternal perfusion. Quantify blood loss but remember abruption can be concealed. Avoid digital vaginal examination until placenta previa is excluded when bleeding makes it a concern. Administer Rh immune globulin and other ordered therapies based on blood type, exposure, timing, and protocol.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Any significant bleeding, syncope, shock, persistent severe abdominal or back pain, rigid tender uterus, reduced fetal movement, fluid leakage with prolapsed-cord concern, seizures, severe hypertension symptoms, or nonreassuring fetal pattern needs immediate obstetric evaluation. Painful bleeding with uterine tenderness suggests abruption, while painless bright bleeding can suggest previa, but presentations overlap and neither should be diagnosed from a slogan. Maternal hypotension after trauma, hemorrhage, regional anesthesia, or supine positioning can rapidly reduce placental flow. Cord prolapse, uterine rupture, severe preeclampsia, and placental separation are time-critical. Maternal stabilization is fetal resuscitation; do not delay it while focusing only on the monitor.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Maternal and fetal blood normally approach across an exchange barrier rather than freely mixing, yet cellular transfer can occur. Umbilical arteries carry relatively oxygen-poor blood away from the fetus and the umbilical vein carries more oxygenated blood toward it; vessel names follow direction relative to the fetal heart. The placenta is not an impenetrable filter. hCG is not produced by the maternal pituitary. A normal single fetal test does not guarantee future placental function. Visible bleeding may underestimate abruption. Preeclampsia is not explained by high blood pressure alone, and bed position is supportive physiology rather than definitive treatment for hemorrhage or placental disease.
