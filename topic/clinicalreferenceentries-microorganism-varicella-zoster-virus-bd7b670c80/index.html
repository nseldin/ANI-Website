Definition

Varicella-zoster virus is a clinically important virus associated with varicella, herpes zoster, postherpetic neuralgia, and disseminated zoster. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

Envelope status helps explain environmental stability and susceptibility to routine controls, while genome type shapes replication and diagnostic targets.   • Content

    Varicella and zoster vaccination, airborne and contact controls when indicated, and exposure management under current guidance reduce disease and spread. The reviewed route description is airborne transmission. The reviewed incubation estimate is 10 to 21 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Primary infection causes varicella, after which virus remains latent in sensory ganglia and may reactivate later as herpes zoster. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

  • Content

    Varicella-zoster virus is associated with varicella, herpes zoster, postherpetic neuralgia, and disseminated zoster. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.
