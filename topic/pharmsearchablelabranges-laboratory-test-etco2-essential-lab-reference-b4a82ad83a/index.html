Category

Perfusion / oxygenation

Name

EtCO2

Range

EtCO2 is the carbon-dioxide partial pressure measured at the end of exhalation, usually shown with a capnography waveform and reported in mm Hg. A commonly taught stable-adult anchor is 35-45 mm Hg, but it is not a universal normal, diagnostic, ventilator, or emergency-action threshold. Interpret the patient's baseline and trend with respiratory rate and tidal volume, cardiac output and pulmonary perfusion, dead space and ventilation-perfusion relationships, airway or sampling interface, spontaneous versus mechanical ventilation, and the clinical setting. Cardiac-arrest values require the separate resuscitation context.

Why

An EtCO2 result and waveform signal the combined effects of carbon-dioxide production, blood flow to the lungs, alveolar ventilation, and exhaled-gas sampling; a change can alter airway, ventilation, and perfusion assessment. A rising EtCO2 trend suggests reduced alveolar ventilation or rebreathing when production and perfusion are otherwise stable. A falling trend can signal hyperventilation, reduced pulmonary blood flow or cardiac output, increased dead space such as pulmonary embolism, a leak or disconnection, or sampling obstruction. Sudden waveform loss raises concern for apnea, airway displacement or obstruction, ventilator or circuit disconnection, or sampling failure; severe shock or cardiac arrest can also reduce exhaled CO2 despite a tracheal airway. Promptly assess the patient before the number: check airway, respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, mental status, SpO2, breath sounds, hemodynamics and perfusion, tube or mask position, circuit and sampling line, waveform shape, medications, ventilator settings, and the trend. Cause-directed evaluation can include an ABG when precise PaCO2 or the PaCO2-EtCO2 difference matters, repeat waveform and equipment verification, and clinician-selected ECG, chest imaging, or pulmonary-embolism evaluation based on the complete pattern. Escalate immediately for absent breathing, sudden waveform loss with inability to ventilate, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, shock, or rapid deterioration while checking the patient and the entire airway and sampling system. During CPR, an abrupt sustained rise can support return of spontaneous circulation and EtCO2 can help monitor compression physiology, but AHA guidance says it must not be used alone to terminate resuscitation. One EtCO2 value does not diagnose hypoventilation, pulmonary embolism, or low cardiac output and does not equal PaCO2; the gap can change with dead space, cardiac output, ventilation-perfusion imbalance, breathing pattern, leaks, secretions, condensate, and device factors.

High Causes

  • Reduced alveolar ventilation from a lower respiratory rate or tidal volume, respiratory depression, fatigue, obstruction, or insufficient mechanical ventilation

  • Rebreathing from a circuit, valve, absorbent, or fresh-gas-flow problem

  • Increased carbon-dioxide delivery or production, including return of pulmonary blood flow during resuscitation, fever, shivering, or seizures, interpreted with the full setting

Low Causes

  • Hyperventilation from increased respiratory rate or tidal volume

  • Reduced pulmonary blood flow or cardiac output, including shock or cardiac arrest

  • Increased dead space, pulmonary embolism, leak, airway or circuit disconnection, apnea, obstruction, or a contaminated or blocked sampling line

Critical Concerns

  • A suddenly absent waveform is an airway, breathing, circuit, perfusion, or sampling emergency until bedside assessment identifies the cause.

  • A low number during cardiac arrest can reflect pulmonary blood flow generated by compressions, but it must never be the only reason to stop resuscitation.

  • A widening or changing PaCO2-EtCO2 difference can make the monitor underestimate arterial CO2; obtain ordered arterial testing when exact PaCO2 matters.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess the patient, then trace the system from airway or interface through the circuit and sampling line while confirming waveform quality, respiratory pattern, perfusion, and ventilator settings.

  • Trend EtCO2 with the waveform and the patient's baseline; document the interface, ventilation mode, hemodynamics, intervention, and response rather than charting an isolated number.

  • Escalate a sudden change promptly and obtain an ordered ABG when the clinical question requires PaCO2 or when the arterial-to-end-tidal relationship may be unstable.

Nclex Traps

  • EtCO2 monitors ventilation and perfusion influences; SpO2 monitors oxygen saturation, so the two are complementary rather than interchangeable.

  • EtCO2 usually differs from PaCO2 and the difference is not fixed when dead space, cardiac output, breathing pattern, or disease changes.

  • During CPR, an abrupt rise can support return of spontaneous circulation, but no single EtCO2 number should make the resuscitation decision.
