Definition

The kidneys are continuously adjusting organs that filter plasma and then selectively reclaim or excrete water and solutes. Their job is not simply to make urine: they stabilize extracellular volume, osmolality, electrolytes, acid-base balance, blood pressure, red-cell production, mineral metabolism, and removal of metabolic waste and many medications.

Etiology

This is normal integrated physiology rather than a disease. Kidney dysfunction becomes clinically important when perfusion, glomerular filtration, tubular transport, urinary drainage, endocrine signaling, or total nephron mass fails, because each failure removes a different part of the kidney's homeostatic reserve.

Pathology

Each kidney contains nephrons supplied by a high-flow microcirculation. Glomeruli create an ultrafiltrate, tubules modify it segment by segment, collecting ducts make final water and electrolyte adjustments, and the renal interstitium and juxtaglomerular apparatus provide endocrine sensing and signaling.

Pathophysiology

Filtration moves water and small solutes into Bowman space while normally retaining cells and most protein. Tubular reabsorption returns needed sodium, water, glucose, bicarbonate, and other solutes; secretion moves selected acids, potassium, organic ions, and drugs into urine. Excretion equals filtration minus reabsorption plus secretion, so a normal urine volume does not prove that filtration or toxin clearance is normal. When functioning nephron mass falls, remaining nephrons initially hyperfilter and increase transport, but that compensation can raise intraglomerular stress and cannot indefinitely replace endocrine, acid-base, and clearance functions.

Risk Factors

  • Diabetes and hypertension that injure glomeruli and vessels

  • Older age or reduced nephron reserve

  • Sepsis, shock, dehydration, heart failure, or cirrhosis that reduce effective renal perfusion

  • Nephrotoxins, obstruction, immune disease, and inherited kidney disorders

Signs Symptoms

  • Early dysfunction may be silent because renal reserve and compensatory hyperfiltration mask nephron loss

  • Edema, hypertension, dyspnea, or rapid weight change when sodium and water balance fails

  • Weakness, dysrhythmia, confusion, nausea, pruritus, or bleeding when electrolyte, acid, or uremic disturbances become severe

  • Anemia and bone-mineral changes when endocrine functions decline

Diagnostics

  • Trend serum creatinine and estimated GFR rather than interpreting one value alone

  • Measure urine albumin or protein because filtration-barrier damage can precede a large GFR decline

  • Assess urine output, urinalysis, sediment, electrolytes, bicarbonate, and volume status

  • Use imaging or targeted serology when obstruction, structural disease, or immune injury is possible

Labs

  • Creatinine and eGFR estimate filtration but are influenced by muscle mass and non-steady-state illness

  • Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio reflects glomerular leak and adds risk information beyond eGFR

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, phosphorus, calcium, hemoglobin, and BUN reveal consequences that filtration alone misses

  • Urine osmolality and sodium help describe water and sodium handling only in the right clinical context

Treatments

  • Treat the cause of kidney stress and preserve perfusion without causing fluid overload

  • Control diabetes, blood pressure, albuminuria, and cardiovascular risk using individualized evidence-based therapy

  • Avoid or adjust nephrotoxins and renally cleared medicines as function changes

  • Replace failed homeostatic functions with dietary, medication, dialysis, transplant, or conservative kidney-management strategies when needed

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess filtration, urine volume, fluid status, electrolytes, acid-base balance, blood pressure, hemoglobin, and bone-mineral markers because kidneys coordinate excretion, homeostasis, and endocrine signaling rather than merely producing urine.

  • Trend creatinine and eGFR with potassium, bicarbonate, sodium, phosphate, calcium, hemoglobin, weight, blood pressure, and symptoms because one filtration marker cannot reveal every lost renal function.

  • Review medication clearance, nephrotoxins, contrast exposure, and over-the-counter products at every transition because reduced filtration and tubular secretion can turn ordinary doses into toxic exposures.

  • Coordinate individualized nutrition, fluid, blood-pressure, anemia, acid-base, and bone-mineral plans because correcting one renal consequence without considering the others can create competing harm.

  • Escalate urgently for hyperkalemic conduction change, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremic confusion, pericarditic pain, anuria, or hypertensive emergency because these indicate failed homeostasis requiring immediate treatment.

Complications

  • Acute kidney injury or progressive chronic kidney disease

  • Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and fluid overload

  • Medication accumulation and toxicity

  • Anemia, CKD-mineral and bone disorder, cardiovascular disease, uremia, and kidney failure

Contraindications

  • Do not infer total kidney health from urine volume, BUN, creatinine, or eGFR in isolation

  • Avoid automatically giving fluid for every creatinine rise because congestion and low effective arterial volume can coexist

Red Flags

  • Hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram change or malignant dysrhythmia

  • Pulmonary edema, anuria, or severe uncontrolled hypertension

  • Severe acidosis with shock, respiratory fatigue, or declining consciousness

  • Uremic encephalopathy, pericarditis, uncontrolled bleeding, or intractable symptoms

Patient Education

  • Explain that kidneys regulate water, electrolytes, acid, blood pressure, red-cell signaling, and vitamin-D activation in addition to removing wastes.

  • Teach patients to carry an updated medication list and kidney-function trend because dosing and safety can change before urine volume changes noticeably.

Nclex Traps

  • Urine production and filtration are related but not identical

  • Creatinine can look deceptively normal in low-muscle-mass patients

  • A swollen patient can still have poor effective arterial filling

  • Kidneys regulate much more than waste removal

Causal Links

  • Nephron loss -> compensatory hyperfiltration -> higher single-nephron pressure -> possible progressive scarring

  • Reduced filtration and secretion -> retained potassium, acids, wastes, and medicines -> systemic toxicity

  • Reduced sodium and water excretion -> extracellular volume expansion -> edema, hypertension, and pulmonary congestion

  • Loss of erythropoietin and vitamin-D activation -> anemia and mineral-bone complications
