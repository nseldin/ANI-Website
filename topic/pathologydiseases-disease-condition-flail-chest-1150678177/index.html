Definition

Flail chest develops when multiple adjacent ribs break in two places, uncoupling a chest-wall segment and worsening ventilation through pain, contusion, and paradoxical motion. The characteristic pattern is a visibly unstable segment moving inward on inspiration, severe pain, crepitus, hypoxemia, and an associated pulmonary contusion. Confirm or stage it with primary trauma survey with continuous oxygenation and ventilation assessment; distinguish it from simple rib fractures, diaphragmatic injury, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and isolated pulmonary contusion by chest-wall stability and imaging. The priority is to provide aggressive regional or multimodal analgesia and pulmonary hygiene because delayed pulmonary-contusion respiratory failure can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • primary trauma survey with continuous oxygenation and ventilation assessment

  • portable chest radiograph plus CT in a stable patient to define fractures and lung injury

  • serial blood gas, respiratory effort, cough, and imaging when deterioration occurs

  • Distinguish Flail chest from simple rib fractures, diaphragmatic injury, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and isolated pulmonary contusion by chest-wall stability and imaging.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • provide aggressive regional or multimodal analgesia and pulmonary hygiene

  • support oxygenation and use positive-pressure ventilation when fatigue or gas failure develops

  • treat associated pneumothorax, hemothorax, and hemorrhage promptly

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for rapidly increasing oxygen need, absent unilateral breath sounds, hypotension, worsening carbon dioxide, or exhaustion because pneumothorax, hemorrhage, or ventilatory collapse may coexist.

  • Assess respiratory effort, paradoxical chest motion, breath sounds, oxygen saturation, and pain because pulmonary contusion often drives hypoxemia more than the visible rib segment.

  • Provide ordered multimodal analgesia while reassessing ventilation because uncontrolled pain prevents deep breathing but oversedation can worsen hypoventilation.

  • Apply oxygen and support noninvasive or invasive ventilation as directed because fatigue and contusion can cause progressive respiratory failure.

  • Encourage supported coughing, pulmonary hygiene, and repositioning when stable because secretion retention increases atelectasis and pneumonia risk.

Complications

  • delayed pulmonary-contusion respiratory failure

  • pneumonia and secretion retention

  • pneumothorax can complicate Flail chest.

  • hemothorax can complicate Flail chest.

  • ventilator complications can complicate Flail chest.

Red Flags

  • Rapidly increasing oxygen need or respiratory fatigue

  • Absent unilateral breath sounds or sudden pressure rise

  • Hypotension or signs of intrathoracic bleeding

  • Rising carbon dioxide or decreasing consciousness

Patient Education

  • Teach splinted coughing, prescribed breathing exercises, safe analgesic use, and early mobility because shallow breathing raises pneumonia risk.

  • Explain that worsening dyspnea, blue color, faintness, fever, or uncontrolled pain after chest trauma requires urgent reassessment.

Nclex Traps

  • Paradoxical motion may be subtle once positive-pressure ventilation begins

  • Pain control is essential to ventilation, not optional comfort care
