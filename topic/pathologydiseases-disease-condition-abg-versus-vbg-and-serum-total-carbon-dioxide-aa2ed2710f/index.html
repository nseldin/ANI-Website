Risk Factors

  • Shock or poor peripheral perfusion makes peripheral venous values less representative of arterial physiology.

  • Severe respiratory failure requires accurate PaCO2 and oxygenation assessment when ventilatory decisions depend on small differences.

  • Air bubbles, delayed transport, excess heparin, difficult draws, and samples obtained after different interventions increase discordance.

Signs Symptoms

  • Work of breathing, mental status, respiratory rate and depth, SpO2 waveform, and hemodynamics determine whether a convenient venous screen is adequate.

  • Cyanosis, severe hypoxemia risk, exhaustion, or rising somnolence raises the need for direct arterial or other definitive monitoring.

  • A clinically implausible result suggests specimen or timing error until verified.

Diagnostics

  • Use VBG for appropriate acid-base screening or trending under local protocol, but obtain ABG when arterial oxygenation, an accurate PaCO2, or a high-stakes ventilation decision requires it.

  • Pair PaO2 with FiO2, oxygen device, SpO2, and respiratory support; do not report a venous PO2 as oxygenation status.

  • When serum total CO2 and gas HCO3 differ meaningfully, compare collection times, therapies, specimen handling, and repeat paired testing if the answer changes care.

Labs

  • ABG reports arterial pH, PaCO2, PaO2, and calculated HCO3; VBG reports venous pH, PvCO2, PvO2, and calculated HCO3.

  • Chemistry total CO2 is usually close to serum bicarbonate but includes dissolved CO2 and carbonic species.

  • Pulse oximetry estimates arterial saturation but does not measure PaCO2, pH, bicarbonate, dyshemoglobins, or tissue perfusion.

Treatments

  • Choose the least invasive specimen that can answer the clinical question without sacrificing needed precision.

  • Correct collection and transport problems before escalating therapy for a discordant number.

  • Treat the underlying oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, or metabolic disorder rather than the specimen label.

Nursing Priorities

  • Label source and site accurately, minimize air exposure, use the correct heparinized equipment, send promptly, and apply arterial-site pressure and distal-perfusion checks after ABG collection.

  • Document oxygen device, flow or FiO2, ventilator settings, patient temperature context when relevant, and time of any intervention.

  • Escalate worsening work of breathing or consciousness even when SpO2 is acceptable because supplemental oxygen can mask CO2 retention.

Complications

  • Arterial puncture can cause pain, hematoma, bleeding, arterial spasm, or rare distal ischemic injury.

  • Misinterpreting VBG oxygen values can delay treatment of hypoxemia.

  • Assuming a fixed venous-to-arterial CO2 difference can miss hypercapnic respiratory failure.

Contraindications

  • Do not use venous PO2 to assess arterial oxygenation.

  • Do not call PvCO2 PaCO2 or apply a universal numeric correction.

  • Do not compare serum total CO2 and gas HCO3 drawn hours apart or across major treatment as if they were simultaneous assays.

Red Flags

  • Severe respiratory distress, exhaustion, altered consciousness, or suspected ventilatory failure

  • Unreliable SpO2 with shock, dyshemoglobinemia, poor waveform, or profound hypoxemia concern

  • Large unexplained chemistry-to-gas bicarbonate discrepancy that would change treatment

Patient Education

  • Explain that a venous gas may answer many pH questions with less discomfort, but an arterial sample is sometimes necessary to measure lung oxygen transfer and precise ventilation.

  • Teach patients to report increasing pain, numbness, swelling, bleeding, or coolness after an arterial puncture.

  • Clarify that an acceptable oxygen saturation does not guarantee normal carbon dioxide removal.

Nclex Traps

  • VBG can support acid-base assessment but cannot replace PaO2.

  • SpO2 assesses oxygen saturation, not ventilation.

  • Serum total CO2 is mostly bicarbonate but is measured differently from calculated blood-gas HCO3.

Name

ABG versus VBG and serum total carbon dioxide

Category

Diagnostic testing and acid-base

Definition

An arterial blood gas directly measures arterial pH, PaCO2, and PaO2, whereas a venous blood gas measures venous pH and PvCO2 after tissue metabolism has added CO2 and removed oxygen. VBG pH can often support initial acid-base assessment, but venous PCO2 has wider agreement limits and cannot simply be relabeled PaCO2. Serum total carbon dioxide is a chemistry measurement composed mostly of bicarbonate; blood-gas bicarbonate is usually calculated from pH and PCO2, so the two are related but not analytically identical.

Etiology

Specimen choice depends on the clinical question. A venous sample is easier and may reduce arterial-puncture pain when screening acid-base status, while an arterial sample is required when precise arterial oxygenation or PaCO2 will change high-stakes management. Poor perfusion, shock, local venous stasis, tourniquet time, supplemental oxygen, air bubbles, delayed processing, and different draw times widen disagreement.

Pathology

Treating venous PO2 as arterial can falsely reassure or alarm because tissues have already extracted oxygen. Applying a fixed PvCO2-to-PaCO2 correction can miss ventilatory failure because the arteriovenous difference is not constant, especially in circulatory shock. A large serum total-CO2 versus gas-HCO3 discrepancy may reflect timing, handling, measurement or calculation differences, or a mislabeled sample and should trigger reconciliation rather than arithmetic averaging.

Pathophysiology

Arterial blood leaves the lungs before systemic extraction, so PaO2 reflects lung oxygen transfer and PaCO2 reflects whole-body CO2 elimination. Venous blood returns after tissue metabolism, making PvO2 much lower and PvCO2 generally higher. Across many patients venous pH tracks arterial pH closely enough for screening, but individual limits matter more than the average difference. Chemistry analyzers measure total CO2, of which bicarbonate is the dominant fraction, while a blood-gas analyzer derives HCO3 through the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation; preanalytic CO2 loss from an uncapped or delayed chemistry sample can lower measured total CO2.
