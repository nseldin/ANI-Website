Definition

Cholangitis develops when biliary obstruction permits infected bile to build under pressure and seed the bloodstream. The characteristic pattern is fever, right-upper-quadrant pain, jaundice, cholestatic tests, and in severe disease hypotension or confusion. Confirm or stage it with CBC, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, transaminases, lactate, cultures, and organ-function tests; distinguish it from acute cholecystitis, hepatitis, liver abscess, pancreatitis, and sepsis from another source by duct dilation, cholestasis, imaging, and cultures. The priority is to resuscitate and give broad biliary antimicrobial coverage because septic shock can complicate Cholangitis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • CBC, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, transaminases, lactate, cultures, and organ-function tests

  • ultrasound followed by CT or MRCP to define obstruction

  • ERCP as the diagnostic and therapeutic route when urgent drainage is needed

  • Distinguish Cholangitis from acute cholecystitis, hepatitis, liver abscess, pancreatitis, and sepsis from another source by duct dilation, cholestasis, imaging, and cultures.

Labs

  • hemoglobin may fall with bleeding

  • AST/ALT and bilirubin may rise with hepatic/biliary injury

  • lipase rises in pancreatitis

  • albumin and INR reflect liver synthetic function

Treatments

  • resuscitate and give broad biliary antimicrobial coverage

  • perform urgent endoscopic biliary drainage for severe or nonresponding obstruction

  • remove stones or address stricture or tumor after stabilization

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for hypotension, confusion, rising lactate, oliguria, persistent fever, or worsening jaundice because antibiotics alone may fail without emergency source control.

  • Obtain ordered blood cultures and administer broad antibiotic therapy promptly because an obstructed infected biliary system can progress rapidly to sepsis.

  • Assess fever, jaundice, right-upper-quadrant pain, hypotension, and mental status because shock or confusion signals severe ascending cholangitis.

  • Monitor bilirubin, liver enzymes, white blood cell count, lactate, blood pressure, urine output, and pain to detect worsening obstruction and organ dysfunction.

  • Maintain intravenous fluids and nothing-by-mouth status as ordered because urgent endoscopic or percutaneous biliary drainage may require sedation or intervention.

Complications

  • septic shock can complicate Cholangitis.

  • liver abscess can complicate Cholangitis.

  • acute kidney injury can complicate Cholangitis.

  • disseminated coagulation can complicate Cholangitis.

  • recurrent obstruction can complicate Cholangitis.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension or altered mental status with jaundice and fever

  • Rising lactate or decreasing urine output

  • Persistent fever despite antibiotic therapy

  • Worsening jaundice or severe right-upper-quadrant pain

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient that fever, jaundice, chills, confusion, or worsening upper abdominal pain with gallstone or stent disease requires emergency care.

  • Explain that opening the blocked bile duct may be necessary because antibiotic therapy cannot reliably sterilize an obstructed system.

Nclex Traps

  • Antibiotics alone may fail while an obstructed infected duct remains pressurized

  • Absence of the complete classic triad does not exclude severe cholangitis
