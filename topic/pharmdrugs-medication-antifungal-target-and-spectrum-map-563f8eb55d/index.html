Used To Treat

A foundation card for choosing and comparing antifungals by molecular target, organism, infection site, formulation, resistance pattern, and host toxicity rather than treating 'antifungal' as one interchangeable class.

Description

Antifungal drugs exploit structures or enzymes that differ from human cells, but each class reaches a different subset of fungi and body sites. Polyenes such as amphotericin B and nystatin bind ergosterol already present in the fungal membrane and create permeability defects. Azoles inhibit lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, encoded by fungal CYP51/ERG11, so ergosterol falls and abnormal methylated sterols accumulate. Echinocandins inhibit Fks-containing beta-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase, weakening a cell-wall polymer humans do not make. Flucytosine is converted inside susceptible fungi into fluorinated metabolites that disrupt RNA and thymidylate synthesis. Terbinafine blocks squalene epoxidase earlier in ergosterol synthesis, while griseofulvin disrupts fungal mitosis and concentrates in newly formed keratin. These mechanistic differences explain why an echinocandin can be excellent for candidemia yet inactive against Cryptococcus, why nystatin treats local Candida but not bloodstream infection, and why a drug active in vitro may still fail if it cannot reach the infected compartment.

Mechanism

A useful choice sequence is organism, site, severity, host, then formulation. Organism matters because Candida species, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, Mucorales, dermatophytes, and endemic dimorphic fungi have different target biology and intrinsic susceptibility. Site matters because urine, cerebrospinal fluid, eye, bone, lung cavities, and avascular necrotic tissue receive drugs differently. Severity determines whether fungicidal induction, combination therapy, surgery, device removal, or rapid IV exposure is needed. Host factors alter clearance, QT risk, immune recovery, and interaction burden. Finally, formulations that share a drug name can have different absorption or toxicity; posaconazole suspension is not dose-for-dose interchangeable with delayed-release tablets, and amphotericin products are not milligram-for-milligram interchangeable. The result is a causal map, not a ranking from weak to strong.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Class membership never substitutes for the exact product label. Major preventable harms include amphotericin-related kidney and electrolyte injury, azole hepatotoxicity and CYP interactions, QT effects, echinocandin hepatic or infusion reactions, and flucytosine marrow toxicity when renal clearance falls.

Adverse Effects

  • Membrane selectivity is incomplete: amphotericin can interact with mammalian cholesterol, helping explain infusion and organ toxicity.

  • Enzyme and transporter interactions are especially important with azoles because human CYP pathways and many co-medications are affected even though fungal CYP51 is the therapeutic target.

Contraindications

  • Do not choose by the word 'yeast' or 'mold' alone. Identify the species and susceptibility when possible, because Candida krusei is intrinsically fluconazole resistant and Mucorales are not treated with voriconazole.

  • Do not assume topical, oral-local, and systemic formulations are interchangeable. Local concentration can be excellent while serum exposure remains negligible.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before the first dose, document organism evidence, suspected site, culture and pathology collection, allergy, pregnancy context, renal and hepatic function, potassium and magnesium, QT-active medicines, immunosuppression, and all interacting drugs.

  • Trend clinical response and microbiology together. Persistent fever alone does not prove failure, while a negative blood culture does not remove the need for source control, eye or metastatic-site assessment, or a full treatment duration.

Interactions

  • Azoles can inhibit or be affected by CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, P-glycoprotein, and other transporters; transplant immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, antiarrhythmics, statins, anticonvulsants, and oncology drugs are common high-risk partners.

  • Amphotericin adds nephrotoxicity and potassium or magnesium loss to other nephrotoxic or electrolyte-wasting drugs; flucytosine accumulation rises when renal function falls.

Key Labs

  • Culture, histopathology, species identification, susceptibility when indicated, CBC, creatinine/eGFR, AST/ALT/bilirubin, potassium, magnesium, and ECG or drug levels when the selected agent and context require them.

  • A beta-D-glucan, galactomannan, cryptococcal antigen, or fungal PCR answers a specific diagnostic question; none is a universal 'fungus test,' and treatment response cannot be inferred from one biomarker in isolation.

Nclex Traps

  • Echinocandins target the fungal cell wall, not ergosterol. Azoles inhibit ergosterol synthesis; polyenes bind existing ergosterol.

  • Nystatin is a polyene, not a statin. Its name does not imply HMG-CoA-reductase inhibition.

Source Note

Mechanisms and safety synthesized from current U.S. labels for amphotericin B liposome, fluconazole, caspofungin, flucytosine, and nystatin plus IDSA candidiasis/aspergillosis guidance.
