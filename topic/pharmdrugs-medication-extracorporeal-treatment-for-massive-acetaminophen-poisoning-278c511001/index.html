Used To Treat

Rare massive acetaminophen poisoning with an extreme concentration plus early altered mental status, severe metabolic acidosis, or elevated lactate consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction, according to EXTRIP criteria. Routine acetaminophen overdose is treated with N-acetylcysteine, not dialysis.

Description

Most acetaminophen overdoses do not need extracorporeal treatment because prompt N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione and detoxifies the reactive metabolite while the parent drug is cleared. A rare, massive ingestion can produce a different early syndrome: extraordinarily high parent-drug concentrations impair mitochondrial function before classic hepatic failure, causing coma, lactic acidosis, hypothermia, and shock. In that narrow setting, acetaminophen is sufficiently small and water soluble for hemodialysis to remove meaningful amounts. EXTRIP therefore reserves ECTR for concentration-plus-clinical criteria rather than for a positive nomogram alone. NAC must continue during dialysis and usually requires an increased protocol rate because the circuit also removes NAC.

Mechanism

Intermittent hemodialysis moves free acetaminophen down a concentration gradient across a high-flux membrane into dialysate, rapidly reducing an extreme circulating parent-drug burden and helping correct acidosis. This is useful only when the parent concentration is high enough to cause direct mitochondrial toxicity; ordinary NAPQI-mediated liver risk is better prevented by NAC. NAC supplies cysteine for glutathione synthesis and also supports reduction and hemodynamics, but it is a small dialyzable molecule. Continuing it at an increased toxicology-directed rate during ECTR prevents dialysis from creating an antidote gap. Dialysis cannot reverse established hepatic necrosis, so transplant-center evaluation remains necessary when liver failure develops.

Administration Timing

  • Give NAC immediately whenever acetaminophen toxicity is possible; never wait for an ECTR decision. Obtain an early concentration, blood gas, lactate, mental status, glucose, and liver/coagulation studies, and call Poison Help/medical toxicology for a massive ingestion.

  • EXTRIP recommends ECTR for defined extreme concentrations when NAC was not given and for very high concentrations accompanied by altered mental status, metabolic acidosis, and elevated lactate, including a higher threshold even when NAC is already running. Use the current EXTRIP units and criteria rather than memory or the Rumack-Matthew nomogram.

  • Intermittent hemodialysis is preferred when ECTR is indicated. Continue NAC during treatment at the poison-center protocol's increased rate, monitor the circuit and serial levels, and continue until clinical and biochemical stopping criteria are met.

Nursing Essentials

  • Start and maintain NAC without interruption across transfers, line procedures, and dialysis because ECTR removes both acetaminophen and the antidote; independently verify any increased infusion rate and total fluid load.

  • Differentiate early massive mitochondrial toxicity from later hepatic failure by timing, concentration, pH/lactate, mental status, glucose, AST/ALT, and INR because the reason for dialysis and the reason for transplant referral are not identical.

  • Use continuous ECG, temperature, glucose, blood pressure, oxygenation, neurologic, and urine-output monitoring; correct hypoglycemia and shock while dialysis access is arranged.

  • Record formulation, ingestion time, amount, staggered dosing, coingestants, every acetaminophen level, NAC bag/rate, ECTR start/stop, clearance, acid-base response, and rebound so no treatment gap is hidden by handoff.

Key Labs

  • Serial acetaminophen concentration with units, arterial or venous blood gas, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, AST/ALT, bilirubin, PT/INR, fibrinogen, phosphate, ammonia when liver failure is suspected, and pregnancy testing when relevant.

  • During and after ECTR, repeat concentration, pH, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, ionized calcium, hemodynamics, and NAC delivery. Continue hepatic-failure prognostic studies because a falling drug level does not erase organ injury.

Adverse Effects

  • ECTR can cause hypotension, access bleeding or infection, dysrhythmia, electrolyte shifts, hypothermia, and clearance of beneficial medications. NAC can cause anaphylactoid reactions but should usually be managed and continued because stopping removes protection.

  • Rapid correction can mask ongoing delayed absorption from a massive tablet burden; post-dialysis rebound requires serial measurement.

Contraindications

  • Do not dialyze routine acetaminophen overdose solely because the concentration is above the treatment line, transaminases are elevated, or NAC has begun. ECTR benefit must exceed procedural risk and meet the rare massive-poisoning pattern.

  • Hemodynamic instability may complicate intermittent HD but does not erase the need for toxin removal; the extracorporeal team selects the feasible modality. Never withhold NAC because dialysis is planned.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Worsening acidosis, lactate, coma, hypothermia, shock, or extreme persistent concentration requires urgent ECTR initiation and resuscitation. Rising INR, hypoglycemia, encephalopathy, renal injury, or severe hepatic acidosis requires early transplant-center consultation.

  • Rebound acetaminophen or recurrent acidosis after ECTR requires reassessment of ongoing absorption, additional ECTR, and NAC continuation. Stop only on toxicology-directed clinical, concentration, and hepatic criteria.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP recommendations address an uncommon phenotype and rely on limited clinical evidence plus pharmacokinetic rationale. Exact concentration thresholds depend on whether NAC is running and whether mitochondrial findings are present.

  • This card intentionally avoids turning ECTR into routine acetaminophen care. NAC remains the standard antidote for nearly all treated overdoses.

Nclex Traps

  • Routine Tylenol overdose gets prompt NAC, not dialysis.

  • Massive APAP ECTR is driven by extreme concentration plus early mitochondrial findings such as coma, lactic acidosis, and shock, not liver enzymes alone.

  • Continue NAC at an increased specialist-directed rate during hemodialysis because the circuit removes NAC.

Regulatory Status

EXTRIP consensus rescue, not an FDA drug indication. ECTR is reserved for rare massive poisoning and requires medical toxicology, nephrology, and critical-care coordination.
