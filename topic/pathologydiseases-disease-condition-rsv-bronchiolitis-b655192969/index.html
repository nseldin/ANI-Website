Definition

RSV bronchiolitis develops when respiratory syncytial virus inflames and plugs infant bronchioles, increasing resistance and causing air trapping, atelectasis, and feeding-related respiratory stress. The characteristic pattern is rhinorrhea followed by cough, wheeze or crackles, tachypnea, retractions, apnea, hypoxemia, and poor feeding in an infant. Confirm or stage it with clinical respiratory, hydration, apnea, and feeding assessment; distinguish it from asthma, bacterial pneumonia, pertussis, heart failure, foreign body, and sepsis by age, fever, focal findings, recurrence, and cardiac signs. The priority is to use nasal suction, hydration, feeding support, and oxygen when persistently needed because apnea can complicate RSV bronchiolitis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • clinical respiratory, hydration, apnea, and feeding assessment

  • pulse oximetry interpreted with persistent state rather than brief dips

  • viral testing or imaging only when it changes infection control or an atypical diagnosis

  • Distinguish RSV bronchiolitis from asthma, bacterial pneumonia, pertussis, heart failure, foreign body, and sepsis by age, fever, focal findings, recurrence, and cardiac signs.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • use nasal suction, hydration, feeding support, and oxygen when persistently needed

  • provide high-flow or ventilatory support for worsening effort or apnea

  • avoid routine bronchodilator, corticosteroid, antibiotic, and radiograph use in typical disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for apnea, cyanosis, exhaustion, poor air movement, dehydration, or rapidly rising oxygen need because high-flow support or ventilation may be required.

  • Suction the nose gently before feeding and respiratory assessment because infants rely heavily on nasal airflow and obstruction increases work of breathing.

  • Assess respiratory rate, retractions, nasal flaring, apnea, color, oxygen saturation, hydration, and feeding because fatigue may appear as a slowing respiratory rate.

  • Provide oxygen and hydration support as ordered while using smaller or alternate feeds when unsafe because tachypnea increases aspiration and dehydration risk.

  • Use contact precautions and avoid routine antibiotic therapy without bacterial evidence because RSV is viral and spreads readily through secretions.

  • Prepare high-flow support, noninvasive ventilation, or intubation when exhaustion appears because infants can lose respiratory reserve rapidly after prolonged retractions.

Complications

  • apnea can complicate RSV bronchiolitis.

  • dehydration can complicate RSV bronchiolitis.

  • respiratory failure can complicate RSV bronchiolitis.

  • atelectasis can complicate RSV bronchiolitis.

  • bacterial coinfection in selected infants

Red Flags

  • Apnea, cyanosis, or decreasing responsiveness

  • Exhaustion or markedly reduced air movement

  • Inability to feed with decreasing urine output

  • Rapidly increasing oxygen or ventilatory requirement

Patient Education

  • Teach caregivers nasal suction technique, hand hygiene, smoke avoidance, hydration cues, and safe sleep positioning.

  • Tell caregivers to seek urgent care for pauses in breathing, blue color, worsening retractions, poor feeding, or fewer wet diapers.

Nclex Traps

  • Wheezing in a first infant illness does not automatically mean asthma

  • A tiring infant may show fewer retractions immediately before failure
