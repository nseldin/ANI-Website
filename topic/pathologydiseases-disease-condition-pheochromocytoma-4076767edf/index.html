Definition

Pheochromocytoma develops when a catecholamine-secreting chromaffin tumor produces episodic or sustained alpha- and beta-adrenergic stimulation. The characteristic pattern is paroxysmal headache, sweating, palpitations, tremor, pallor, and labile hypertension, sometimes triggered by anesthesia, procedures, medicines, or tumor manipulation. Confirm or stage it with plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines obtained under appropriate conditions; distinguish it from panic disorder, medication or stimulant effects, hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, and pseudopheochromocytoma by metanephrine results and context. The priority is to establish alpha blockade and volume preparation before definitive surgery because catecholamine crisis with stroke can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Urine output change, edema, thirst, dry mucosa, dizziness, flank/suprapubic pain, dysuria, confusion, weakness, weight change, or abnormal heart rhythm can reveal renal, fluid, or electrolyte stress.

  • Escalate anuria, severe potassium abnormality, pulmonary edema, seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, or worsening acidosis.

Diagnostics

  • plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines obtained under appropriate conditions

  • CT or MRI only after biochemical evidence is established

  • genetic evaluation because hereditary syndromes and multifocal disease are clinically important

  • Distinguish Pheochromocytoma from panic disorder, medication or stimulant effects, hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, and pseudopheochromocytoma by metanephrine results and context.

Labs

  • glucose may be high or low depending on condition

  • sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate changes can become life-threatening

  • anion gap and ketones matter in DKA

Treatments

  • establish alpha blockade and volume preparation before definitive surgery

  • add beta blockade only after adequate alpha blockade when tachycardia requires it

  • avoid known provoking drugs and coordinate experienced perioperative care

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for severe labile hypertension, chest pain, pulmonary edema, focal deficit, seizure, hyperthermia, arrhythmia, rising lactate, or shock and institute seizure precautions when indicated because catecholamine crisis requires expert intravenous control and critical care.

  • Measure blood pressure and pulse in a calm setting, assess episodic headache, sweating, palpitations, chest or abdominal pain, tremor, and pallor, and obtain an ECG and troponin during chest pain or crisis because catecholamine surges can injure heart, brain, and vessels.

  • Avoid unapproved abdominal palpation; review anesthesia, procedures, decongestants, stimulants, dopamine-blocking drugs, glucocorticoids, and other triggers; and trend prescribed metanephrines, glucose, and potassium because tumor manipulation and certain medicines can provoke crisis and metabolic shifts.

  • Administer prescribed alpha blockade before beta blockade and before planned adrenalectomy, then monitor orthostatic pressure, hydration, sodium intake, and falls and prepare protocol-directed IV fluid or vasopressor support because reversing chronic catecholamine vasoconstriction can cause marked hypotension.

  • Do not give isolated beta blockade before adequate alpha control and clarify the sequence because unopposed alpha vasoconstriction can worsen hypertensive crisis.

Complications

  • catecholamine crisis with stroke

  • arrhythmia can complicate Pheochromocytoma.

  • pulmonary edema can complicate Pheochromocytoma.

  • perioperative hypertensive or hypotensive collapse

  • recurrent or metastatic disease

Red Flags

  • Severe or rapidly fluctuating hypertension with headache and diaphoresis

  • Chest pain, pulmonary edema, or unstable arrhythmia

  • Focal neurologic deficit, seizure, or altered consciousness

  • Hypotension or shock after a hypertensive surge or medication change

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to keep a complete medicine list and tell every procedural and anesthesia team about the tumor because common drugs or manipulation can trigger a crisis.

  • Explain why alpha medicine comes before beta medicine, rise slowly during preparation, and seek emergency care for severe headache, chest pain, weakness, or breathlessness.

Nclex Traps

  • Never give isolated beta blockade before alpha blockade because unopposed vasoconstriction can worsen crisis

  • Do not image incidental symptoms before obtaining interpretable biochemical testing
