Used To Treat

Severe central antimuscarinic delirium after a compatible exposure, using cooling, fluids, agitation and seizure control, ECG-guided treatment of sodium-channel effects, and carefully selected physostigmine when contraindications are absent.

Description

Antimuscarinic poisoning produces agitation or delirium, hallucinations, mydriasis, dry flushed skin, dry mouth, urinary retention, reduced bowel sounds, tachycardia, and hyperthermia. Central delirium distinguishes it from simple peripheral side effects, while dry skin and absent bowel sounds help separate it from a sympathomimetic state, which is often sweaty. Diphenhydramine and some other agents also block cardiac sodium channels, so a wide QRS is not just a severity marker: it changes treatment toward sodium bicarbonate and away from physostigmine. Physostigmine can rapidly reverse a selected pure antimuscarinic delirium, but its cholinergic effects can cause bradycardia, bronchospasm, seizure, or heart block when the diagnosis or substrate is wrong.

Mechanism

Antimuscarinic drugs competitively block acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors in brain, eye, heart, glands, bowel, and bladder. Physostigmine is a tertiary acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood-brain barrier, increases synaptic acetylcholine, and can overcome central and peripheral receptor blockade. Neostigmine is quaternary and does not reliably reverse central delirium. Physostigmine does not correct sodium-channel blockade, and rapid or excessive administration can create a cholinergic crisis; this explains the need for slow titration, continuous ECG monitoring, and atropine immediately available for excessive muscarinic effect.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 and Poison Help for severe agitation, hyperthermia, seizure, collapse, or suspected large ingestion. Stabilize airway and circulation, check glucose and temperature, obtain an ECG before antidote selection, actively cool severe hyperthermia, and treat wide-QRS sodium-channel blockade under protocol.

  • Reserve physostigmine for a convincing antimuscarinic delirium after toxicology review and exclusion of high-risk mixed or tricyclic exposure, QRS widening or conduction disease, significant bradycardia, and seizure risk. Give by slow monitored titration using the verified locally available product; exact adult and pediatric regimens are not interchangeable.

  • Observe for recurrent delirium because physostigmine may wear off before the culprit drug. Repeat treatment is specialist directed and must never become a substitute for rechecking ECG, temperature, bladder, bowel, airway, and the diagnosis.

Nursing Essentials

  • Search for antihistamines, tricyclics, antipsychotics, antispasmodics, bladder drugs, motion-sickness products, plants, eye drops, and patches. Remove transdermal sources with gloves and inspect for mixed products.

  • Use a low-stimulation environment, external cooling, IV fluids, bladder scanning, fall protection, and benzodiazepines for seizure or dangerous agitation as directed. Physical restraint without adequate sedation can worsen heat and acidosis.

  • For physostigmine, verify concentration and availability, continuous ECG and oxygen monitoring, IV patency, slow administration, and atropine plus resuscitation equipment at bedside. Document the pre-dose QRS, mental status, and response.

Key Labs

  • ECG with QRS and QTc, glucose, electrolytes, bicarbonate, creatinine, temperature, CK, urinalysis, and co-ingestion studies such as acetaminophen when indicated. Drug screens do not reliably identify every antimuscarinic agent.

  • Rising CK, creatinine, potassium, or acidosis can reflect agitation, hyperthermia, seizure, rhabdomyolysis, or sodium-channel toxicity and should change escalation.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause traumatic agitation, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, urinary retention, ileus, seizure, wide-complex dysrhythmia, coma, and death.

  • Physostigmine can cause salivation, vomiting, diarrhea, bronchospasm, bradycardia, heart block, asystole, seizure, and cholinergic weakness, especially after rapid dosing or wrong-patient selection.

Contraindications

  • Do not use physostigmine with suspected tricyclic or other mixed cardiotoxic overdose, QRS widening or significant conduction disease, active seizure risk, severe asthma or bronchospasm, mechanical GI or urinary obstruction, or an uncertain diagnosis because raising acetylcholine can precipitate bradyarrhythmia, bronchospasm, seizure, or obstructive complications in those settings.

  • Do not confuse dry antimuscarinic hyperthermia with infection and assume antipyretics will fix it; heat is generated by agitation and impaired sweating and requires cooling and toxidrome control.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Wide QRS, ventricular dysrhythmia, seizure, severe hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, coma, recurrent delirium, urinary obstruction, or physostigmine-induced cholinergic findings requires ICU and medical toxicology escalation.

Interactions

  • Tricyclic antidepressants, diphenhydramine, carbamazepine, and other sodium-channel blockers can combine antimuscarinic delirium with cardiotoxicity, making the ECG decisive.

  • Other cholinesterase inhibitors amplify cholinergic toxicity. Beta blockers or conduction-slowing drugs increase bradycardia risk after physostigmine.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Plants, antihistamine liquids, patches, and adult tablets can cause abrupt delirium; all antidote dosing is weight based and requires continuous monitoring.

  • Population

    Older adults

    Note

    Therapeutic anticholinergic burden, dementia, urinary obstruction, glaucoma, and conduction disease complicate both diagnosis and rescue.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Severe maternal hyperthermia, seizure, and hypoxia are immediate threats; physostigmine evidence is limited and selection should be specialist directed.

Evidence Limitations

  • Physostigmine evidence includes observational studies and expert toxicology practice rather than modern large randomized trials. U.S. availability has been unstable; the cited FDA page documents 2023 temporary importation and does not guarantee current local supply or a current U.S.-approved presentation. Verify product and shortage status before authoring a local protocol.

Nclex Traps

  • Physostigmine crosses the blood-brain barrier; neostigmine does not reliably reverse central delirium.

  • A wide QRS after diphenhydramine points to sodium-channel blockade and bicarbonate, not reflex physostigmine.

  • Atropine is kept ready to treat excessive physostigmine effect even though atropine itself can worsen the original antimuscarinic syndrome.

Regulatory Status

Physostigmine use for antimuscarinic delirium is specialist-directed and product availability is time sensitive. The FDA source documents temporary importation during a shortage, not guaranteed current availability; local pharmacy must verify the lawful product, concentration, labeling, and supply.

Boxed Warning

PHYSOSTIGMINE SELECTION WARNING: severe delirium or hyperthermia requires emergency care, but physostigmine is not a blind antidote. Obtain an ECG and exclude tricyclic or other cardiotoxic mixed exposure, wide QRS, conduction disease, and seizure risk because physostigmine does not reverse sodium-channel blockade and can worsen conduction or provoke seizure in the wrong substrate. Give only by slow monitored specialist protocol with atropine and resuscitation equipment ready because excessive cholinergic effect can cause bradycardia, bronchospasm, or asystole.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
