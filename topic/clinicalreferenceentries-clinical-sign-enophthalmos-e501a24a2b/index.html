Definition

Enophthalmos means one or both eyeballs sit abnormally far back within the bony eye socket, creating a sunken-eye appearance.

Plain Language

Enophthalmos means the eye has moved backward into the eye socket.

Why It Matters

The finding can reveal an orbital fracture, loss of supporting tissue, sinus disease, scarring, surgery-related change, or systemic illness. After facial trauma, associated double vision, restricted eye movement, reduced vision, severe pain, nausea, or an abnormal pupil can signal muscle entrapment or another urgent orbital injury.

Associated Conditions

  • orbital-wall fracture or expansion, loss or atrophy of orbital fat, silent sinus syndrome, scarring after inflammation, infection, surgery or radiation, congenital orbital difference, systemic wasting, or connective-tissue disease

Assessment Method

Compare both globes from the front and above and with prior photographs, inspect the superior eyelid sulcus and lid opening, assess visual acuity, pupils, eyelid closure, eye position and movements, diplopia, facial sensation, trauma, sinus disease, surgery, radiation, and systemic weight loss, and use exophthalmometry and orbital imaging when clinically indicated.

Positive Finding

One or both eyeballs sit farther back within the bony orbit, often producing a sunken appearance, a deeper upper-lid hollow, narrower lid opening, or associated eyelid malposition and limited eye movement.

Mechanism

Loss of orbital soft-tissue volume, enlargement of the bony orbital cavity, or fibrotic contraction reduces forward support and pulls or allows the globe to move posteriorly.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Enophthalmos finding raises concern for orbital-wall fracture or expansion, loss or atrophy of orbital fat, silent sinus syndrome, scarring after inflammation, infection, surgery or radiation, congenital orbital difference, systemic wasting, or connective-tissue disease because loss of orbital soft-tissue volume, enlargement of the bony orbital cavity, or fibrotic contraction reduces forward support and pulls or allows the globe to move posteriorly. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Enophthalmos does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Enophthalmos to focus evaluation of orbital-wall fracture or expansion, loss or atrophy of orbital fat, silent sinus syndrome, scarring after inflammation, infection, surgery or radiation, congenital orbital difference, systemic wasting, or connective-tissue disease and the reviewed alternatives: pseudo-enophthalmos, microphthalmos, ptosis, contralateral proptosis, facial hemiatrophy. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compare both globes from the front and above and with prior photographs, inspect the superior eyelid sulcus and lid opening, assess visual acuity, pupils, eyelid closure, eye position and movements, diplopia, facial sensation, trauma, sinus disease, surgery, radiation, and systemic weight loss, and use exophthalmometry and orbital imaging when clinically indicated. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Enophthalmos as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Facial asymmetry, small globe size, ptosis, contralateral proptosis, old photographs, and viewing angle can create a false sunken appearance; mild change is difficult to measure, and the apparent enophthalmos of Horner syndrome is often caused by lid narrowing rather than true globe displacement. Therefore, Enophthalmos cannot confirm or exclude orbital-wall fracture or expansion, loss or atrophy of orbital fat, silent sinus syndrome, scarring after inflammation, infection, surgery or radiation, congenital orbital difference, systemic wasting, or connective-tissue disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • pseudo-enophthalmos

  • microphthalmos

  • ptosis

  • contralateral proptosis

  • facial hemiatrophy

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as pseudo-enophthalmos, microphthalmos, ptosis, contralateral proptosis, facial hemiatrophy.

False Negative Situations

Enophthalmos may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of orbital-wall fracture or expansion, loss or atrophy of orbital fat, silent sinus syndrome, scarring after inflammation, infection, surgery or radiation, congenital orbital difference, systemic wasting, or connective-tissue disease.

Modern Status

Current descriptive orbital sign, not a diagnosis; true posterior displacement must be separated from pseudo-enophthalmos, and post-traumatic muscle entrapment or open-globe injury can be time-sensitive. Interpret Enophthalmos in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Facial asymmetry, small globe size, ptosis, contralateral proptosis, old photographs, and viewing angle can create a false sunken appearance; mild change is difficult to measure, and the apparent enophthalmos of Horner syndrome is often caused by lid narrowing rather than true globe displacement.

Safety Precautions

Observe Enophthalmos with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Compare both globes from the front and above and with prior photographs, inspect the superior eyelid sulcus and lid opening, assess visual acuity, pupils, eyelid closure, eye position and movements, diplopia, facial sensation, trauma, sinus disease, surgery, radiation, and systemic weight loss, and use exophthalmometry and orbital imaging when clinically indicated. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not dismiss new enophthalmos after facial trauma; reduced vision, severe eye pain, abnormal pupil, restricted or painful eye movement, diplopia, nausea or vomiting with attempted gaze, facial numbness, suspected open globe, or rapidly changing position requires urgent orbital and ophthalmic evaluation.
