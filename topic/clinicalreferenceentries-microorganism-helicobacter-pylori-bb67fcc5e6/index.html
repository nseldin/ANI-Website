Definition

Helicobacter pylori is a curved, microaerophilic Gram-negative bacterium adapted to long-term residence in the gastric mucus layer. Persistent infection causes chronic gastritis and is a major cause of peptic ulcer disease; it also raises the risk of gastric adenocarcinoma and gastric MALT lymphoma. Infection may be silent for years, so finding the organism and deciding whether symptoms are caused by it are related but distinct clinical steps.

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    H. pylori is one Helicobacter species. The abbreviation names the organism, while H. pylori infection, gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer name different clinical outcomes. Not every infected person develops an ulcer, and not every ulcer is caused by H. pylori because NSAIDs and other disorders can produce similar injury.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The organism is a curved or spiral, motile, non-spore-forming Gram-negative rod that grows best under low-oxygen conditions. Flagella let it move through gastric mucus. Urease converts urea to ammonia and carbon dioxide, buffering acid in the local microenvironment. This does not neutralize the entire stomach; it creates a narrow protective niche that helps the organism survive close to epithelial cells.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Humans are the principal reservoir, with colonization centered in gastric mucus and along the epithelial surface. Transmission is incompletely defined but can involve contact with infected saliva, vomit, or fecal material, often acquired in childhood. Once established, infection can persist for decades without eradication therapy, so a short species-wide incubation period does not describe the chronic biology.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Urease, motility, adhesins, and strain-specific factors support colonization despite acidity. Chronic immune activation injures the mucosa and alters acid regulation. Antral-predominant inflammation can increase gastrin and acid delivery to the duodenum, favoring duodenal ulceration, whereas extensive gastric injury can impair protective mechanisms and contribute to gastric ulcers and atrophy. Repeated epithelial injury and repair help explain the long-term cancer association.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Many infected people have no symptoms. When disease occurs, dyspepsia or ulcer pain may be present, but symptoms alone cannot establish the organism. Gastrointestinal bleeding can produce melena, hematemesis, anemia, dizziness, or shock; sudden severe abdominal pain can signal perforation. These are emergency findings, not indications to wait for routine outpatient testing.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Urea breath testing and stool antigen testing detect active infection and can also confirm eradication when timed correctly after therapy. Endoscopy with biopsy supports diagnosis when alarm features or another indication exists. Serology may reflect prior exposure and cannot reliably prove current infection or cure. Proton-pump inhibitors, bismuth, and recent antibiotics can suppress organism burden and create false-negative active tests, so preparation and timing matter.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Hand hygiene, safe food and water practices, and avoidance of exposure to vomit or fecal contamination reduce enteric transmission opportunities. Eradication requires a complete combination regimen selected for prior antibiotic exposure, local resistance, allergy, and current guidance, followed by a test of cure. One antibiotic alone is not adequate and encourages failure or resistance. Bleeding, perforation, obstruction, or suspected cancer requires urgent disease-focused care.
