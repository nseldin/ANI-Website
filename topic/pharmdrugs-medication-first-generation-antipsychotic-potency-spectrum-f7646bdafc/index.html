Used To Treat

Mechanism-based selection and adverse-effect prediction among first-generation antipsychotics used for schizophrenia-spectrum illness, severe agitation, and selected product-specific indications.

Description

Potency describes how much drug is needed for D2 blockade, not how powerful the final antipsychotic response is. High-potency haloperidol and fluphenazine bind D2 strongly at relatively small doses, so nigrostriatal EPS, akathisia, prolactin elevation, and NMS dominate. Low-potency chlorpromazine requires more milligrams and occupies alpha-1, H1, and muscarinic receptors more strongly, so orthostasis, sedation, weight gain, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, and heat intolerance dominate. Every member can still cause every class toxicity.

Mechanism

D2 antagonism is the shared antipsychotic mechanism. High D2 selectivity creates a steep motor/endocrine cost because little off-target receptor activity limits the dose first. Chlorpromazine's alpha-1 blockade prevents reflex vasoconstriction, causing orthostatic hypotension; H1 blockade promotes somnolence and appetite; M1 blockade reduces secretions, bowel and bladder contraction, sweating, accommodation, and memory. These off-target effects often become dose-limiting before severe EPS, which is why low potency means a different toxicity pattern rather than safer therapy.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING: Elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotic drugs have increased mortality, commonly from cardiovascular or infectious causes; first-generation antipsychotics is not approved for dementia-related psychosis. High potency predicts more EPS; low potency predicts more hypotension, sedation and anticholinergic toxicity. Both groups can cause tardive dyskinesia, NMS and QT-related dysrhythmia.

Adverse Effects

  • Haloperidol/fluphenazine: dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, prolactin elevation and NMS

  • Chlorpromazine: orthostasis, sedation, anticholinergic toxicity, weight gain and photosensitivity

  • All: tardive dyskinesia, QT risk, seizures, temperature dysregulation and blood dyscrasias

Contraindications

  • Severe CNS depression/coma and product hypersensitivity

  • Agent-specific Parkinson disease, marrow suppression, hepatic, cardiac or seizure concerns

  • Do not assume low-potency therapy is suitable when falls, ileus, glaucoma or urinary retention are present

Nursing Essentials

  • Assess orthostatic vitals and anticholinergic burden for low-potency drugs; perform structured EPS/AIMS surveillance for every drug, especially high-potency agents.

  • Acute painful dystonia is an emergency when the larynx or neck is involved; notify, protect the airway, and give the ordered anticholinergic treatment promptly.

Interactions

  • Metoclopramide and other D2 blockers add EPS/NMS risk.

  • Sedatives and antihypertensives magnify chlorpromazine hypotension and CNS depression.

  • QT-prolonging drugs and low potassium or magnesium increase torsades risk.

Key Labs

  • AIMS/EPS examination

  • Orthostatic blood pressure, ECG and electrolytes

  • CBC and liver tests when symptoms or the product plan warrant

Nclex Traps

  • High potency means lower milligram dose, not superior efficacy.

  • Akathisia is internal motor restlessness, not automatically worsening psychosis.

  • Low-potency drugs cause less EPS on average, not zero EPS.
