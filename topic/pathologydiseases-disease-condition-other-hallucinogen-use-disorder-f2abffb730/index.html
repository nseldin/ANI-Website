Plain Language

Other hallucinogen use disorder is a treatable illness in which repeated use of non-PCP hallucinogens—such as LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, or similar drugs—becomes hard to control and causes harm. It is not diagnosed from one psychedelic experience, a brief intoxication, or supervised participation in legitimate research or treatment.

Why It Matters

Hallucinogen effects can become dangerous through panic, psychosis, impaired judgment, trauma, overheating, seizures, or substance-specific toxicity, and street products may not contain what the patient expected. The disorder needs ongoing behavioral care, while acute intoxication, persistent perceptual symptoms, and a primary psychiatric illness require separate assessment.

Definition

Other hallucinogen use disorder is a problematic pattern of non-phencyclidine hallucinogen use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR uses at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months and a 10-feature framework because a hallucinogen withdrawal criterion is not included. Phencyclidine use disorder is a separate diagnosis, and intoxication, a substance-induced disorder, or a supervised therapeutic exposure is not automatically this use disorder.

Pathology

There is no single laboratory, brain image, or perceptual symptom that confirms the disorder. Hallucinogens act through different receptor systems and have different toxicities; repeated use may produce tolerance, craving, risky behavior, and impaired control, while acute harm often results from altered perception and judgment, panic, trauma, contaminants, hyperthermia, autonomic effects, or substance-specific complications.

Pathophysiology

  • Classic psychedelics such as LSD and psilocybin mainly alter serotonin signaling, while other drugs grouped in clinical conversation as hallucinogenic can have additional stimulant, empathogenic, or dissociative effects. They must not be treated as physiologically interchangeable.

  • Intoxication may cause intensified or distorted perception, altered sense of time or self, anxiety, panic, impaired judgment, enlarged pupils, tachycardia, sweating, tremor, or poor coordination. Severe agitation, psychosis, trauma, seizure, hyperthermia, or cardiovascular instability requires emergency care.

  • DSM-5-TR does not define an other-hallucinogen withdrawal syndrome. Fatigue, mood, sleep, or anxiety symptoms after use still require assessment, but a seizure-delirium detox protocol should not be invented unless another substance or medical condition explains it.

Etiology

Risk reflects frequency and context of exposure, drug identity and potency, rapid or polysubstance use, contaminated products, reward and coping patterns, trauma, psychiatric vulnerability, peer environment, and access to care. Experimental medical research does not establish safety for unsupervised products, doses, patients, or settings, and one difficult experience does not prove a chronic use disorder.

Risk Factors

  • Frequent or escalating use, repeated risky settings, unverified products, polysubstance exposure, and prior hallucinogen-related injury or psychiatric emergency

  • Personal or family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, severe anxiety, trauma, suicidal thinking, sleep deprivation, and poor social support

  • Cardiovascular disease, seizure disorder, pregnancy, serotonergic or sympathomimetic medicines, hot environments, prolonged exertion, and dehydration

  • Adolescence, impaired judgment, driving or heights, unsafe supervision, and drug use in coercive or exploitative settings

Signs Symptoms

  • Impaired control, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, substantial time obtaining or recovering, role failure, risky use, and continued use despite physical, psychiatric, relationship, or work harm

  • Intoxication with visual or sensory distortion, altered time or self-experience, anxiety or panic, suspiciousness, impaired judgment, enlarged pupils, tachycardia, sweating, tremor, or incoordination

  • Dangerous intoxication with severe agitation, psychosis, suicidal or impulsive behavior, trauma, hyperthermia, seizure, chest pain, severe hypertension, or collapse

  • Recurrent or persistent visual trails, afterimages, flashes, halos, altered size perception, or other perceptual symptoms after intoxication has ended require assessment for persisting perception disorder and other causes

Diagnostics

  • Identify the exact reported substance, source, dose if known, route, time, expected and unexpected effects, co-exposures, medications, setting, sleep, hydration, prior episodes, psychiatric history, function, and all use-disorder features during the same 12 months.

  • Confirm at least 2 qualifying DSM-5-TR features from the 10-feature framework. Do not add a withdrawal criterion, and do not merge PCP use disorder into this identity.

  • Assess intoxication, delirium, hallucinogen-induced psychosis or mood disorder, persisting perception symptoms, trauma, poisoning, and primary psychiatric or neurologic illness separately.

  • Routine toxicology may not detect LSD, psilocybin, many novel psychoactive substances, or contaminants. Treat the clinical syndrome and use poison or toxicology consultation rather than using a negative screen as reassurance.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses the use disorder. In acute toxicity, select glucose, electrolytes including sodium, renal and liver measures, creatine kinase, ECG, temperature, pregnancy testing, blood gas, toxicology, and imaging from the drug, symptoms, co-exposures, and protocol.

  • Check sodium and fluid history when MDMA or a similar product, excessive water intake, headache, confusion, vomiting, or seizure raises concern for hyponatremia; this is substance- and context-dependent, not a universal psychedelic effect.

  • Persistent perceptual, cognitive, or neurologic symptoms may require vision, neurologic, medication, migraine, seizure, and psychiatric evaluation rather than repeated nonspecific toxicology.

Assessment

First assess airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, cardiac rhythm, seizure, trauma, severe agitation, dangerous psychosis, suicide risk, and co-exposures. Move to a calm low-stimulation area when safe, identify the actual or suspected product, and repeatedly assess orientation and behavior. After stabilization, establish the chronic use pattern, functional harm, persistent symptoms, and treatment goals.

Differential

Differentiate intoxication from PCP or ketamine exposure, stimulant toxicity, cannabis or synthetic cannabinoid exposure, serotonin toxicity, anticholinergic poisoning, delirium, panic, mania, primary psychosis, migraine aura, seizure, stroke, and metabolic or infectious illness. Differentiate persisting perception disorder from ongoing intoxication, visual disease, neurologic illness, and ordinary brief afterimages.

Treatments

  • For the ongoing use disorder, use collaborative behavioral treatment that addresses triggers, craving, risky contexts, distorted expectations, trauma, co-occurring illness, and recovery support. No medication is FDA approved specifically for other hallucinogen use disorder.

  • For uncomplicated acute anxiety or perceptual distress, provide reassurance, a quiet low-stimulation environment, hydration appropriate to clinical status, observation, and a trusted support person when safe and desired.

  • For severe agitation, psychosis, seizure, hyperthermia, cardiovascular instability, or a suspected mixed or novel substance exposure, use monitored emergency and poison or toxicology-guided care; specific medicines are chosen from the actual toxidrome rather than the broad hallucinogen label.

  • Treat persistent psychosis, mood disorder, trauma, sleep disturbance, and persisting perceptual symptoms with specialist evaluation and condition-specific care.

Contraindications

  • Do not assume all hallucinogens have the same mechanism, withdrawal, interaction, or emergency treatment; verify the substance and co-exposures whenever possible.

  • Do not use restraint or a highly stimulating confrontation when verbal de-escalation and a safer low-stimulation approach are possible; agitation plus heat and struggle can worsen injury.

  • Do not encourage unsupervised psychedelic use based on early clinical research, and do not equate a regulated study protocol with an illicit or unverified product.

  • Do not diagnose hallucinogen withdrawal or prescribe a detoxification regimen when no recognized syndrome is present; investigate alcohol, sedative, stimulant, medical, or psychiatric causes.

Safety Considerations

  • Protect from traffic, heights, water, weapons, falls, assault, and unsafe driving while avoiding punitive or shaming interactions.

  • Monitor temperature, hydration, sodium-related symptoms, rhythm, blood pressure, mental status, and muscle injury according to the suspected drug; neither forced water intake nor universal fluid restriction is appropriate.

  • Assess suicidal intent, command hallucinations, panic, trauma, coercion, and consent. A frightening experience can lead to dangerous escape behavior even without a chronic use disorder.

  • Report persistent or recurrent perceptual symptoms, psychosis, severe insomnia, or functional decline after the acute effect ends for specialist assessment.

Nursing Priorities

  • Use calm, simple communication; reduce noise, light, crowding, and confrontation; maintain observation and protect the patient from accidental injury.

  • Document reported product, packaging or image if safely available, route, time, dose, source, co-use, medications, vital signs, temperature, neurologic and psychiatric findings, and changes over time.

  • Escalate seizure, hyperthermia, chest pain, severe hypertension, hyponatremia symptoms, delirium, trauma, dangerous psychosis, or suicide risk and contact Poison Help or toxicology when the exposure is uncertain.

  • At discharge, confirm sober safe transport, supervision when needed, urgent return symptoms, no driving, behavioral follow-up, and assessment for persistent perception or psychiatric symptoms.

Red Flags

  • Seizure, hyperthermia, severe agitation, delirium, chest pain, dangerous hypertension, arrhythmia, collapse, or focal neurologic deficit

  • Severe headache, vomiting, confusion, or seizure after MDMA-like exposure or excessive water intake, suggesting possible hyponatremia

  • Command hallucinations, suicidal or violent intent, dangerous escape behavior, inability to remain safe, or psychosis persisting after expected intoxication

  • Major trauma, suspected adulterant or mixed overdose, pregnancy with severe symptoms, or persistent neurologic or visual change

Complications

  • Falls, motor-vehicle injury, drowning, burns, assault, unsafe sexual exposure, and trauma during impaired perception or judgment

  • Panic, substance-induced psychosis or mood disorder, suicide, prolonged insomnia, and hallucinogen persisting perception disorder

  • Hyperthermia, dehydration, hyponatremia, seizure, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, cardiovascular injury, and organ failure with selected substances or co-exposures

  • Functional decline, relationship or occupational harm, exposure to adulterants, and missed primary psychiatric or neurologic illness

Prognosis

Many acute effects resolve with time and supportive care, but outcome depends on the actual substance, dose, trauma, complications, and psychiatric vulnerability. The use disorder can improve with behavioral treatment and reduced exposure; persistent psychosis or perceptual symptoms should not be dismissed as a normal flashback and may require prolonged follow-up.

Prevention

  • Use nonjudgmental education about uncertain product identity, dose, adulterants, mixing, unsafe environments, driving, overheating, and delayed help-seeking.

  • Screen early for psychosis or bipolar vulnerability, trauma, suicide risk, and co-occurring substance use, and provide evidence-based alternatives for the symptom the person is trying to manage.

  • Keep regulated clinical research or treatment within its approved screening, consent, monitoring, and follow-up system; do not generalize it to unsupervised use.

Patient Education

  • A use disorder is diagnosed from loss of control and harm over time, not from one psychedelic experience.

  • Call emergency services for seizure, overheating, chest pain, severe confusion, suicidal behavior, or inability to keep the person safe; bring product information if it can be obtained safely.

  • A negative routine drug screen does not prove the product was safe or absent because many hallucinogens and newer substances are not detected.

  • There is no standard hallucinogen withdrawal syndrome. Persistent visual changes, paranoia, or severe mood symptoms after the drug should be evaluated.

Special Populations

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate assessment of peers, coercion, school, driving, family safety, psychosis, trauma, and suicide risk, with caregiver involvement when safe.

  • Pregnancy and lactation require avoidance counseling and coordinated obstetric or pediatric assessment; research in nonpregnant adults cannot be generalized to fetal or infant safety.

  • Patients with psychosis or bipolar vulnerability, seizure disorder, cardiovascular disease, or serotonergic polypharmacy need a lower threshold for emergency and specialist evaluation.

  • People using substances in group, sexual, ceremonial, or research contexts need respectful assessment of consent, assault, infection exposure, cultural context, and the difference between supervised and unsupervised conditions.

Nclex Traps

  • Other hallucinogen use disorder excludes PCP use disorder and should not be expanded into one interchangeable hallucinogen class.

  • DSM-5-TR does not include an other-hallucinogen withdrawal criterion; do not invent a seizure-delirium detox syndrome.

  • A negative routine toxicology screen does not rule out LSD, psilocybin, novel substances, or contaminants.

  • Acute treatment is toxidrome- and complication-based; no medication is FDA approved for the chronic use disorder.
