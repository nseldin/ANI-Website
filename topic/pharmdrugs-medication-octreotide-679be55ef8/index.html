Used To Treat

Recurrent or anticipated sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia as an off-label toxicology treatment alongside dextrose, food when safe, and prolonged glucose observation. It does not neutralize injected insulin and does not replace immediate dextrose for a symptomatic low glucose.

Mechanism

Sulfonylureas close ATP-sensitive potassium channels in pancreatic beta cells, depolarizing the membrane, opening calcium channels, and releasing insulin even when glucose is already low. Dextrose raises serum glucose but can further drive that same beta-cell response. Octreotide activates somatostatin receptors, particularly those that couple to inhibitory G proteins, reducing calcium-dependent insulin release. By turning down endogenous insulin secretion, it decreases recurrent hypoglycemia and the amount of dextrose required. It cannot remove a sulfonylurea, reverse neuroglycopenic injury already sustained, or stop the action of insulin that was injected from outside the body.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity to octreotide is a contraindication. Bradyarrhythmia and interacting rate-slowing drugs require closer monitoring rather than assuming the toxicology benefit is risk-free.

  • Do not use octreotide as a substitute for dextrose in a currently symptomatic patient, and do not expect it to reverse exogenous-insulin overdose because there is no endogenous beta-cell secretion to suppress.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check bedside glucose immediately and repeatedly, but send a laboratory glucose when results and symptoms conflict. Give dextrose first for neuroglycopenia because octreotide prevents recurrence rather than supplying glucose to the brain.

  • After the patient is awake and can swallow safely, provide complex carbohydrate and protein as directed because sustained enteral substrate helps maintain glucose without the sharp insulin stimulus of repeated large dextrose boluses.

  • Use seizure, fall, and aspiration precautions; monitor mental status, ECG and heart rate because hypoglycemia itself can cause dysrhythmia or seizure and octreotide can cause bradycardia or conduction effects.

  • Secure all diabetes medications and identify the exact sulfonylurea, strength, formulation, amount, time, coingestants, kidney/liver disease, and whether a child had access because even one tablet can cause prolonged pediatric hypoglycemia.

Interactions

  • By suppressing both insulin and glucagon release, octreotide can unpredictably change requirements for insulin or other glucose-lowering drugs; it can also add to bradycardia or conduction slowing with beta blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, and other rate-slowing agents.

  • Octreotide changes gastrointestinal motility and secretory physiology, so it can alter oral-drug absorption, reduce cyclosporine exposure, and increase bromocriptine bioavailability; medication-specific follow-up matters because the direction and clinical consequence are not uniform.

Key Labs

  • Serial bedside and laboratory glucose, potassium, magnesium, creatinine, liver tests, and ECG. Consider insulin and C-peptide only when the diagnosis is unclear; treatment must not wait for send-out results.

  • Document every glucose value with timing of dextrose, octreotide, food, symptoms, and recurrence. A stable glucose during an active dextrose infusion is not proof that sulfonylurea effect has ended.

Nclex Traps

  • Dextrose treats the immediate low glucose but may trigger more sulfonylurea-driven insulin; octreotide breaks that feedback loop.

  • Octreotide is not an insulin antidote. Injected insulin continues to act even when pancreatic secretion is suppressed.

  • A normal glucose while dextrose is running does not establish recovery; watch after therapy is withdrawn.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children with possible sulfonylurea exposure

    Note

    A small tablet count can create a large weight-adjusted exposure in a child, and recurrent hypoglycemia can outlast an initial normal glucose because the secretagogue remains active; involve Poison Help early, use protocol-directed pediatric dosing, and observe beyond the last dextrose and octreotide effect.

  • Population

    Older adults and patients with renal or cardiac disease

    Note

    Sulfonylurea or active-metabolite clearance may be prolonged when kidney function is reduced, while baseline conduction disease and rate-slowing medicines reduce tolerance of octreotide-associated bradycardia; these mechanisms justify longer glucose observation and closer ECG/hemodynamic assessment.

  • Population

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Pregnancy-specific toxicology evidence is limited, but severe maternal hypoglycemia threatens maternal and fetal oxygen delivery, so immediate glucose rescue and specialist-guided recurrence prevention take priority while fetal assessment and subsequent dosing follow obstetric, poison-center, and product guidance.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): Hypersensitivity to octreotide is a contraindication. Bradyarrhythmia and interacting rate-slowing drugs require closer monitoring rather than assuming the toxicology benefit is risk-free. Seizure, coma, recurrent glucose below the treatment threshold, increasing dextrose requirement, dysrhythmia, or inability to maintain airway requires critical-care escalation and renewed toxicology guidance.

Class Pathway

  • Somatostatin receptor activation, especially SSTR2/SSTR5

  • Reduced calcium-dependent hormone exocytosis

  • Suppressed insulin, growth hormone, glucagon, and GI/pancreatic secretion

Description

Octreotide is a somatostatin analog that suppresses pancreatic insulin secretion. In sulfonylurea poisoning, it prevents the cycle in which dextrose corrects glucose briefly but also stimulates a sensitized beta cell to release still more insulin, causing another glucose crash. This makes octreotide a mechanistically targeted adjunct after the patient is rescued with dextrose. Its labeled uses include acromegaly and selected secretory tumors; sulfonylurea poisoning is an important OFF-LABEL use. A 2025 systematic review found variation in dose, route, and observation practice, so this card does not invent one universal regimen.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea, injection-site pain, bradycardia, conduction abnormalities, and either hypo- or hyperglycemia can occur. Gallstones and biliary problems are more characteristic of longer treatment but remain part of the drug's safety profile.

  • Suppressing insulin can overshoot into hyperglycemia, while stopping too early can uncover recurrent hypoglycemia. Both are detected by continued glucose surveillance rather than symptoms alone.

Administration Timing

  • Treat symptomatic hypoglycemia immediately with IV dextrose and airway support; do not wait for octreotide to act. Once sulfonylurea exposure is suspected or recurrent hypoglycemia emerges, call Poison Help and begin protocol-directed octreotide early enough to interrupt the dextrose-insulin-rebound cycle.

  • Use the route, interval, age/weight approach, and repeat duration directed by the current poison-center or institutional protocol. Published practice includes subcutaneous and IV approaches, but evidence does not establish a single best regimen for every patient.

  • Continue frequent glucose checks through an observation period after the last octreotide and dextrose because long-acting agents, renal impairment, extended-release formulations, or large ingestions can outlast treatment.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Seizure, coma, recurrent glucose below the treatment threshold, increasing dextrose requirement, dysrhythmia, or inability to maintain airway requires critical-care escalation and renewed toxicology guidance.

  • Rebound after octreotide ends suggests persistent absorption or impaired clearance and requires renewed treatment plus a longer observation clock; discharge should never be based on one normal glucose.

Evidence Limitations

  • Sulfonylurea poisoning is an OFF-LABEL octreotide use. The 2025 systematic review supports effectiveness and generally favorable safety but documents dosing and route variation and limited high-certainty comparative evidence.

  • Observation duration is exposure-specific. Long-acting products, renal dysfunction, pediatric ingestion, and recurrent events require individualized poison-center guidance.

Regulatory Status

OFF-LABEL for sulfonylurea poisoning. Octreotide injection is FDA approved for other indications; toxicology dosing and observation are poison-center or protocol directed.
