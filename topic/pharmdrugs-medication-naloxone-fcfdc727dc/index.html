Used To Treat

Known or suspected opioid overdose with respiratory or central nervous system depression. Naloxone buys time by reversing opioid effect, but it does not replace ventilation, CPR, emergency evaluation, or treatment of non-opioid causes of unresponsiveness.

Mechanism

Naloxone competes with opioids at mu and other opioid receptors, displacing agonist signaling so respiratory drive and airway protection can recover. Its receptor effect can wear off before a long-acting, high-potency, or repeatedly absorbed opioid clears; therefore recurrent sedation is possible even after an initially dramatic response. Partial agonists such as buprenorphine bind tightly, so ventilation and repeated antagonist assessment may be more important than expecting one device to produce complete awakening. The clinical goal is adequate breathing, not forced full arousal, because abrupt complete reversal can trigger severe withdrawal and sympathetic stress.

Boxed Warning

ACTIVE OVERDOSE WARNING: call 911, support breathing, and give the available naloxone product exactly as labeled. Use a new single-use device for each repeat and continue watching the patient because respiratory depression can recur after naloxone wears off.

Contraindications

  • Known hypersensitivity to naloxone or a product ingredient is the labeled contraindication because re-exposure can provoke another hypersensitivity reaction.

  • Opioid dependence, pregnancy, or chronic pain is not a reason to withhold emergency reversal when breathing is failing; use immediate risk-benefit judgment because untreated patient or maternal hypoxia is more dangerous, while abrupt withdrawal still requires monitoring.

  • Do not use naloxone response as proof that every coma is opioid-only or as a substitute for ventilation and emergency care because it does not reverse hypoglycemia, stroke, trauma, or non-opioid sedatives.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check breathing, pulse, oxygenation, responsiveness, exposure history, glucose, and co-ingestants while supporting the airway because naloxone corrects opioid receptor depression but not hypoglycemia, stroke, trauma, or sedative co-ingestion.

  • Document the exact product, strength, route, time, response, and every repeat because nominal milligrams and device volumes differ.

  • Place the breathing patient in a recovery position when appropriate and prepare suction and ventilation because vomiting and abrupt withdrawal can threaten the airway.

Interactions

  • Methadone, sustained-release opioids, large fentanyl or analog exposure with ongoing absorption, or body-packed drug can outlast naloxone, so initial improvement does not prevent recurrent respiratory depression.

  • Buprenorphine and pentazocine may be incompletely or gradually reversed because tight receptor binding or mixed agonist-antagonist behavior reduces the response to one naloxone dose.

  • Benzodiazepines, alcohol, xylazine, gabapentinoids, and other non-opioid depressants remain active because naloxone only antagonizes opioid receptors, so persistent sedation still needs airway support and a broader differential.

Key Labs

  • Do not wait for a toxicology result or opioid concentration before giving naloxone because prolonged hypoventilation causes hypoxic injury and bedside rescue is a clinical decision.

  • Trend respiratory rate, chest movement, pulse oximetry, and capnography when available, and obtain a blood gas when ventilation remains impaired because oxygen saturation alone can look acceptable while carbon dioxide rises.

  • Check point-of-care glucose and use ECG, electrolytes, renal and hepatic tests, toxicology studies, and complication-directed imaging as indicated because hypoglycemia, co-ingestants, organ failure, aspiration, trauma, or stroke can explain persistent coma or change recurrence risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Naloxone can wear off before the opioid, so improvement is not permission to leave the patient alone.

  • Do not compare 3, 4, 8, and 10 mg products as though their device volumes, routes, or instructions are interchangeable.

  • Buprenorphine/naloxone is a maintenance combination, not the same search intent as emergency naloxone.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Infants and children

    Note

    Use the labeled pediatric-capable product and airway support; sudden withdrawal can include seizures or excessive crying, and very small patients still need emergency monitoring.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal hypoxia threatens both patients, while abrupt withdrawal may stress the fetus; emergency reversal and obstetric assessment should not be delayed because restoring maternal oxygenation is the best immediate protection for the fetus.

  • Population

    Opioid dependence or chronic pain

    Note

    The lowest effective clinical reversal may reduce abrupt withdrawal, but lay rescue devices are given as labeled and adequate ventilation remains the immediate endpoint.

Description

Naloxone is a competitive opioid-receptor antagonist used when opioid effect is suppressing breathing or consciousness. Product identity matters because Narcan 4 mg/0.1 mL, Kloxxado 8 mg/0.1 mL, RiVive 3 mg/0.1 mL, Rextovy 4 mg/0.25 mL, Rezenopy 10 mg/0.11 mL nasal products and Zimhi 5 mg/0.5 mL intramuscular or subcutaneous injection are different single-use delivery systems rather than interchangeable volume instructions. A response supports opioid involvement, but absent response does not exclude fentanyl exposure, delayed absorption, hypoxia, or another emergency. Rextovy received FDA over-the-counter approval in June 2026; access and labeling differ across products.

Adverse Effects

  • Abrupt receptor displacement can precipitate acute withdrawal with agitation, pain, vomiting, diarrhea, hypertension, and tachycardia because a dependent nervous system suddenly loses opioid signaling.

  • Vomiting, agitation, and rapid return of pain can cause aspiration, falls, line removal, or injury, so protect the airway and use the breathing response rather than forced full arousal as the monitored endpoint.

  • Pulmonary edema, ventricular dysrhythmia, blood-pressure instability, seizure, or cardiac arrest have been reported after abrupt reversal, particularly with cardiovascular disease or other cardiotoxic drugs, so monitor high-risk patients because sympathetic stress and the preceding hypoxic event may both contribute.

Administration Timing

  • Give immediately for suspected opioid overdose while another person calls 911 and begins rescue breathing or CPR as indicated; do not wait for certainty because hypoxia causes injury quickly.

  • Use each nasal device or Zimhi syringe once. Follow the exact product label for repeat timing; marketed rescue products generally direct a new device every 2 to 3 minutes when the patient does not respond or relapses, and Rextovy directs reassessment after 2 minutes then every 2 to 3 minutes.

  • Continue observation and emergency transport after improvement because recurrent respiratory depression can occur when the opioid outlasts naloxone.

Escalation Recurrence

  • No response after initial and repeat labeled doses, recurrent slowing of breathing, pulmonary edema, severe agitation, chest pain, seizure, or persistent coma requires immediate advanced airway, toxicology, and emergency evaluation.

Evidence Limitations

  • A product strength does not predict clinical superiority across all opioids or patients; real-world response depends on exposure, route, time, ventilation, and repeat access.

Regulatory Status

Naloxone is FDA approved in multiple prescription and nonprescription U.S. rescue presentations; Rextovy received over-the-counter approval in June 2026. The selected product's labeling governs its device-specific route, strength, and repeat instructions.
