Definition

Cholinergic crisis develops when excess acetylcholine at muscarinic and nicotinic receptors causes secretions, bradycardia, weakness, fasciculation, and respiratory failure, sometimes from excess myasthenia medication. The characteristic pattern is worsening weakness with miosis, sweating, diarrhea, salivation, bronchorrhea, bradycardia, or fasciculations. Confirm or stage it with immediate airway, secretion, vital-capacity, and negative-inspiratory-force assessment; distinguish it from myasthenic crisis, sepsis, botulism, brainstem disease, and opioid toxicity by pupil, secretion, autonomic, exposure, and electrophysiologic context. The priority is to stop the offending cholinesterase inhibitor because aspiration can complicate Cholinergic crisis. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Altered level of consciousness, focal weakness, speech change, pupil change, seizure activity, sensory loss, severe headache, gait change, or new swallowing trouble.

  • Sudden change from baseline is a red flag even when vital signs are not dramatic.

Diagnostics

  • immediate airway, secretion, vital-capacity, and negative-inspiratory-force assessment

  • medication and pesticide exposure review

  • blood gas, electrocardiogram, electrolytes, and toxicology consultation without delaying treatment

  • Distinguish Cholinergic crisis from myasthenic crisis, sepsis, botulism, brainstem disease, and opioid toxicity by pupil, secretion, autonomic, exposure, and electrophysiologic context.

Labs

  • glucose must be checked with altered mental status

  • sodium abnormalities can worsen seizures/confusion

  • infection markers may rise with meningitis/encephalitis

Treatments

  • stop the offending cholinesterase inhibitor

  • support ventilation and suction secretions

  • use atropine for dangerous muscarinic effects and cause-specific toxicology treatment

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for increasing secretions, hypoxemia, bradycardia with poor perfusion, generalized weakness, seizure, or recurrent symptoms after improvement because rapid intubation and repeated antidote may be needed.

  • Protect staff with exposure-appropriate personal protective equipment and remove contaminated clothing before routine care because organophosphate or carbamate residue can poison rescuers and continue patient absorption.

  • Prioritize suction, oxygenation, and assisted ventilation because copious secretions, bronchospasm, and respiratory-muscle weakness are the immediate causes of death.

  • Place the patient on continuous rhythm and oxygen monitoring and assess pupils, secretions, fasciculations, strength, bowel activity, and consciousness because both muscarinic and nicotinic effects guide treatment response.

  • Administer ordered atropine to drying of dangerous respiratory secretions and prescribed oxime therapy when indicated because heart rate alone does not show adequate reversal of airway toxicity.

  • Trend blood gases, electrolytes, cholinesterase testing when ordered, temperature, urine output, and recurrent weakness to detect respiratory failure and intermediate syndrome.

Complications

  • aspiration can complicate Cholinergic crisis.

  • hypoxemic or hypercapnic respiratory failure

  • bradyarrhythmia can complicate Cholinergic crisis.

  • seizure can complicate Cholinergic crisis.

  • death can complicate Cholinergic crisis.

Red Flags

  • Copious secretions, severe bronchospasm, or falling oxygen saturation

  • Generalized weakness, shallow breathing, or rising carbon dioxide

  • Bradycardia with hypotension, seizure, or coma

  • Recurrent cholinergic findings after apparent stabilization

Patient Education

  • Explain that contaminated clothing and skin can keep releasing pesticide, so decontamination protects both the patient and everyone nearby.

  • Teach household members never to enter a contaminated area without trained help and to call emergency services and Poison Help after suspected exposure.

Nclex Traps

  • More pyridostigmine can worsen a cholinergic crisis

  • Respiratory weakness plus wet secretions points away from a purely myasthenic exacerbation
