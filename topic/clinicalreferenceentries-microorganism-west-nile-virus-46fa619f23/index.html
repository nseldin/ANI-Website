Definition

West Nile virus is a clinically important virus associated with West Nile fever, meningitis or encephalitis, and acute flaccid paralysis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    West Nile virus is an acellular, obligately intracellular virus with a positive sense ssrna genome and a enveloped particle. Envelope status helps explain environmental stability and susceptibility to routine controls, while genome type shapes replication and diagnostic targets. Capsid symmetry is left explicitly unknown in this bounded record rather than inferred without a reviewed taxonomic source.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Mosquito-bite prevention, vector control, screened blood supplies, and protecting high-risk adults from exposure reduce disease; no licensed human vaccine is available. The reviewed route description is arthropod borne transmission. The reviewed incubation estimate is 2 to 14 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    After mosquito-borne infection, West Nile virus may produce viremia and rarely invade the nervous system, injuring neurons and anterior horn cells. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    West Nile virus is associated with West Nile fever, meningitis or encephalitis, and acute flaccid paralysis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Serum or cerebrospinal-fluid IgM is commonly used for neuroinvasive disease, with confirmatory testing when flavivirus cross-reactivity is possible. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
