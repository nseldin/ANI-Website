Risk Factors

  • Diabetes treatment, poor intake, renal failure, alcohol use, or sepsis raises hypoglycemia risk because glucose supply and medication clearance can change.

  • Prior epilepsy, cortical scar, recent medication withdrawal, or witnessed convulsion raises seizure likelihood but does not exclude stroke.

  • Migraine history supports aura only when the current evolution matches the prior pattern; a new or atypical focal deficit still needs stroke assessment.

Signs Symptoms

  • Hypoglycemia may cause sweating, tremor, tachycardia, confusion, seizure, or focal deficit, but autonomic signs can be absent.

  • Todd paralysis follows a seizure and usually improves, while witnessed onset, tongue injury, incontinence, or postictal confusion may support it without proving it.

  • Bell palsy affects upper and lower peripheral facial movement, whereas cortical stroke often spares forehead strength but may add arm, speech, gaze, or sensory findings.

Diagnostics

  • Obtain bedside glucose immediately and correct a dangerous low value because prolonged neuroglycopenia injures brain.

  • Perform NCCT and vascular imaging when stroke remains possible because glucose correction, seizure history, or migraine does not rule out occlusion or hemorrhage.

  • Use history, medication reconciliation, focused neuro-vestibular exam, electrolytes, toxicology, infection testing, EEG, or MRI according to the remaining differential.

Labs

  • Bedside glucose is the fastest high-yield mimic test; repeat it after treatment because recurrence can occur.

  • Electrolytes, renal and liver function, CBC, oxygenation, and toxicology are selected because systemic disturbances can alter neurologic function.

  • The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline advises treating glucose below 60 mg/dL and avoids intensive 80-to-130 mg/dL control because severe hypoglycemia risk rises without improved stroke outcome; persistent hyperglycemia is generally managed with monitored protocol targets rather than aggressive normalization.

Treatments

  • Correct hypoglycemia promptly while continuing stroke assessment because reversal is urgent but dual pathology is possible.

  • Treat seizure, infection, intoxication, migraine, or other confirmed cause while avoiding premature closure on a plausible story.

  • Proceed with reperfusion evaluation when a disabling vascular deficit remains and hemorrhage is excluded because delay sacrifices penumbra.

Nursing Priorities

  • Check bedside glucose immediately while documenting exact onset and focal findings because hypoglycemia can imitate stroke and is rapidly treatable without delaying the stroke pathway.

  • Assess seizure and postictal state, migraine, intoxication, infection, electrolyte disorder, functional symptoms, tumor, and prior deficits because a mimic can coexist with stroke and one plausible alternative does not safely exclude occlusion.

  • Correct protocol-defined hypoglycemia and repeat glucose plus neurologic examination promptly because persistent focal deficit after normalization still requires emergency brain and vascular imaging.

  • Monitor glucose trend, NIH Stroke Scale findings, consciousness, pupils, vital signs, oxygenation, seizure activity, temperature, and response to targeted treatment because fluctuation may clarify the cause or expose evolving cerebral injury.

  • Escalate for glucose below the severe threshold with inability to swallow, persistent deficit after correction, recurrent hypoglycemia, NIH Stroke Scale increase, fever with meningismus, seizure lasting five minutes, or declining consciousness because stroke, infection, status, and metabolic brain injury remain emergencies.

Complications

  • Missed stroke leads to lost reperfusion opportunity and preventable disability.

  • Missed hypoglycemia causes seizure, coma, dysrhythmia, and neuronal injury.

  • Incorrectly labeling stroke as intoxication, migraine, psychiatric illness, or postictal weakness can create dangerous diagnostic bias.

Contraindications

  • Do not wait for all mimic labs before noncontrast CT and vascular assessment when stroke is plausible.

  • Do not give oral carbohydrate to a patient who has not passed swallowing assessment because aspiration risk may coexist.

  • Do not use one atypical feature to rule out posterior or nondominant stroke.

Red Flags

  • Severe hypoglycemia with inability to swallow, seizure, coma, or repeated recurrence

  • Persistent focal deficit after glucose normalizes or a rising NIH Stroke Scale score

  • Seizure lasting five minutes, recurrent seizures without recovery, or new Todd-like deficit

  • Fever with meningismus, declining consciousness, unequal pupils, or severe headache and vomiting

Patient Education

  • Do not assume an abnormal glucose fully explains one-sided weakness because stroke evaluation continues when focal symptoms persist after correction.

  • Call emergency services rather than treating at home when sudden focal symptoms occur because imaging is needed to separate stroke from its mimics.

Nclex Traps

  • Glucose is checked immediately, but a corrected value does not replace brain and vessel imaging when deficits persist.

  • Todd weakness and stroke can coexist because acute stroke can provoke seizure.

  • Never give food or drink to an unscreened acute-stroke patient simply to treat a suspected low glucose.

Stroke mimics and bedside glucose

Category

Acute neurologic differential diagnosis

Definition

Stroke mimics are nonvascular conditions that produce sudden focal or global neurologic symptoms resembling stroke. Bedside glucose is an immediate test because hypoglycemia can cause weakness, aphasia, confusion, seizure, or reduced consciousness and is rapidly reversible. Checking glucose does not replace stroke imaging: true stroke and glucose abnormality can coexist, and persistent focal deficits after correction still require the full emergency pathway.

Etiology

Common mimics include hypoglycemia, seizure with postictal Todd weakness, migraine aura, functional neurologic disorder, intoxication or medication effect, electrolyte disturbance, infection or encephalopathy, tumor, peripheral vestibular disease, Bell palsy, and demyelination. Hemorrhage, TIA, and ischemic stroke remain vascular diagnoses rather than mimics and require imaging distinction.

Pathology

Mimics disrupt neural function without acute arterial infarction. Low glucose deprives neurons of fuel; seizure causes excessive synchronized activity followed by transient regional suppression; migraine produces spreading cortical electrophysiologic change; toxic-metabolic disease alters distributed networks; peripheral lesions affect nerve, muscle, or vestibular pathways outside the brain.

Pathophysiology

Stroke usually causes a sudden negative deficit such as lost strength, language, field, or sensation, while migraine aura often spreads over minutes with positive visual or sensory phenomena and seizure may begin with jerking, gaze, automatisms, or altered awareness. These are tendencies, not safe rules. Posterior stroke can mimic vestibular disease, and seizure can be triggered by an acute stroke. The correct strategy is parallel stabilization, focused differential testing, glucose correction, and urgent imaging rather than waiting for a mimic to declare itself.
