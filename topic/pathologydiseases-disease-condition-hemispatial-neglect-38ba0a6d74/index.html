Risk Factors

  • Large right MCA and parietal lesions produce severe neglect because broad attention networks are disconnected.

  • Coexisting hemianopia, sensory loss, weakness, or impaired arousal magnifies functional disability.

  • Poor insight and impulsivity increase injury risk because the patient does not reliably compensate.

Signs Symptoms

  • The patient ignores food, people, grooming, limbs, or obstacles on the contralesional side.

  • Line bisection, cancellation, drawing, clock placement, and bilateral simultaneous stimulation reveal biased attention or extinction.

  • Repeated unsafe transfers, denial of paralysis, or collision with the neglected side suggests anosognosia and spatial inattention.

Diagnostics

  • Test fields one side at a time, then bilateral visual and sensory stimulation because extinction distinguishes attention failure from simple primary loss.

  • Observe eating, grooming, reading, wheelchair navigation, and body awareness because real-world behavior may reveal neglect better than a brief score.

  • Use lesion imaging and a full language, vision, strength, sensation, and arousal exam because neglect often coexists with other deficits.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses neglect; bedside attention testing and imaging do.

  • Glucose and metabolic assessment exclude global or fluctuating mimics when attention is impaired.

  • Vascular and cardiac cause testing follows the stroke mechanism rather than the side of neglect.

Treatments

  • Use visual scanning, limb activation, environmental anchors, prism or rehabilitation strategies as prescribed because repeated attention toward the affected side can improve compensation.

  • Provide close mobility and transfer supervision because poor awareness makes verbal safety agreement unreliable.

  • Treat the acute stroke and coexisting field, sensory, motor, or arousal problems because each reinforces neglect.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess visual, tactile, auditory, and personal-space inattention with extinction testing, line cancellation, grooming, eating, mobility, and safety tasks because neglect is an attention disorder that may persist despite intact primary sensation.

  • Differentiate neglect from hemianopia, aphasia, delirium, weakness, and poor vision because each problem changes communication, rehabilitation, environmental setup, and the meaning of an apparently missed stimulus.

  • Place essential items on the attended side during acute care, supervise mobility and meals, and introduce structured scanning toward the neglected side because awareness failure creates collision, fall, and malnutrition risks.

  • Monitor attention, NIH Stroke Scale components, meal completion, skin and limb protection, transfers, collisions, and rehabilitation response because fluctuating neglect may signal neurologic change and causes preventable functional injury.

  • Escalate for sudden-onset neglect, worsening inattention, new weakness or aphasia, NIH Stroke Scale increase, reduced consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, or seizure because acute stroke, hemorrhage, edema, or recurrent ischemia requires immediate reassessment.

Complications

  • Falls, collisions, burns, pressure injury, limb trauma, missed food, and medication errors occur because hazards are not consciously registered.

  • Rehabilitation is slower when the patient cannot attend to or believe in the affected side.

  • Driving and community navigation may remain unsafe despite recovered strength.

Contraindications

  • Do not call neglect a visual-field cut without separate field and extinction testing.

  • Do not assume denial of weakness is psychological resistance.

  • Do not leave the patient unattended based on verbal confidence when observed scanning and transfer safety remain poor.

Red Flags

  • New hemispatial neglect, extinction, gaze preference, or failure to recognize one side

  • Worsening neglect with new weakness, aphasia, visual loss, or NIH Stroke Scale increase

  • Reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, repeated vomiting, seizure, or posturing

  • Repeated collisions, falls, unrecognized limb injury, aspiration, or inability to complete meals

Patient Education

  • Neglect is reduced awareness, not stubbornness, so calm cueing and repeated scanning practice are more useful than criticism.

  • Turn the head and eyes systematically toward the affected side before eating or moving because hazards may not enter awareness automatically.

Nclex Traps

  • Neglect is attention failure; hemianopia is visual-field loss.

  • Right-hemisphere injury commonly causes severe left neglect.

  • Moving every object permanently to the intact side protects short-term access but does not replace graded rehabilitation toward the neglected side.

Name

Hemispatial neglect

Category

Stroke attention syndrome

Definition

Hemispatial neglect is failure to attend, orient, or respond to the contralateral side of space or the body despite adequate primary strength and sensation to do so. It most often follows right, nondominant parietal or frontoparietal injury and affects the left side. Neglect is an attention-network disorder rather than simple blindness: the patient may eat from one side of a plate, groom half the body, collide with obstacles, deny weakness, or miss a stimulus only when both sides are presented together.

Etiology

Acute nondominant MCA stroke is common, but hemorrhage, tumor, trauma, seizure, or neurodegeneration can disrupt the same attention network. Severity depends on lesion location, network disconnection, coexisting field loss, arousal, and insight.

Pathology

Right-lateralized frontoparietal and subcortical networks normally allocate attention broadly across both sides of space. The left hemisphere directs attention more strongly toward the right. Right-network injury therefore leaves an unopposed rightward bias and often produces more severe left neglect than the reverse pattern.

Pathophysiology

Primary visual pathways may carry the stimulus, but attentional selection fails, so the stimulus does not guide awareness or action. Extinction appears when a contralesional stimulus is detected alone but ignored during simultaneous bilateral stimulation. Personal neglect affects one's body, peripersonal neglect affects reaching space, and extrapersonal neglect affects the wider environment. Anosognosia can coexist because self-monitoring networks are injured, which makes the patient underestimate fall and mobility risk.
