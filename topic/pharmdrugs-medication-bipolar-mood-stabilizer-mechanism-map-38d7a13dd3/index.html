Used To Treat

Lithium for acute mania and maintenance; valproate for mania/mixed episodes and seizure indications; lamotrigine for bipolar I maintenance with particular value in delaying depressive recurrence, not acute mania.

Description

Mood stabilizer is a clinical role, not one receptor class. Lithium is a renally handled monovalent ion with a narrow therapeutic window and broad second-messenger effects. Valproate increases inhibitory GABA tone and dampens sodium/calcium-dependent repetitive firing, making it useful for acute mania and epilepsy but highly teratogenic and hepatotoxic. Lamotrigine stabilizes presynaptic sodium channels and reduces glutamate release; it is titrated slowly because rapid exposure, especially with valproate, increases life-threatening rash risk. Their different time courses and organ risks prevent casual substitution.

Mechanism

Lithium likely alters inositol and GSK-3 signaling, circadian and neuroplastic pathways, but its complete antimanic mechanism is unresolved; kidneys reabsorb it like sodium, explaining dehydration toxicity. Valproate increases GABA availability and prolongs sodium-channel inactivation while affecting T-type calcium currents, reducing pathologic excitability. Lamotrigine binds preferentially to inactivated voltage-gated sodium channels and reduces glutamate/aspartate release. Because acute mania requires rapid control, slow lamotrigine titration and its maintenance evidence make it the wrong rescue drug despite the mood-stabilizer label.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNINGS differ: lithium toxicity can occur near therapeutic concentrations; valproate carries fatal hepatotoxicity, fetal-risk and pancreatitis warnings; lamotrigine can cause fatal SJS/TEN, especially with valproate or rapid titration.

Adverse Effects

  • Lithium: tremor, GI effects, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, hypothyroidism and toxicity

  • Valproate: weight gain, tremor, thrombocytopenia, hyperammonemia, liver injury, pancreatitis and fetal harm

  • Lamotrigine: rash/SJS/TEN, dizziness, diplopia, ataxia and aseptic meningitis

Contraindications

  • Use each agent's renal, hepatic, pregnancy, mitochondrial, urea-cycle and hypersensitivity contraindications

  • Do not use valproate for migraine prevention in pregnancy

  • Do not restart lamotrigine at the previous full dose after a clinically meaningful interruption without retitration guidance

Nursing Essentials

  • Match phase to drug: acute mania, bipolar depression, and maintenance are not interchangeable targets.

  • Teach lithium hydration/salt consistency, valproate pregnancy and liver/pancreas warnings, and lamotrigine stop-and-call rash rules.

Interactions

  • Thiazides, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and NSAIDs commonly raise lithium.

  • Carbapenems can collapse valproate concentrations; valproate raises lamotrigine exposure and rash risk.

  • Estrogen-containing contraceptives lower lamotrigine concentrations and changes can destabilize levels.

Key Labs

  • Lithium trough, renal, thyroid and calcium monitoring

  • Valproate level, LFTs, CBC/platelets, pregnancy and ammonia when symptomatic

  • Lamotrigine clinical rash/titration and interaction review

Nclex Traps

  • Lamotrigine is not an acute-mania rescue simply because it is a mood stabilizer.

  • Normal lithium dose does not guarantee safe concentration after dehydration or a new NSAID.

  • Valproate hyperammonemic encephalopathy can occur even when liver tests are not dramatically abnormal.
