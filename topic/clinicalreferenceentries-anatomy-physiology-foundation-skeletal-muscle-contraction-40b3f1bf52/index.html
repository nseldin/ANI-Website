Summary

Skeletal muscle contracts when a somatic motor neuron releases acetylcholine, the muscle membrane generates an action potential, T-tubules carry depolarization inward, the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases calcium, and calcium moves tropomyosin away from actin binding sites through troponin. Myosin then uses ATP in repeated cross-bridge cycles to slide thin filaments and shorten sarcomeres.

Quick Answer

Electrical excitation opens access to the contractile machinery; calcium is the switch and ATP powers both cycling and relaxation. ATP binds myosin so it can detach from actin, is hydrolyzed to recock the head, and also powers calcium pumps that return calcium to the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Without ATP, cross-bridges cannot release normally.

Sections

    • Muscle organization

    • A whole skeletal muscle is wrapped by epimysium and divided into fascicles by perimysium; each muscle fiber is wrapped by endomysium. A fiber is a long multinucleated cell containing myofibrils. Myofibrils consist of repeating sarcomeres bounded by Z discs. Thin filaments contain actin, troponin, and tropomyosin; thick filaments contain myosin. T-tubules carry surface depolarization deep into the fiber near terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Tendons and connective-tissue layers transmit sarcomere force to bone. Structure therefore connects molecular shortening to joint movement rather than allowing fibers to contract in isolation.

    • Excitation-contraction sequence

    • A motor-neuron action potential reaches the terminal, calcium enters through voltage-gated channels, and acetylcholine is released. Acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate, producing an end-plate potential that triggers a muscle action potential. Depolarization travels along sarcolemma and T-tubules, activating voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptors mechanically linked to ryanodine receptors in skeletal-muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium floods cytosol, binds troponin C, shifts tropomyosin, and exposes actin. This ordered chain explains why nerve, junction, membrane, calcium store, regulatory proteins, or contractile proteins can each cause weakness.

    • Cross-bridge cycling and relaxation

    • An energized myosin head binds exposed actin and releases phosphate, producing a power stroke. ADP leaves, ATP binds myosin and causes detachment, and ATP hydrolysis recocks the head for another cycle. Cycling continues while cytosolic calcium and ATP are available. Acetylcholinesterase ends the junction signal, muscle membrane repolarizes, and SERCA calcium pumps return calcium to the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium dissociates from troponin, tropomyosin blocks actin, and tension falls. Rigor mortis occurs because ATP depletion prevents cross-bridge detachment and calcium resequestration after death.

    • Force regulation and relationships

    • One motor neuron and its fibers form a motor unit. Small motor units permit fine control; larger units generate gross force. Increasing firing frequency allows temporal summation, and recruiting more motor units increases force. Muscle length affects overlap between actin and myosin, so extreme shortening or stretch reduces active force. Eccentric contraction produces force while lengthening, concentric contraction while shortening, and isometric contraction without joint movement. Sensory feedback from muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs helps regulate length, force, posture, and protection. Muscle performance also depends on perfusion, oxygen, substrate, temperature, electrolytes, and central drive.

    • Energy and fatigue

    • Immediate stored ATP lasts briefly. Creatine phosphate rapidly regenerates ATP; anaerobic glycolysis supplies fast ATP with lactate and hydrogen-ion consequences; oxidative phosphorylation sustains longer activity when oxygen delivery and mitochondria are adequate. Fatigue is multifactorial and can arise from central drive, metabolite accumulation, impaired calcium handling, substrate depletion, membrane excitability changes, pain, or reduced perfusion. Lactate is not simply a waste toxin and can be reused as fuel. Training changes mitochondrial density, capillaries, enzymes, fiber characteristics, neuromuscular recruitment, and connective tissue rather than creating one universal 'stronger muscle' mechanism.

    • Failure consequences and disease links

    • Myasthenia gravis reduces effective postsynaptic acetylcholine-receptor signaling; botulinum toxin blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release; organophosphates inhibit acetylcholinesterase and cause persistent cholinergic stimulation followed by dysfunction. Muscular dystrophies disrupt structural proteins, inflammatory myopathies injure fibers, rhabdomyolysis releases intracellular contents, and electrolyte disorders change excitability. Upper motor-neuron disease alters tone and control, lower motor-neuron disease denervates fibers, and prolonged immobility causes atrophy. Malignant hyperthermia produces uncontrolled calcium release, sustained metabolism, heat, acidosis, rigidity, potassium release, and rhabdomyolysis.

    • Clinical connections, labs, and diagnostics

    • CK rises with skeletal-muscle injury but must be interpreted with symptoms, timing, exercise, procedures, and cardiac-specific markers. Potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, renal function, urine heme with few red cells, AST, and ECG findings help evaluate severe weakness or rhabdomyolysis. Electromyography and nerve-conduction studies localize nerve, junction, or muscle patterns; antibody tests support selected neuromuscular diagnoses; biopsy and genetic testing answer other questions. Neuromuscular blockers interrupt transmission for anesthesia, reversal agents restore signaling through mechanism-specific routes, dantrolene reduces pathologic skeletal-muscle calcium release, and local anesthetics block nerve sodium channels upstream.

    • Nursing assessment and actions

    • Assess symmetry, proximal versus distal pattern, tone, bulk, fasciculations, pain, fatigability, gait, swallowing, cough, voice, neck flexion, and respiratory effort. Distinguish true weakness from pain-limited movement or sedation. Trend vital capacity or negative inspiratory force when ordered for neuromuscular respiratory risk. Review electrolytes, medication timing, anesthesia exposure, toxins, immobility, infection, and kidney function. Protect against falls and aspiration, schedule activity with rest for fatigable disorders, maintain hydration and urine-output monitoring in muscle breakdown, and never give paralytic medication without assured ventilation and sedation because paralysis does not produce unconsciousness or analgesia.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Rapidly ascending or generalized weakness, weak cough, pooling secretions, dysphagia, nasal speech, shallow breathing, declining vital capacity, or rising carbon dioxide suggests respiratory-muscle failure. Hyperthermia, rigidity, tachycardia, acidosis, rising end-tidal CO2, hyperkalemia, or dark urine after anesthesia suggests malignant hyperthermia. Severe muscle pain or weakness with CK elevation, hyperkalemic ECG change, oliguria, or pigment urine requires urgent rhabdomyolysis care. New unilateral weakness may be stroke rather than muscle disease; preserve time-sensitive neurologic evaluation.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Calcium does not supply contraction energy; it exposes binding sites. ATP is required for relaxation as well as contraction. A paralytic stops movement but not awareness or pain. Normal CK does not exclude neuromuscular-junction or nerve disease. Weakness after repeated activity suggests a different mechanism from spastic weakness after an upper motor-neuron lesion. Potassium can be high in blood while total-body stores are depleted in some metabolic states. Do not encourage exercise through acute rhabdomyolysis, new neurologic weakness, or respiratory fatigue. Localize the failing step before assuming all weakness is deconditioning.
