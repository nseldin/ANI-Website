Used To Treat

Selected cisplatin and bendamustine extravasation under an oncology extravasation protocol. The 2025 ONS/ASCO guideline supports sodium thiosulfate for bendamustine and for greater than 20 mL of high-concentration cisplatin above 0.5 mg/mL; it is not a universal antidote for every chemotherapy infiltration.

Description

Sodium thiosulfate can be infiltrated locally around selected cisplatin or bendamustine extravasations to convert reactive drug into less tissue-injuring species. Stop the infusion, preserve the access device for aspiration, and avoid flushing because continued delivery or flushing drives more vesicant into tissue; identify the agent and follow its antidote and thermal-care pathway because extravasation treatments are chemistry specific. Evidence is low certainty, so exact preparation and technique belong to the current institutional protocol.

Mechanism

Thiosulfate supplies a nucleophilic sulfur group that reacts with electrophilic platinum or alkylating species and forms less-reactive products. Local delivery places the neutralizer near drug trapped in interstitial tissue, thereby reducing continued DNA and protein injury before necrosis expands. It cannot remove tissue already destroyed, and diffusion is limited by anatomy and delay; this explains why immediate aspiration, mapped assessment, timely antidote, and follow-up matter as much as the chemistry.

Administration Timing

  • Stop the antineoplastic infusion immediately, disconnect tubing, leave the catheter or needle in place initially for aspiration, and do not flush because flushing can drive vesicant deeper into tissue.

  • Identify the exact agent, concentration, estimated volume, route, site, time, and symptoms; activate the current oncology extravasation protocol before preparing local sodium thiosulfate.

  • Use only the protocol's dilution, infiltration pattern, needle changes, compress strategy, and follow-up schedule. Photograph and serially measure the site because injury can evolve after apparent early stability.

Nursing Essentials

  • Keep the access device available for aspiration until the protocol directs removal because withdrawing residual drug can reduce the burden that must be neutralized.

  • Mark erythema and swelling, record pain and neurovascular findings, and use reproducible measurements because trend—not one photograph—detects advancing injury.

  • Verify cisplatin concentration and estimated volume because guideline selection differs for large-volume high-concentration cisplatin, and a vague label of 'cisplatin leak' is insufficient.

  • Arrange wound, plastic-surgery, or other specialty review when progression, central-line extravasation, deep-tissue concern, or functional compromise is present because antidote cannot reverse necrosis already established.

Key Labs

  • Track site measurements, pain, perfusion, motor and sensory function, photographs, estimated extravasated volume, and drug concentration because no serum laboratory confirms local neutralization and the evolving examination is the primary injury signal.

  • Obtain CBC, creatinine, electrolytes, and agent-specific toxicity monitoring after substantial exposure because systemic therapy or absorption can add marrow, kidney, and metabolic injury; obtain CK and inflammatory studies when deep tissue injury is suspected because muscle necrosis or compartment physiology may be developing below a small skin lesion.

Adverse Effects

  • Local injection can cause pain, edema, irritation, or additional needle trauma; systemic absorption can contribute sodium load, nausea, hypotension, or hypersensitivity, although local rescue experience is limited.

  • Treatment failure may present as delayed blistering, ulceration, necrosis, neuropathy, impaired range of motion, infection, or chronic functional loss.

Contraindications

  • Do not use this focused pathway for an unidentified agent or a drug assigned to dexrazoxane, hyaluronidase, DMSO, phentolamine, or supportive care because each extravasated agent has different tissue chemistry and the wrong antidote or compress can worsen injury or waste the rescue window.

  • Hypersensitivity and inability to deliver local treatment safely require expert risk-benefit planning; do not improvise concentration or inject into compromised vascular structures.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Increasing pain, swelling, blistering, pallor, delayed capillary refill, sensory change, weakness, ulceration, fever, or suspected compartment syndrome requires urgent surgical or wound-specialty escalation.

  • Central venous access-device extravasation, uncertain anatomic spread, or a large exposure requires early imaging or specialty planning rather than reassurance from a small skin finding.

Interactions

  • Agent-specific compresses and antidotes are not interchangeable. Combining incompatible warm/cold strategies or substituting DMSO, dexrazoxane, or hyaluronidase can worsen injury or delay the correct rescue.

  • PEDMARK and cyanide sodium-thiosulfate regimens use different products, routes, doses, and purposes; no dosing or administration instruction should be copied between these identities.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Small limb compartments and limited ability to describe pain can allow proportionally important injury to progress; pediatric evidence is limited and early specialty review is prudent.

  • Population

    Patients with neuropathy, impaired communication, or poor perfusion

    Note

    Pain and sensory warning signals may be muted while ischemic or necrotic injury progresses, so objective serial neurovascular assessment is essential.

  • Population

    Pregnancy, lactation, and medically fragile oncology patients

    Note

    Local rescue evidence is limited, but untreated vesicant injury can be severe; choose therapy through the oncology, pharmacy, and specialty protocol rather than withholding or improvising.

Evidence Limitations

  • The ONS/ASCO recommendation is strong because morbidity can be severe, but certainty of evidence for extravasation antidotes is very low and much literature is observational.

  • The 2025 guideline addresses adult antineoplastic extravasation; pediatric use and uncommon agents require individualized oncology-pharmacy and toxicology guidance.

Nclex Traps

  • First stop the infusion and aspirate; do not flush an extravasated vesicant into more tissue.

  • Sodium thiosulfate is agent specific, not a universal chemotherapy-leak antidote.

  • A normal laboratory panel does not exclude evolving local necrosis.

Regulatory Status

Guideline-directed OFF-LABEL local rescue for selected antineoplastic extravasations; not an FDA-labeled extravasation indication.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): extravasation is time-sensitive. Stop infusion, do not flush, preserve access for aspiration, identify the agent and concentration, and activate the oncology protocol immediately; delayed or wrong-agent treatment can permit irreversible necrosis.

Boxed Warning Specificity
