Used To Treat

Strongly suspected or confirmed cyanide poisoning from fire smoke, industrial exposure, salts, or cyanogenic compounds. Hydroxocobalamin is a specific binding antidote; sodium thiosulfate supports sulfur-dependent detoxification, while nitrite-induced methemoglobinemia is a context-limited strategy that can worsen oxygen carriage in smoke or carbon-monoxide coexposure.

Description

Cyanide is a mitochondrial cytochrome-c-oxidase inhibitor that blocks cellular oxygen use, so a patient can be profoundly hypoxic at the tissue level despite oxygenated blood. Collapse, altered consciousness, seizure, hypotension, severe lactic acidosis, soot, and enclosed-space fire exposure raise suspicion, but no single lactate or smell is diagnostic and a cyanide concentration usually returns too late. Remove the patient and rescuers from exposure, decontaminate when material remains on skin or clothing, provide 100% oxygen and airway or burn care, and give antidote promptly when the clinical probability and severity justify it. Hydroxocobalamin is often favored in smoke inhalation because it binds cyanide without intentionally reducing hemoglobin oxygen capacity. Nitrites deliberately create methemoglobin to bind cyanide and can be hazardous when carbon monoxide or smoke already compromises oxygen delivery.

Mechanism

Cyanide binds ferric iron in cytochrome c oxidase, halting mitochondrial electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation. Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism, lactate rises, venous oxygen extraction falls, and brain and heart fail rapidly. Hydroxocobalamin directly binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin, which is excreted. Thiosulfate donates sulfur to rhodanese and related pathways that convert cyanide to thiocyanate. Nitrites oxidize hemoglobin to methemoglobin, providing an alternative ferric binding site, but that benefit trades away functional hemoglobin and can compound carbon-monoxide and smoke hypoxia.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911, protect responders, remove the patient from the source, strip and wash external contamination as indicated, and begin 100% oxygen, airway, ventilation, circulation, seizure, and burn care. Avoid mouth-to-mouth exposure to contaminated vomit or off-gassing material.

  • Give hydroxocobalamin promptly under the exact product label when severe suspected cyanide poisoning justifies empiric treatment; do not delay for a cyanide concentration. Use sodium thiosulfate or nitrites only under poison-center and toxicology guidance with the exposure source, oxygen carriage, and smoke or carbon-monoxide coexposure explicitly considered.

  • Escalate persistent shock or arrest to advanced resuscitation and an ECMO-capable center when feasible. Antidote does not replace airway edema surveillance, bronchoscopy or burn care, carbon-monoxide oxygen therapy, or trauma management.

Nursing Essentials

  • Document fire enclosure, duration, soot in mouth or nose, altered mental status, hypotension, lactate and acidosis, industrial chemicals, ingestion source, and co-victims. Bitter-almond odor is insensitive and genetically undetectable by many people.

  • Hydroxocobalamin turns skin, plasma, and urine dark red and can interfere with colorimetric laboratory tests, co-oximetry, pulse oximetry, and some hemodialysis blood-leak detectors. Draw critical labs first only if that causes no antidote delay, and notify laboratory and dialysis teams after administration.

  • Track airway swelling, bronchoscopy findings when indicated, carboxyhemoglobin, ECG, blood pressure, lactate, pH, neurologic status, urine output, burns, and compartment or trauma injuries because deterioration may reflect evolving airway edema, carbon-monoxide exposure, cellular hypoxia, burn shock, or occult trauma rather than one toxin alone.

Key Labs

  • Blood gas, lactate, electrolytes, glucose, ECG, carboxyhemoglobin and methemoglobin by co-oximetry, renal and hepatic studies, and cyanide level if available without delaying treatment. Interpret lactate with shock, seizure, epinephrine, and fire context.

  • A normal arterial oxygen tension does not exclude cyanide because dissolved oxygen can be present while mitochondria cannot use it. Venous hyperoxia is suggestive but not specific.

Adverse Effects

  • Cyanide can cause headache, confusion, seizure, coma, severe lactic acidosis, hypotension, pulmonary edema, dysrhythmia, arrest, and death.

  • Hydroxocobalamin can cause transient hypertension, headache, nausea, erythema, chromaturia, allergy, and extensive test interference. Nitrites can cause hypotension and dangerous methemoglobinemia; thiosulfate can cause nausea, hypotension, and osmotic or sodium burden.

Contraindications

  • Do not wait for a cyanide level in a crashing high-probability patient. Do not use nitrites reflexively in enclosed-space smoke inhalation or carbon-monoxide coexposure because induced methemoglobin can further reduce oxygen carriage.

  • Hydroxocobalamin hypersensitivity requires risk-benefit judgment, but there is no absolute delay-worthy contraindication in life-threatening suspected cyanide poisoning. Red discoloration is expected and not proof of hemolysis or bleeding.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent lactate or acidosis, shock, seizure, coma, dysrhythmia, airway edema, severe burn, or arrest after antidote requires ICU, burn and toxicology teams, and advanced circulatory support consideration. Reassess the diagnosis and dose or exposure source rather than assuming one antidote dose guarantees detoxification.

Interactions

  • Carbon monoxide coexposure lowers oxygen carrying and unloading; this favors hydroxocobalamin over intentional methemoglobin formation in many smoke patients.

  • Hydroxocobalamin can interfere with dialysis machines and laboratory assays and is physically incompatible with selected drugs or antidotes in the same line; follow the product label and use separate access or flushing as directed.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Use measured weight and the exact antidote label; airway edema and small circulating volume magnify both smoke injury and dosing error.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Empiric antidote and oxygen should not be delayed because untreated maternal cellular hypoxia can rapidly kill both pregnant patient and fetus; arrange obstetric monitoring in parallel.

  • Population

    Smoke-inhalation patients

    Note

    Carbon monoxide, thermal airway injury, particulate lung injury, trauma, and cyanide can coexist; a single antidote cannot treat the whole fire exposure.

Evidence Limitations

  • AHA 2025 recommendations rely on limited observational, case, and mechanistic evidence because randomized trials are impractical. Empiric thresholds and combination choices vary by exposure context; no single lactate value proves cyanide poisoning.

Nclex Traps

  • Cyanide prevents oxygen use; PaO2 can be normal.

  • Hydroxocobalamin binds cyanide without intentionally creating methemoglobin.

  • Nitrites can worsen oxygen delivery in carbon-monoxide or smoke coexposure.

Regulatory Status

Hydroxocobalamin and the sodium-nitrite/sodium-thiosulfate kit are FDA-approved cyanide antidote products with product-specific labeling. Empiric selection and combination in smoke inhalation remain clinical toxicology decisions.

Boxed Warning

CYANIDE/SMOKE WARNING: collapse, seizure, severe shock, or lactic acidosis after enclosed-space fire or cyanide exposure requires 911, 100% oxygen, airway and burn care, and prompt antidote assessment. Do not wait for a cyanide level because it usually returns too late to guide lifesaving treatment, and do not use nitrite-induced methemoglobinemia reflexively because carbon monoxide and methemoglobin can compound the loss of oxygen-carrying capacity.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
