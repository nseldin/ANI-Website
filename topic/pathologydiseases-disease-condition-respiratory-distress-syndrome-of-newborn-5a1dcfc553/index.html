Definition

Respiratory distress syndrome of newborn is surfactant deficiency in premature lungs causing alveolar collapse, low compliance, shunting, and hypoxemia. Grunting, retractions, nasal flaring, tachypnea, and rising oxygen need are classic.

Pathology

Respiratory distress syndrome of newborn is surfactant deficiency in premature lungs causing alveolar collapse, low compliance, shunting, and hypoxemia. Grunting, retractions, nasal flaring, tachypnea, and rising oxygen need are classic.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • maternal vital signs

  • fetal status when pregnant

  • bleeding assessment

  • CBC

  • urine protein when hypertensive

  • ultrasound or pelvic exam as indicated

Labs

  • platelets and LFTs matter in HELLP

  • urine protein supports preeclampsia but severe features can diagnose without it

  • glucose matters in gestational diabetes and newborn hypoglycemia

Treatments

  • stabilize mother first

  • seizure precautions and magnesium sulfate when indicated

  • control bleeding and blood pressure

  • prepare delivery or newborn support when indicated

Nursing Priorities

  • Maintain a neutral thermal environment because cold stress increases oxygen and glucose consumption in a premature newborn with limited respiratory reserve.

  • Apply prescribed continuous positive airway pressure or ventilation because positive pressure helps keep surfactant-deficient alveoli open.

  • Administer ordered surfactant and reassess oxygen and pressure needs because improving surface tension can rapidly change ventilator requirements.

  • Monitor respiratory rate, grunting, retractions, apnea, oxygen saturation target, blood gases, temperature, glucose, and perfusion to detect fatigue and treatment complications.

  • Escalate immediately for recurrent apnea, rising carbon dioxide, severe retractions, rapidly increasing oxygen need, hypotension, or air-leak signs because intubation or chest decompression may be required.

Complications

  • acute respiratory failure

  • hypoxemia

  • hypercapnia

  • aspiration

  • sepsis or pulmonary hypertension depending on cause

Red Flags

  • Recurrent apnea or rapidly increasing oxygen need

  • Rising carbon dioxide with respiratory fatigue

  • Hypotension or worsening peripheral perfusion

  • Sudden deterioration suggesting pneumothorax

Patient Education

  • Explain that immature lungs lack enough surfactant to stay open, which is why pressure support and replacement surfactant may be needed.

  • Teach caregivers how temperature control, feeding support, infection prevention, and developmental care aid recovery.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal-looking oxygen number can lag behind fatigue; increased work of breathing and mental-status change can be the earlier danger cues.
