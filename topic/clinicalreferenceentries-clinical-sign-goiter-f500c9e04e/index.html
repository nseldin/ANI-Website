Definition

Goiter means the thyroid gland in the lower front of the neck is enlarged. The enlargement may be smooth or nodular, and it does not reveal whether thyroid hormone is high, low, or normal.

Plain Language

A goiter is an enlarged thyroid gland that may look or feel like swelling at the lower front of the neck.

Why It Matters

A goiter can be caused by common benign thyroid conditions, but it still needs thyroid-function and structural evaluation. Rapid growth, a hard fixed area, abnormal lymph nodes, hoarseness, trouble swallowing, stridor, or breathing difficulty can signal compression or a serious thyroid disorder and needs prompt assessment.

Associated Conditions

  • diffuse or nodular thyroid enlargement from iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, benign nodules, inflammation, medication effects, congenital or physiologic demand, or less commonly malignancy

Assessment Method

Inspect the lower anterior neck from the front and side, ask the patient to swallow, and palpate gently for gland size, symmetry, nodules, tenderness, consistency, mobility, lower border, tracheal position, cervical nodes, bruit when indicated, and breathing, swallowing, or voice symptoms.

Positive Finding

The thyroid is visibly or palpably enlarged diffusely or nodularly and usually rises with swallowing; asymmetry, tenderness, a bruit, or compressive findings may accompany it.

Mechanism

Thyroid follicles enlarge or multiply in response to trophic stimulation, autoimmune activation, hormone-synthesis disturbance, nodular growth, inflammation, infiltration, or tumor, producing increased gland volume.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Goiter finding raises concern for diffuse or nodular thyroid enlargement from iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, benign nodules, inflammation, medication effects, congenital or physiologic demand, or less commonly malignancy because thyroid follicles enlarge or multiply in response to trophic stimulation, autoimmune activation, hormone-synthesis disturbance, nodular growth, inflammation, infiltration, or tumor, producing increased gland volume. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Goiter does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Goiter to focus evaluation of diffuse or nodular thyroid enlargement from iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, benign nodules, inflammation, medication effects, congenital or physiologic demand, or less commonly malignancy and the reviewed alternatives: thyroglossal duct cyst, cervical lymphadenopathy, neck lipoma, laryngeal prominence. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect the lower anterior neck from the front and side, ask the patient to swallow, and palpate gently for gland size, symmetry, nodules, tenderness, consistency, mobility, lower border, tracheal position, cervical nodes, bruit when indicated, and breathing, swallowing, or voice symptoms. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Goiter as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Neck habitus, strap muscles, normal variants, examiner technique, and retrosternal extension affect palpation; a normal examination does not exclude a thyroid nodule or substernal goiter. Therefore, Goiter cannot confirm or exclude diffuse or nodular thyroid enlargement from iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, benign nodules, inflammation, medication effects, congenital or physiologic demand, or less commonly malignancy by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • thyroglossal duct cyst

  • cervical lymphadenopathy

  • neck lipoma

  • laryngeal prominence

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as thyroglossal duct cyst, cervical lymphadenopathy, neck lipoma, laryngeal prominence.

False Negative Situations

Goiter may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of diffuse or nodular thyroid enlargement from iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, benign nodules, inflammation, medication effects, congenital or physiologic demand, or less commonly malignancy.

Modern Status

Safety Precautions

Observe Goiter with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect the lower anterior neck from the front and side, ask the patient to swallow, and palpate gently for gland size, symmetry, nodules, tenderness, consistency, mobility, lower border, tracheal position, cervical nodes, bruit when indicated, and breathing, swallowing, or voice symptoms. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not repeatedly compress a tender or highly vascular gland; rapidly enlarging swelling, stridor, dyspnea, dysphagia, hoarseness, facial congestion, fixed hard tissue, or abnormal cervical nodes requires prompt evaluation.
