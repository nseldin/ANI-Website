Definition

Finger-nose dysmetria is a bedside coordination sign. It is a clue to cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction. A positive finding means the finger overshoots, undershoots, or develops terminal intention tremor.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Finger-nose dysmetria means the finger overshoots, undershoots, or develops terminal intention tremor.

Why It Matters

The observed Finger-nose dysmetria finding can signal cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction. Cerebellar prediction and error correction fail to scale movement amplitude. Finger-nose dysmetria cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction

Assessment Method

Ask the patient to alternate between touching their nose and the examiner's moving finger.

Positive Finding

The finger overshoots, undershoots, or develops terminal intention tremor.

Mechanism

Cerebellar prediction and error correction fail to scale movement amplitude.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Finger-nose dysmetria finding raises concern for cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction because cerebellar prediction and error correction fail to scale movement amplitude. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Finger-nose dysmetria does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Finger-nose dysmetria to focus evaluation of cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: sensory neuropathy, ocular motor deficit, functional disorder. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask the patient to alternate between touching their nose and the examiner's moving finger. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Finger-nose dysmetria as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Visual loss, proprioceptive loss, and motor weakness can impair performance. Therefore, Finger-nose dysmetria cannot confirm or exclude cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • sensory neuropathy

  • ocular motor deficit

  • functional disorder

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as sensory neuropathy, ocular motor deficit, functional disorder.

False Negative Situations

Finger-nose dysmetria may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction.

Modern Status

Current localization adjunct. When performed as described, Finger-nose dysmetria can adjust concern for cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Finger-nose dysmetria: Visual loss, proprioceptive loss, and motor weakness can impair performance.

Safety Precautions

Observe Finger-nose dysmetria with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Ask the patient to alternate between touching their nose and the examiner's moving finger. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Move the target and compare sides; touching only a fixed nose is insufficient.

Nursing Relevance

Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: Ask the patient to alternate between touching their nose and the examiner's moving finger. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.
