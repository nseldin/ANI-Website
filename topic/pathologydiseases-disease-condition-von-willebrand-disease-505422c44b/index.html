Pathology

Von Willebrand disease develops when deficient or dysfunctional von Willebrand factor impairs platelet adhesion and may lower factor VIII stability. The characteristic pattern is lifelong nose, gum, menstrual, postpartum, dental, or surgical bleeding with easy bruising rather than isolated deep hemarthrosis. Confirm or stage it with von Willebrand antigen, activity, factor VIII, and blood type context with repeat testing when stress or hormones may distort results; distinguish it from platelet disorder, hemophilia A, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, medication effect, and connective-tissue bleeding by pattern and assays. The priority is to use desmopressin only when subtype and prior response support it because major surgical or postpartum hemorrhage can follow if the process advances. Mechanistically, deficient or dysfunctional von Willebrand factor impairs platelet adhesion and may lower factor VIII stability.

Etiology

Causes vary by condition: nutritional deficiency, autoimmune destruction, inherited disorders, marrow failure, malignancy, medication effect, infection, or coagulation activation.

Signs Symptoms

  • fatigue

  • pallor

  • bleeding/bruising

  • infection signs

  • bone pain

  • lymphadenopathy

  • dyspnea

  • clotting symptoms

Diagnostics

  • von Willebrand antigen, activity, factor VIII, and blood type context with repeat testing when stress or hormones may distort results

  • subtype testing for abnormal ratios or severe disease

  • CBC and iron studies for chronic blood loss

  • Distinguish Von Willebrand disease from platelet disorder, hemophilia A, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, medication effect, and connective-tissue bleeding by pattern and assays.

Treatments

  • use desmopressin only when subtype and prior response support it

  • give von Willebrand factor replacement for major bleeding or procedures

  • use antifibrinolytic and hormonal approaches for mucosal or menstrual bleeding when appropriate

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for head injury or neurologic change, airway or neck bleeding, hematemesis or melena, hemodynamic decline, uncontrolled procedural or postpartum hemorrhage, rapidly expanding swelling, or confusion or seizure after desmopressin because intracranial, airway, major internal bleeding, or acute hyponatremia is an emergency.

  • Confirm the von Willebrand disease subtype, baseline and recent bleeding pattern, prior desmopressin or concentrate response, menstrual and obstetric history, current medicines, and the planned procedure because treatment differs by subtype, bleeding site, and documented response.

  • Quantify mucosal, menstrual, gastrointestinal, postpartum, wound, or procedure-related bleeding and trend hemodynamics, blood counts, iron status, and ordered von Willebrand factor and factor VIII measures because visible blood alone can underestimate cumulative loss and hemostatic response.

  • Administer prescribed desmopressin, von Willebrand factor concentrate, or antifibrinolytic therapy and document timing and response because each treatment addresses a different mechanism and not every subtype responds safely to desmopressin.

  • When desmopressin is used, follow the prescribed fluid plan and monitor sodium, headache, nausea, confusion, and seizure risk; avoid aspirin and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs unless specifically directed because water retention and platelet inhibition create preventable complications.

Complications

  • major surgical or postpartum hemorrhage

  • iron-deficiency anemia can complicate Von Willebrand disease.

  • treatment hyponatremia can complicate Von Willebrand disease.

  • thrombosis during replacement can complicate Von Willebrand disease.

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to carry the subtype, effective prior treatments, desmopressin-response status, and hematology contact because emergency and procedure teams need more detail than the diagnosis name alone.

  • Explain that heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnancy, delivery, dental work, and surgery each need advance planning because the safest hemostatic strategy changes with the site and duration of bleeding risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Normal screening PT and aPTT do not exclude von Willebrand disease

  • Desmopressin is unsafe or ineffective in some subtypes and requires fluid and sodium precautions

Red Flags

  • Head injury, severe headache, vomiting, confusion, seizure, or focal neurologic change

  • Tongue or neck swelling, voice change, dysphagia, dyspnea, or airway bleeding

  • Hematemesis, melena, syncope, rapidly falling hemoglobin, or hemodynamic instability

  • Uncontrolled surgical or postpartum bleeding, expanding hematoma, or symptoms after desmopressin
