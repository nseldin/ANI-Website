Used To Treat

Antenatal acceleration of fetal lung, brain, and gastrointestinal maturation when preterm birth is likely within the guideline-defined window; formulation-specific treatment of inflammatory, allergic, rheumatologic, dermatologic, endocrine, and other corticosteroid-responsive disorders.

Mechanism

Betamethasone diffuses across cell membranes and binds cytosolic glucocorticoid receptors. The complex dissociates chaperones, enters the nucleus, binds glucocorticoid-response elements, and recruits coactivators or corepressors. Anti-inflammatory effects include increased lipocortin/annexin A1, reduced phospholipase A2 products, lower COX-2 and cytokine transcription, and altered leukocyte trafficking. In fetal type II pneumocytes, glucocorticoid signaling increases surfactant proteins and phospholipid synthesis, promotes lamellar-body maturation, thins alveolar septa, and increases epithelial sodium-channel and sodium-potassium-ATPase expression so lung fluid is absorbed at birth. Broader transcription accelerates vascular, gut, and brain maturation. Hyperglycemia follows increased gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Betamethasone products have no current U.S. boxed warning, but systemic corticosteroids can suppress immunity, mask infection, worsen hyperglycemia, cause psychiatric symptoms, increase GI and bone risk, and suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis with repeated or prolonged exposure. Antenatal courses should follow current gestational-age and repeat-course guidance rather than being given every time contractions recur. Maternal diabetes requires a proactive glucose plan, and exposed preterm or low-birth-weight neonates should be monitored for hypoglycemia, especially when delivery follows soon after dosing.

Contraindications

  • Systemic fungal infection and serious hypersensitivity; live vaccines and active untreated infection require product- and exposure-specific review.

  • Antenatal use requires a credible risk of preterm delivery in the recommended window; do not give repeated courses by habit without reassessing actual delivery risk and cumulative exposure.

Nursing Essentials

  • Document gestational age, first and second dose times, expected delivery window, previous courses, membrane/infection status, and maternal diabetes plan. This prevents duplicate courses during transfer.

  • Monitor glucose closely enough for the patient's diabetes status and steroid response. Do not interpret steroid leukocytosis alone as infection or use it to dismiss infection signs.

Interactions

  • CYP3A inducers can lower and inhibitors can raise systemic corticosteroid exposure; other immunosuppressants increase infection risk.

  • Diuretics or beta agonists can add potassium loss, NSAIDs increase GI injury, and vaccines or glucose-lowering therapy may require adjustment.

Key Labs

  • Maternal glucose, infection assessment, electrolytes and blood pressure by risk; no fetal surfactant laboratory level is used to titrate a standard course

  • Dose timing, fetal status, delivery timing, maternal mental and infectious symptoms, and neonatal glucose, respiratory status and gestational complications

Nclex Traps

  • Betamethasone prepares fetal organs; it does not directly stop labor. A tocolytic may be used only to create time for the steroid effect when delay is safe.

  • The steroid raises maternal glucose because glucocorticoid signaling increases hepatic glucose output and insulin resistance, so diabetes management must be anticipated rather than reactive.

Description

Betamethasone is a potent long-acting glucocorticoid that activates intracellular glucocorticoid receptors to change gene transcription, suppress inflammation, and accelerate fetal surfactant and organ maturation with minimal mineralocorticoid activity. In threatened preterm birth, maternal intramuscular dosing crosses the placenta and increases lung-fluid absorption, reducing respiratory distress and several complications of prematurity. It does not stop contractions and should not be confused with a tocolytic. The course is timed because benefit is greatest when birth occurs after enough transcriptional response has developed but within the period of expected effect. Maternal glucose can rise, infection can be masked, and neonatal hypoglycemia is more likely when exposure occurs close to delivery, so the reason and timing belong in handoff.

Adverse Effects

  • Transient hyperglycemia, insomnia, mood change, flushing, dyspepsia, fluid change, injection-site discomfort, and leukocyte demargination

  • Serious infection or masked sepsis, adrenal suppression, psychosis, GI bleeding, osteonecrosis with systemic exposure, neonatal hypoglycemia, or complications from unnecessary repeated courses
