Definition

Tenderness is pain or discomfort reported when an examiner gently palpates or applies a defined stress to a specific structure. It is an examination-elicited symptom, not an independently objective sign.

Plain Language

Tenderness means a particular area hurts when it is gently pressed or examined.

Why It Matters

The exact location and tissue that is tender can focus assessment toward bone, joint, muscle, tendon, ligament, bursa, infection, inflammation, or ischemia. Tenderness alone does not prove a diagnosis, and severe pain out of proportion, neurovascular change, fever, or rapidly spreading inflammation requires urgent evaluation.

Assessment Method

Explain the examination and obtain consent, ask the patient to identify baseline pain, begin away from the painful area with light pressure, and localize the structure and smallest reproducible area while documenting pressure, severity, guarding, swelling, warmth, skin change, function, sensation, and comparison with the opposite side.

Positive Finding

The patient reports pain or discomfort when a specific structure is gently palpated or stressed in a defined, reproducible way.

Mechanism

Mechanical pressure activates sensitized pain receptors in injured, inflamed, ischemic, or otherwise irritated tissue, producing a patient-reported pain response.

Clinical Interpretation

The provoked Tenderness symptom raises concern for localized tissue irritation, inflammation, injury, infection, ischemia, or sensitization involving bone, joint, muscle, tendon, ligament, bursa, fascia, or soft tissue because mechanical pressure activates sensitized pain receptors in injured, inflamed, ischemic, or otherwise irritated tissue, producing a patient-reported pain response. Evidence Limitations

Pain reporting, fear, analgesia, neuropathy, impaired communication, examiner pressure, and referred pain alter the response; absent tenderness does not exclude serious disease and tenderness does not prove a specific cause. Therefore, Tenderness cannot confirm or exclude localized tissue irritation, inflammation, injury, infection, ischemia, or sensitization involving bone, joint, muscle, tendon, ligament, bursa, fascia, or soft tissue by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive response can reflect another painful or sensory process such as allodynia, hyperalgesia, guarding, referred pain, or a trigger that was not applied as defined.

False Negative Situations

Tenderness may not reproduce when the trigger is incomplete, symptoms are intermittent, treatment has reduced the response, or communication is limited.
