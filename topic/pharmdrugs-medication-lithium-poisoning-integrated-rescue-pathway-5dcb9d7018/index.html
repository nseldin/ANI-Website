Used To Treat

Acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic lithium poisoning causing gastrointestinal symptoms, tremor, ataxia, confusion, myoclonus, seizure, dysrhythmia, kidney injury, or prolonged elevation.

Description

Lithium is a small mood-stabilizing cation that accumulates when renal clearance falls and disrupts neurologic, neuromuscular, and cardiac signaling; poisoning severity therefore cannot be graded from one serum number alone. Acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic exposures distribute lithium differently between blood and brain, so the same concentration can carry very different clinical risk. After a new acute ingestion, the blood concentration can be high before lithium has entered the brain, so early gastrointestinal symptoms may dominate and neurologic toxicity can evolve later. In chronic or acute-on-chronic poisoning, a lower concentration may accompany substantial brain accumulation, especially when dehydration or kidney dysfunction has reduced clearance. Coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, agitation, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, weakness, seizure, and coma are therefore more important than a threshold in isolation. Sustained-release tablets can absorb slowly or clump, and serum levels can continue rising. There is no binding antidote and activated charcoal does not adsorb lithium. Treatment stops lithium and interacting medicines, restores sodium and intravascular volume with monitored isotonic fluid when appropriate, follows serial concentrations and neurologic findings, and uses hemodialysis early when EXTRIP severity criteria are met. After dialysis, redistribution from tissue can raise the serum concentration again, so rebound monitoring is part of rescue.

Mechanism

Lithium is a small monovalent cation handled by the kidney similarly to sodium. When effective circulating volume or sodium delivery falls, the proximal nephron reabsorbs more sodium and lithium, which reduces lithium clearance; thiazides, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin-receptor blockers, NSAIDs, and kidney injury amplify that effect. Within cells, lithium alters second-messenger and ion-transport pathways, disrupting cerebellar, cortical, neuromuscular, and cardiac function. Isotonic sodium-containing fluid reverses volume-driven reabsorption and improves filtration when the patient is volume depleted, but forced diuresis adds risk without a reliable benefit. Intermittent hemodialysis removes lithium efficiently because it is small, water soluble, minimally protein bound, and has a low initial distribution volume. Tissue-to-plasma redistribution explains post-dialysis rebound and why serial clinical and concentration reassessment must determine whether another session is needed.

Administration Timing

  • Stop lithium and call Poison Help for any symptomatic patient, acute ingestion, rising level, kidney impairment, or interacting-drug illness. Call 911 for confusion, severe ataxia, seizure, coma, syncope, or dysrhythmia; assess airway, glucose, ECG, hydration, sodium, kidney function, and exposure pattern.

  • Give monitored isotonic fluid when volume depletion is present, correcting sodium abnormalities at a safe clinical rate and avoiding fluid overload. Consider whole-bowel irrigation only for selected substantial sustained-release ingestion with a protected airway and functioning bowel; charcoal does not bind lithium.

  • Contact nephrology and medical toxicology early rather than after prolonged deterioration. EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment when kidney function is impaired and lithium exceeds 4.0 mEq/L, or when decreased consciousness, seizure, or a life-threatening dysrhythmia is present regardless of level. EXTRIP suggests treatment when lithium exceeds 5.0 mEq/L, significant confusion is present, or optimal management is not expected to lower lithium below 1.0 mEq/L within 36 hours. Interpret every threshold with exposure pattern, symptoms, trend, and kidney function.

Nursing Essentials

  • Reconstruct acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic exposure; formulation; last dose; adherence; vomiting or diarrhea; fever; oral intake; urine output; recent dose change; kidney disease; pregnancy; and NSAID, thiazide, ACE-inhibitor, ARB, or other interacting use.

  • Perform serial neurologic examinations focused on tremor quality, gait, speech, coordination, clonus or myoclonus, reflexes, cognition, and level of consciousness. Institute fall, seizure, aspiration, and delirium precautions and document whether symptoms improve with concentration decline.

  • Measure strict intake and output, daily weight in prolonged care, sodium and fluid balance, ECG, and dialysis access. After dialysis, continue scheduled lithium levels and clinical checks because a rebound value without symptoms and a recurrent symptomatic rise are not managed identically.

Key Labs

  • Serial serum lithium concentrations using the same units, electrolytes including sodium and calcium, creatinine and urea, glucose, serum osmolality when indicated, urinalysis, thyroid studies in chronic exposure, pregnancy testing, and serial ECGs. Draw levels in timed relation to ingestion, dose, fluid, and dialysis.

  • A concentration collected in the wrong lithium-heparin tube can be falsely elevated. Chronic neurologic toxicity can be serious at a level that appears modest, and acute early levels may overstate tissue burden, so laboratory and clinical patterns must be reconciled.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause vomiting, diarrhea, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, sodium and volume disturbance, coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, delirium, myoclonus, seizure, coma, QT or conduction abnormalities, dysrhythmia, aspiration, kidney injury, and death.

  • Persistent neurologic injury after concentrations normalize is described as syndrome of irreversible lithium-effectuated neurotoxicity, or SILENT, although definitions and causality vary. Hemodialysis can cause hypotension, electrolyte shifts, access complications, and lithium withdrawal or psychiatric destabilization.

Contraindications

  • Do not use activated charcoal for isolated lithium because it does not meaningfully bind the ion. Do not force diuresis or create hypernatremia; restore normal volume and sodium physiology under monitoring.

  • Do not wait for an arbitrary level when severe neurologic or cardiac toxicity is present, and do not order dialysis from a number alone without exposure pattern, kidney function, trend, symptoms, and EXTRIP context.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Decreased consciousness, seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, progressive ataxia or myoclonus, kidney failure, rising sustained-release levels, inability to achieve safe clearance, or predicted prolonged elevation requires ICU and early dialysis planning. After extracorporeal treatment is stopped, obtain serial lithium measurements over 12 hours, as EXTRIP recommends, because tissue rebound may require another session.

Interactions

  • NSAIDs, thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, dehydration, low-sodium intake, and kidney injury can reduce lithium clearance. Serotonergic or antipsychotic medicines can complicate tremor, clonus, fever, or altered mental status.

  • Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and other medicines may alter lithium concentrations in variable directions; acute illness and volume status remain central, so reconcile every prescription and recent change rather than memorizing one interaction list.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Older adults

    Note

    Lower kidney reserve, polypharmacy, and nonspecific confusion or falls make chronic toxicity easy to miss at apparently modest levels.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal volume and kidney physiology change lithium handling; stabilize toxicity immediately and coordinate obstetric, nephrology, psychiatric, and fetal assessment.

  • Population

    Kidney disease

    Note

    Reduced elimination magnifies chronic accumulation and lowers the threshold for prolonged toxicity and specialist extracorporeal treatment.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP recommendations are based largely on low-quality observational evidence and expert consensus. Concentration thresholds help structure consultation but do not replace exposure pattern, neurologic examination, kidney function, trajectory, or feasibility of safe dialysis.

Nclex Traps

  • Chronic lithium neurotoxicity can be severe at a lower level than an early acute ingestion.

  • Activated charcoal does not bind lithium, and forced diuresis is not a safe shortcut.

  • Post-dialysis rebound occurs when lithium redistributes from tissues, so serial levels must continue.

Regulatory Status

There is no FDA-approved lithium antidote. Isotonic volume restoration, selective whole-bowel irrigation, and extracorporeal treatment are protocol- and specialist-directed; EXTRIP provides evidence-graded dialysis recommendations.

Boxed Warning

LITHIUM-TOXICITY WARNING: call 911 for confusion, severe ataxia, seizure, coma, or dysrhythmia. A single level can mislead because acute and chronic exposure distribute differently; stop lithium, restore appropriate volume, obtain serial levels and neurologic exams, and involve dialysis experts early for severe toxicity.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
