Risk Factors

  • Pregnancy creates a hypercoagulable state that persists into the puerperium; dehydration, delivery-related changes, infection, or immobility can add risk after birth.

  • Estrogen-containing contraception and hormone therapy increase venous thrombosis risk, particularly when combined with obesity, smoking, dehydration, or inherited thrombophilia.

  • Factor V Leiden, prothrombin variants, protein C or S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome, JAK2-associated myeloproliferative disease, active cancer, and autoimmune or inflammatory disease can sustain a prothrombotic state.

  • Head or neck infection, sepsis, respiratory infection, dehydration, anemia, trauma, neurosurgery, and a tumor compressing a sinus can provoke local or systemic thrombosis.

  • Young age does not protect against CVT; the disorder disproportionately affects younger adults and women of reproductive age compared with common arterial stroke.

Signs Symptoms

  • A new persistent, progressive, unusual, or severe headache is the most common presentation and may be the only early symptom; it can be diffuse, migraine-like, or rarely thunderclap.

  • Raised intracranial pressure can cause nausea, vomiting, papilledema, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual dimming, sustained visual loss, or diplopia, often from sixth-nerve dysfunction.

  • Focal or generalized seizure, unilateral weakness or numbness, aphasia, visual-field loss, neglect, or another focal deficit can result from cortical irritation, venous infarction, or hemorrhage.

  • Encephalopathy, increasing somnolence, coma, pupil change, abnormal posturing, or irregular breathing suggests severe edema, deep venous involvement, hemorrhage, or impending herniation.

  • Bilateral thalamic findings, a lesion that crosses arterial territories, or hemorrhage in an unusual cortical location should prompt a venous mechanism differential.

Diagnostics

  • Ask about headache time course, pregnancy or recent delivery, estrogen exposure, prior venous thrombosis, thrombophilia, malignancy, autoimmune disease, dehydration, infection, trauma, procedures, and medications because the history often supplies the missing venous clue.

  • Perform a complete neurologic examination plus visual acuity, fields, pupils, ocular movements, and funduscopic assessment for papilledema because threatened vision may be the major complication even without a dramatic NIHSS score.

  • Use CT venography or MR venography to confirm absent venous flow or filling from thrombus; choose the modality according to urgency, availability, contraindications, and local expertise. MRI also characterizes edema, venous infarction, hemorrhage, and deep-structure injury.

  • Noncontrast CT may reveal hemorrhagic infarct, edema, a dense sinus, or another diagnosis, but it can be normal and does not exclude CVT. Arterial CTA alone is not an adequate substitute unless the acquisition and interpretation include the cerebral venous system.

  • Catheter cerebral angiography is now usually reserved for unresolved high-suspicion cases, procedural planning, or selected endovascular treatment because CTV and MRV are the principal confirmatory tests.

Labs

  • CBC and platelets, coagulation studies, renal and hepatic function, electrolytes, glucose, pregnancy testing when relevant, and infection evaluation support anticoagulant, contrast, procedural, and mimic safety.

  • D-dimer can support probability in some acute presentations, but a normal result does not reliably exclude CVT, especially with isolated headache, smaller clot burden, or longer symptom duration; imaging follows clinical suspicion.

  • Targeted thrombophilia, antiphospholipid, myeloproliferative, autoimmune, infection, and malignancy testing is timed and selected with neurology or hematology because acute thrombosis, pregnancy, and anticoagulants can distort some results.

  • No laboratory result can substitute for CTV or MRV when the syndrome raises concern for CVT.

Treatments

  • Begin therapeutic anticoagulation under the acute stroke, neurology, and hematology pathway when CVT is confirmed or sufficiently established and no overriding contraindication exists. Parenteral heparin is commonly used first, followed by a specialist-selected vitamin K antagonist or direct oral anticoagulant, because stopping propagation and enabling venous recanalization treats the mechanism.

  • Do not automatically cancel anticoagulation because imaging shows a hemorrhagic venous infarct. The team distinguishes pressure-related hemorrhage within the venous lesion from another active bleeding process and weighs mass effect, procedural need, platelet count, and overall bleeding risk.

  • Treat the provoking cause, such as bacterial head or neck infection, dehydration, cancer, inflammatory disease, or unsafe estrogen exposure, because anticoagulation alone may not remove the driver. Duration and agent depend on whether the event was transiently provoked, unprovoked, recurrent, pregnancy-associated, cancer-associated, or linked to a persistent thrombophilia.

  • Reserve endovascular thrombus disruption or extraction for carefully selected severe cases with progressive neurologic deterioration or thrombus propagation despite appropriate medical treatment, using an experienced multidisciplinary team. Arterial LVO time windows, NIHSS thresholds, core-penumbra criteria, and routine arterial thrombectomy expectations do not determine venous EVT eligibility.

  • Manage intracranial hypertension, threatened vision, seizure, edema, airway, and mass effect concurrently. Clinical seizures require prompt antiseizure treatment; worsening mass effect or impending herniation can require emergency decompressive surgery even while the thrombotic mechanism is addressed.

Nursing Priorities

  • Trend headache severity and pattern, neurologic findings, consciousness, pupils, vision, eye movements, vomiting, and seizure activity because pressure or parenchymal injury may worsen before obvious vital-sign collapse.

  • Use seizure precautions, protect the airway during a seizure, time the event, document focal onset and recovery, and escalate persistent altered consciousness because nonconvulsive seizure or worsening ICP may require EEG and urgent imaging.

  • Administer and monitor anticoagulation exactly as ordered; assess gums, urine, stool, skin, puncture sites, hemoglobin, platelets, and new headache or deficit because bleeding surveillance must occur without assuming every hemorrhagic finding means therapy failure.

  • Keep swallowing, aspiration, mobility, fall, skin, and DVT precautions matched to the neurologic deficit; CVT can cause the same functional hazards as arterial stroke even when headache was the first symptom.

  • Avoid dehydration while respecting individualized ICP, renal, cardiac, and pregnancy needs; reconcile estrogen products, pregnancy status, infection treatment, and planned procedures at each transition because these details change risk and medication choice.

  • Escalate visual obscurations, diplopia, declining acuity, or papilledema promptly because sustained intracranial hypertension can cause permanent visual loss despite preserved limb strength.

Complications

  • Hemorrhagic venous infarction, cerebral edema, mass effect, obstructed CSF absorption, intracranial hypertension, and herniation can cause rapid deterioration.

  • Seizures can occur at presentation or later; cortical injury, hemorrhage, and an initial seizure increase later epilepsy risk.

  • Papilledema can progress to irreversible optic-nerve injury and visual loss if intracranial pressure remains elevated.

  • Thrombus propagation, recurrent CVT, systemic venous thromboembolism, and anticoagulant-related bleeding can complicate the acute or recovery course.

  • Persistent headache, fatigue, slowed cognition, mood symptoms, and difficulty returning to work or school can remain even when standard disability scores appear favorable.

Contraindications

  • Do not apply an arterial LVO thrombolysis or thrombectomy algorithm automatically; CVT is a venous outflow disorder with different imaging and treatment logic.

  • Do not treat hemorrhagic venous infarction as an automatic absolute contraindication to anticoagulation, and do not start or stop anticoagulation without the specialist assessment that separates venous congestion from another bleeding mechanism.

  • Do not dismiss a progressive postpartum, estrogen-associated, cancer-associated, infectious, or prothrombotic headache as routine migraine when visual, seizure, focal, or pressure features are present.

  • Do not use a normal noncontrast CT, modest NIHSS, or normal D-dimer as a stand-alone rule-out when clinical suspicion remains high.

  • Do not delay urgent venous imaging for an exhaustive thrombophilia panel because confirming and stabilizing the acute event comes first.

Red Flags

  • New persistent or rapidly worsening headache with pregnancy, recent delivery, estrogen exposure, thrombophilia, cancer, infection, dehydration, or prior venous thrombosis

  • Headache with papilledema, transient visual loss, diplopia, repeated vomiting, focal deficit, or seizure

  • Declining consciousness, pupil change, new weakness, abnormal posturing, or irregular breathing suggesting mass effect or herniation

  • Neurologic deterioration or thrombus propagation despite appropriate anticoagulation

  • New bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, hemodynamic instability, or urgent procedural need during anticoagulation

Patient Education

  • Explain that this clot blocks a brain vein rather than a brain artery: pressure backs up behind the obstruction, which is why headache, vision symptoms, seizure, swelling, and even bleeding can occur together.

  • Take anticoagulation exactly as prescribed and ask before pregnancy attempts, estrogen use, new medication, surgery, dental procedures, or stopping therapy because both recurrence and bleeding risk depend on coordinated planning.

  • Seek emergency care for a new severe or progressive headache, visual loss, seizure, weakness, language change, repeated vomiting, fainting, or unusual bleeding; do not wait for a classic FAST pattern.

  • Discuss hydration, mobility, infection treatment, smoking, cancer or thrombophilia follow-up, pregnancy planning, and contraception with the care team because prevention must address the actual provoking factors.

  • Expect follow-up for headache, vision, seizure, cognition, fatigue, mood, and return to work or school because a good motor outcome does not mean recovery is complete.

Nclex Traps

  • CVT blocks venous outflow; arterial LVO blocks arterial inflow. The two mechanisms do not share identical thrombectomy selection rules.

  • A hemorrhagic venous infarct does not automatically mean anticoagulation is contraindicated because venous congestion caused the hemorrhage; the specialist team still assesses other bleeding hazards.

  • CTV or MRV confirms CVT. A normal early noncontrast CT, normal D-dimer, or low NIHSS can miss it.

  • Headache, papilledema, seizure, and postpartum or estrogen context may be more revealing than classic unilateral FAST findings.

  • Endovascular treatment is a selected rescue consideration for deterioration despite appropriate treatment, not routine first-line arterial thrombectomy.

Definition

Cerebral venous thrombosis is a clot in one or more dural venous sinuses, cerebral veins, or both. It is a venous stroke, but its mechanism differs fundamentally from an arterial large-vessel occlusion. An arterial LVO blocks oxygenated blood from entering a downstream arterial territory; CVT blocks deoxygenated blood from leaving the brain. Venous pressure then backs up into small veins and capillaries, which can produce edema, reduced tissue perfusion, venous infarction, and hemorrhage. Sinus obstruction can also impair cerebrospinal-fluid absorption and raise intracranial pressure. The presentation is therefore unusually varied: progressive or severe headache may dominate, while papilledema, visual change, seizure, focal deficit, encephalopathy, or coma may appear alone or in combination. Because headache can precede focal findings and an initial noncontrast CT can be unrevealing, diagnosis requires deliberate consideration and venous imaging rather than applying only an arterial-stroke screen.

Etiology

Most CVT reflects a temporary or persistent prothrombotic condition. Important contexts include pregnancy and especially the puerperium, estrogen-containing contraception or hormone therapy, inherited thrombophilia, antiphospholipid syndrome, myeloproliferative disease or another malignancy, autoimmune or inflammatory disease, head and neck infection, sepsis, dehydration, anemia, obesity, trauma, neurosurgery, and a mass compressing a sinus. Some medications and rare immune thrombotic syndromes also provoke CVT. Several factors may interact; for example, estrogen exposure plus an inherited thrombophilia or dehydration may create more risk than either factor alone. When no obvious trigger exists, clinicians investigate for persistent thrombophilia, cancer, inflammatory disease, or another hidden cause because that mechanism influences recurrence risk and anticoagulation planning.

Pathology

A thrombus may involve a large dural sinus, smaller cortical veins, the deep cerebral venous system, or multiple connected segments. Occlusion prevents normal venous drainage, so upstream venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure rises. The blood-brain barrier leaks, producing vasogenic edema; severe congestion lowers the arterial-to-venous pressure gradient and impairs local perfusion, adding cytotoxic edema and infarction. Fragile congested veins can rupture, so venous infarcts are often hemorrhagic and may cross ordinary arterial territory boundaries. Superior sagittal sinus obstruction can also reduce CSF absorption through arachnoid granulations, creating intracranial hypertension and papilledema. Deep venous thrombosis can injure both thalami or deep structures, while cortical-vein involvement can irritate cortex and provoke focal seizures.

Pathophysiology

CVT creates two linked injury pathways. First, blocked venous outflow raises intracranial venous pressure. That pressure is transmitted backward into capillaries, causing fluid leakage, tissue swelling, reduced capillary perfusion, and sometimes hemorrhagic venous infarction. Second, thrombosed major sinuses can impair CSF resorption, raising intracranial pressure even when no large infarct is present. Headache, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations, diplopia from sixth-nerve dysfunction, and papilledema arise from the pressure pathway; weakness, aphasia, sensory loss, neglect, seizure, or altered consciousness arise from focal parenchymal injury or mass effect. Symptoms often evolve over hours to days rather than beginning as one maximal arterial-territory deficit, but CVT can still present suddenly, including thunderclap headache or an acute focal syndrome. Anticoagulation addresses the upstream cause by preventing clot propagation and permitting recanalization. This explains the important hemorrhage nuance: blood within a venous infarct resulted from congested, pressurized microvessels, so hemorrhagic venous infarction is not by itself an automatic reason to withhold specialist-directed anticoagulation. The team must still assess bleeding mechanism, size, mass effect, platelets, procedures, and other contraindications rather than applying that principle blindly.
