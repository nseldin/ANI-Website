Definition

Cholinergic toxicity develops when organophosphate, carbamate, medication, or nerve-agent exposure causes acetylcholine accumulation at muscarinic, nicotinic, and central receptors. The characteristic pattern is miosis, bronchorrhea, wheeze, vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, bradycardia, fasciculations, weakness, confusion, or seizure. Confirm or stage it with exposure and toxidrome recognition with immediate airway assessment; distinguish it from opioid toxicity, nicotine exposure, mushroom poisoning, myasthenic crisis, and pulmonary edema by secretions, pupils, fasciculation, exposure, and response. The priority is to protect staff and decontaminate clothing and skin because respiratory arrest can complicate Cholinergic toxicity. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • exposure and toxidrome recognition with immediate airway assessment

  • continuous ECG, oxygenation, ventilation, secretions, strength, and temperature monitoring

  • cholinesterase testing when available without waiting to treat

  • Distinguish Cholinergic toxicity from opioid toxicity, nicotine exposure, mushroom poisoning, myasthenic crisis, and pulmonary edema by secretions, pupils, fasciculation, exposure, and response.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • protect staff and decontaminate clothing and skin

  • give atropine repeatedly until bronchial secretions and ventilation improve

  • give an oxime early for organophosphate nicotinic toxicity and support seizures and ventilation

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for copious secretions, hypoxemia, bradycardia, seizures, progressive weakness, apnea, or recurrent symptoms after initial response because repeated antidote dosing and prolonged ventilatory support may be required.

  • Protect staff with appropriate personal protective equipment and remove contaminated clothing with protocol-directed decontamination because secondary exposure can poison rescuers and spread the agent.

  • Assess airway secretions, wheeze, respiratory effort, oxygenation, heart rate, pupils, mental status, fasciculations, and weakness because bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, and neuromuscular failure are the immediate lethal mechanisms.

  • Suction aggressively, provide oxygen and assisted ventilation, and administer atropine as ordered until pulmonary secretions and ventilation improve because atropine reverses muscarinic airway and cardiovascular effects rather than simply normalizing pupil size.

  • Administer prescribed pralidoxime early for significant organophosphate poisoning and benzodiazepine therapy for seizures because oxime reactivation becomes less effective after enzyme aging and seizures add brain injury.

Complications

  • respiratory arrest can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

  • aspiration can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

  • seizure can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

  • dysrhythmia can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

  • delayed neuropathy can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

  • intermediate weakness syndrome can complicate Cholinergic toxicity.

Red Flags

  • Copious bronchial secretions, wheeze, or rapidly falling oxygen saturation

  • Bradycardia, hypotension, or unstable rhythm

  • Seizure, coma, fasciculations, or progressive muscle weakness

  • Apnea or recurrent cholinergic findings after apparent improvement

Patient Education

  • Explain that contaminated clothing and skin can keep exposing the patient and others, so follow responder instructions and do not carry contaminated belongings into a clean area.

  • After recovery, review occupational or household pesticide safety and seek urgent care for recurrent sweating, diarrhea, secretions, weakness, breathing difficulty, or confusion.

Nclex Traps

  • Atropine is titrated to drying the lungs, not pupil size

  • Bradycardia is not required; tachycardia can occur in severe poisoning
