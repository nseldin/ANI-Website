Class

Ophthalmic alpha-1 adrenergic agonist mydriatic without cycloplegia

Used To Treat

Pupil dilation for examination or procedures, often combined with an antimuscarinic when both strong mydriasis and cycloplegia are required.

Description

Phenylephrine ophthalmic stimulates alpha-1 receptors in the iris radial dilator, enlarging the pupil without reliably paralyzing ciliary accommodation. That distinction explains why it can improve dilation but cannot replace cyclopentolate for cycloplegic refraction. Systemic absorption, especially from concentrated 10% solution, can cause severe hypertension, reflex bradycardia, arrhythmia, myocardial ischemia or pulmonary edema.

Mechanism

Alpha-1-Gq signaling raises calcium in radial iris smooth muscle, causing contraction and mydriasis. Because muscarinic ciliary muscle remains active, near-focus accommodation persists unless an antimuscarinic is also given. Vascular alpha-1 activation after systemic absorption causes vasoconstriction and increased afterload; baroreflex bradycardia may accompany hypertension.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Concentrated ophthalmic phenylephrine can produce severe cardiovascular reactions. Avoid the 10% concentration in patients with severe cardiovascular disease and in low-birth-weight infants under relevant labels; use 2.5% when adequate. Apply punctal occlusion and monitor blood pressure/rhythm in high-risk patients. It can precipitate acute angle closure.

Adverse Effects

  • Stinging, photophobia, rebound miosis, increased IOP/angle closure, hypertension, reflex bradycardia, tachyarrhythmia, headache, myocardial ischemia, syncope and pulmonary edema.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity, narrow-angle/angle-closure risk and product/concentration-specific cardiovascular or neonatal restrictions. Clarify severe hypertension, coronary disease, aneurysm, thyrotoxicosis and interacting adrenergic drugs.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify concentration before administration; 2.5% and 10% are not interchangeable. Check blood pressure and cardiac history in high-risk patients and remember that dilation without cycloplegia may be insufficient for refraction.

  • Verify the exact eye, formulation, concentration, frequency, contact-lens instructions, and whether other drops must be separated by at least 5 minutes. Never let the bottle tip touch the eye, lashes, fingers, or another surface.

  • After the drop, gently close the eyelid and press the nasolacrimal duct for 1 to 2 minutes when appropriate. This reduces drainage into the nose and systemic exposure; wiping excess from the skin also limits local irritation.

Interactions

  • MAO inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, other sympathomimetics and some antihypertensives can intensify pressor/arrhythmic effects. Halogenated anesthetics can sensitize the myocardium. Antimuscarinics add dilation and angle-closure risk.

Key Labs

  • Pupil response and angle/IOP context

  • Blood pressure, pulse and ECG when high concentration or cardiovascular risk is present

  • Pulmonary status if dyspnea develops

Nclex Traps

  • Phenylephrine causes mydriasis without dependable cycloplegia.

  • A slow pulse after the drop can be reflex bradycardia from severe hypertension.

  • Always verify 2.5% versus 10%.
