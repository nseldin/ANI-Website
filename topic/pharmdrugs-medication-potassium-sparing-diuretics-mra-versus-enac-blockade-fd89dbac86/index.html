Used To Treat

Depending on the exact drug: heart-failure or kidney-disease outcome reduction, primary aldosteronism, resistant hypertension, edema with potassium loss, prevention of diuretic-induced hypokalemia, and selected ENaC-driven disorders. They are not interchangeable despite sharing hyperkalemia risk.

Description

Potassium-sparing diuretics reduce collecting-duct sodium uptake, so the electrical drive for potassium and hydrogen secretion falls. They do this through two mechanisms: mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists, or MRAs, block aldosterone-dependent transcription, while amiloride and triamterene directly close ENaC from the tubular lumen. MRAs include spironolactone, eplerenone, and finerenone and also limit inflammatory and fibrotic signaling. Both groups make the lumen less negative, which explains potassium conservation, hyperkalemia, and normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. MRAs can improve clinical outcomes through anti-remodeling biology even when their natriuretic effect is mild; ENaC blockers are principally transport drugs and do not reproduce all MRA outcome evidence.

Mechanism

Aldosterone-bound mineralocorticoid receptor increases SGK1, prevents NEDD4-2 from removing ENaC, and increases basolateral sodium-potassium-ATPase. Spironolactone, eplerenone, and finerenone suppress that receptor program over hours to days. Direct ENaC blockers reach the lumen through tubular secretion and inhibit the channel immediately. In either case, less sodium crosses principal cells and less negative electrical potential develops in the tubular lumen, reducing ROMK and BK-mediated potassium secretion. Lower mineralocorticoid or ENaC activity also reduces alpha-intercalated-cell hydrogen secretion, explaining type-4-RTA-like acidosis in susceptible patients. Because the distal nephron handles a small sodium fraction, these drugs are weak stand-alone natriuretics; their clinical power comes from potassium conservation, aldosterone antagonism, disease modification, or correction of a specific distal transport problem.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: The class-defining danger is hyperkalemia, especially with CKD, diabetes, older age, dehydration, acute kidney injury, potassium supplements or salt substitutes, trimethoprim, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, or duplicate potassium-sparing therapy.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyperkalemia can cause weakness, paresthesia, conduction slowing, ventricular arrhythmia, or cardiac arrest; reduced hydrogen secretion can produce a normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.

  • Spironolactone can cause gynecomastia, breast pain, sexual dysfunction, and menstrual effects; eplerenone is more receptor selective; triamterene can crystallize and contribute to stones; amiloride commonly causes gastrointestinal or neurologic symptoms.

Contraindications

  • Clinically significant hyperkalemia, severe renal impairment or anuria, and duplicate potassium-sparing therapy are central contraindication patterns, with exact eGFR and product rules differing by drug and indication.

  • Do not treat the class name as permission to replace potassium automatically. A falling potassium on a loop and a rising potassium on an MRA are different physiologic states.

Nursing Essentials

  • Check potassium and kidney function before initiation, shortly after a start or dose change, and periodically thereafter according to the drug, indication, and risk. Hold and escalate according to the clinical plan when potassium rises or kidney function deteriorates.

  • Reconcile potassium chloride, salt substitutes, ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, NSAID, trimethoprim, heparin, and other MRAs or ENaC blockers. Teach that healthy-sounding potassium products can become dangerous in this pathway.

Interactions

  • RAAS blockers, potassium products, trimethoprim, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, NSAIDs, and heparin can converge on hyperkalemia. CYP3A inhibitors are especially important for eplerenone and finerenone.

  • Lithium and digoxin have drug-specific interactions; spironolactone can interfere with some digoxin assays, while electrolyte changes alter arrhythmia susceptibility.

Key Labs

  • Potassium, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, bicarbonate, sodium, blood pressure, volume status, and ECG when hyperkalemia is significant. Add indication-specific albuminuria, heart-failure, or endocrine markers.

  • Potassium monitoring is front-loaded because an early rise may appear before the long-term benefit. Stable chronic monitoring does not replace reassessment during acute illness, dehydration, NSAID use, or dose change.

Nclex Traps

  • Spironolactone and amiloride both conserve potassium, but one blocks an intracellular hormone receptor and the other directly blocks a luminal ion channel.

  • Finerenone is called a nonsteroidal MRA, but it should not be presented as a rescue diuretic for pulmonary edema; its value is disease-modifying receptor blockade.
