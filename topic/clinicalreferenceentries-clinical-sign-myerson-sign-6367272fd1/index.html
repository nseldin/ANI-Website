Definition

Myerson sign is a bedside primitive-reflex sign. It is a clue to frontal release or parkinsonian disease. A positive finding means blinking persists without habituation.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Myerson sign means blinking persists without habituation.

Why It Matters

A positive Myerson sign response can signal frontal release or parkinsonian disease. Impaired basal-ganglia or frontal inhibitory control prevents normal suppression of the blink response. Myerson sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • frontal release or parkinsonian disease

Assessment Method

Tap repeatedly over the glabella at a steady pace while the patient looks ahead.

Positive Finding

Blinking persists without habituation.

Mechanism

Impaired basal-ganglia or frontal inhibitory control prevents normal suppression of the blink response.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Myerson sign response raises concern for frontal release or parkinsonian disease because impaired basal-ganglia or frontal inhibitory control prevents normal suppression of the blink response. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Myerson sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Myerson sign to focus evaluation of frontal release or parkinsonian disease and the reviewed alternatives: anxiety, dry eye, frontal dementia. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Tap repeatedly over the glabella at a steady pace while the patient looks ahead. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Myerson sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Healthy older adults and other neurologic disorders may show persistent blinking. Therefore, Myerson sign cannot confirm or exclude frontal release or parkinsonian disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • anxiety

  • dry eye

  • frontal dementia

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from anxiety, dry eye, frontal dementia, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Myerson sign may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Limited adjunct; not specific for Parkinson disease. When performed as described, Myerson sign can adjust concern for frontal release or parkinsonian disease, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Myerson sign: Healthy older adults and other neurologic disorders may show persistent blinking.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Myerson sign, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Tap repeatedly over the glabella at a steady pace while the patient looks ahead. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not count the first few expected blinks as positive; assess failure to habituate.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact Myerson sign observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: Blinking persists without habituation. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: Tap repeatedly over the glabella at a steady pace while the patient looks ahead. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, frontal release or parkinsonian disease, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

For Myerson sign, apply this exact authored caution when deciding whom to notify and how quickly: Do not count the first few expected blinks as positive; assess failure to habituate. Base escalation for Myerson sign on the patient's current condition, associated findings, and the cause under evaluation; this card does not assign a universal urgency level.

Common Pitfalls
