Definition

Meckel diverticulum develops when a persistent omphalomesenteric duct remnant forms a true ileal diverticulum that may contain acid-secreting ectopic gastric tissue. The characteristic pattern is painless lower gastrointestinal bleeding in a child, or obstruction, inflammation, or perforation that can mimic appendicitis. Confirm or stage it with technetium pertechnetate scan for ectopic gastric mucosa in a stable bleeding child; distinguish it from appendicitis, intussusception, inflammatory bowel disease, polyp, and other bleeding sources by location, imaging, endoscopy, and operative findings. The priority is to resuscitate significant bleeding or obstruction because major hemorrhage and anemia can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Abdominal pain pattern, distention, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea or constipation, stool/emesis blood, jaundice, dysphagia, poor intake, dehydration, or guarding are the useful GI clues.

  • Escalate rigid abdomen, peritonitis signs, GI bleeding, shock, severe dehydration, fever with jaundice, or worsening mental status.

Diagnostics

  • technetium pertechnetate scan for ectopic gastric mucosa in a stable bleeding child

  • CBC and serial hemodynamics during bleeding

  • ultrasound, CT, endoscopy, or operative evaluation according to age and presentation

  • Distinguish Meckel diverticulum from appendicitis, intussusception, inflammatory bowel disease, polyp, and other bleeding sources by location, imaging, endoscopy, and operative findings.

Labs

  • do not apply adult ranges without checking age

  • glucose, bilirubin, CBC, and electrolytes often require age-specific interpretation

Treatments

  • resuscitate significant bleeding or obstruction

  • perform surgical resection for symptomatic diverticulum

  • individualize incidental-diverticulum management by risk rather than removing every lesion

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for hemodynamic change, ongoing maroon or bright-red stool, bilious vomiting, peritonitis, rigid distention, fever with worsening pain, or reduced urine because hemorrhage, volvulus, strangulation, or perforation threatens bowel and circulation.

  • Assess stool color and amount, abdominal pain and distention, emesis character, bowel sounds, hydration, perfusion, and prior episodes because Meckel diverticulum may present as painless bleeding, obstruction, intussusception, inflammation, or perforation.

  • Quantify blood loss, trend pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, hemoglobin, urine output, and mental status, and secure intravenous access because children can compensate until substantial blood volume is lost.

  • Keep the child nil by mouth, provide ordered isotonic fluids, analgesia, antiemetic, and gastric decompression when obstructed, and avoid forcing oral intake because surgery or anesthesia may be urgent.

  • Prepare for radionuclide imaging, ultrasound, surgery, or transfusion as directed and preserve stool or emesis descriptions because no single presentation identifies every Meckel complication.

Complications

  • major hemorrhage and anemia

  • intussusception can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

  • volvulus can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

  • or bowel obstruction can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

  • diverticulitis can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

  • perforation can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

  • peritonitis can complicate Meckel diverticulum.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, tachycardia with poor perfusion, hypotension, altered mental status, or ongoing large-volume bleeding

  • Bilious vomiting, rigid or rapidly distending abdomen, absent stool or gas, or severe colicky pain

  • Guarding, rebound tenderness, fever, free air, or other perforation and peritonitis findings

  • Oliguria, severe dehydration, rapidly falling hemoglobin, or transfusion requirement

Patient Education

  • Painless dark-red or maroon stool in a child is not harmless; save a photo if safe and seek prompt care, especially with pallor, weakness, pain, or faintness.

  • After surgery, follow wound, activity, and diet instructions and return for fever, bilious vomiting, increasing abdominal swelling or pain, bloody stool, or inability to pass stool or gas.

Nclex Traps

  • A negative scan does not exclude Meckel diverticulum when suspicion remains high

  • The classic pediatric bleeding is often painless
