Pathology

Autoimmune adrenalitis is common in high-resource settings; TB, hemorrhage, metastasis, or infiltrative disease are other causes. Low cortisol removes vascular/catecholamine support and low aldosterone wastes sodium while retaining potassium.

Etiology

Autoimmune adrenalitis is common. Other causes include tuberculosis or other infection, adrenal hemorrhage, adrenal metastasis, bilateral adrenalectomy, genetic adrenal disorders, and medication-related adrenal injury. Abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroids can cause adrenal insufficiency, but that is usually secondary or tertiary rather than primary Addison disease.

Signs Symptoms

  • Fatigue and muscle weakness

  • weight loss and anorexia

  • nausea/vomiting or abdominal pain

  • orthostatic hypotension

  • dehydration

  • salt craving

  • hyperpigmentation in primary disease

  • hyponatremia symptoms

  • hyperkalemia dysrhythmia risk

  • confusion or shock in adrenal crisis

Diagnostics

  • Cortisol low

  • morning cortisol low

  • ACTH high in primary adrenal insufficiency

  • cosyntropin stimulation test shows poor cortisol response

  • aldosterone often low in primary disease

  • renin may be high

  • sodium low

  • potassium high when aldosterone is low

  • glucose low

  • BUN/creatinine may rise with dehydration

Treatments

  • Lifelong glucocorticoid replacement such as hydrocortisone

  • mineralocorticoid replacement such as fludrocortisone when aldosterone deficient

  • stress-dose steroids during illness/procedure per plan

  • adrenal crisis: IV hydrocortisone, isotonic fluids, dextrose if hypoglycemic, and treatment of trigger

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess fatigue, weight loss, nausea, abdominal pain, postural symptoms, skin change, salt craving, infection, vomiting, trauma, pregnancy, and steroid access because chronic adrenal insufficiency can decompensate when cortisol demand rises.

  • Administer glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement exactly as prescribed and trend pressure, orthostasis, weight, sodium, potassium, glucose, and symptoms because inadequate replacement causes volume and metabolic instability while excess causes treatment harm.

  • During illness or procedures, verify the written stress-dose plan, ability to retain oral medicine, emergency injection supply, and medical identification because vomiting or delayed dose escalation is a common pathway to adrenal crisis.

  • If crisis is suspected, obtain ordered samples without delaying immediate hydrocortisone and isotonic fluid and monitor glucose, electrolytes, rhythm, urine output, and perfusion because untreated cortisol deficiency can produce refractory shock and hypoglycemia.

  • Escalate immediately for persistent vomiting or diarrhea, hypotension, collapse, confusion, severe weakness, abdominal pain with shock, hypoglycemia, fever, or inability to take steroids because adrenal crisis treatment must begin before confirmatory results.

Complications

  • Adrenal crisis

  • shock

  • severe hypoglycemia

  • hyperkalemia-related dysrhythmias

  • AKI from dehydration

Patient Education

  • Teach sick-day dosing, when and how to use emergency injectable hydrocortisone, and when to call emergency services because oral medication may fail during vomiting or severe illness.

  • Reinforce carrying steroid emergency identification and spare medication and never stopping long-term steroid replacement abruptly because the body cannot rapidly replace missing cortisol.

Nclex Traps

  • Addison disease is adrenal hormone deficiency, not hormone excess. Think low cortisol, often low aldosterone, low sodium, high potassium, low glucose, dehydration, hypotension, weight loss, hyperpigmentation, and crisis risk.

  • Memory trick: Addison = Add is gone. Too little adrenal hormone means the nurse watches for shock, hypoglycemia, and hyperkalemia.

Definition

Addison disease is primary adrenal insufficiency: damaged adrenal cortex cannot make enough cortisol and often aldosterone, causing fatigue, weight loss, hyperpigmentation, hypotension, salt craving, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and shock risk under stress. The crash-course danger is adrenal crisis when infection, trauma, surgery, vomiting, or missed steroids outstrips cortisol reserve.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension, collapse, confusion, or signs of shock

  • Persistent vomiting or diarrhea preventing steroid absorption

  • Severe hypoglycemia, weakness, or seizure

  • Fever, major injury, labor, or procedure without an effective stress-dose plan

    Cue

    Add-is-gone

    Expansion
    • Cue

      Add

      Meaning

      The adrenal cortex normally adds cortisol support for stress, vascular responsiveness, glucose regulation, and immune modulation; aldosterone also supports sodium retention and potassium excretion.

    • Cue

      Is gone

      Meaning

      In primary adrenal insufficiency, cortisol is deficient and aldosterone is often deficient, explaining fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, volume depletion, and crisis risk.

    Id

    mnemonic:addison-add-is-gone

    Limitations

    • Addison disease refers to primary adrenal insufficiency; secondary or tertiary adrenal insufficiency may preserve aldosterone and may not cause hyperkalemia or hyperpigmentation.

    • The cue does not diagnose adrenal insufficiency or replace cortisol, ACTH, stimulation testing, medication review, and specialist interpretation.

    • Not every patient has every classic sign, especially early in disease or during partial treatment.
