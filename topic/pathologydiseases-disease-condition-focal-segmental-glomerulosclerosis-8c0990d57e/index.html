Definition

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis is a pattern of podocyte injury and scarring seen in only some glomeruli and only part of each affected glomerular tuft. It is a clinicopathologic syndrome with primary, genetic, secondary or adaptive, and undetermined-cause forms rather than one uniform disease with one treatment.

Pathology

Focal means that only a subset of sampled glomeruli is affected, and segmental means that only part of an affected tuft is sclerosed. Injured podocytes retract or lose foot-process architecture, weakening the filtration barrier so albumin escapes into urine; progressive scar then reduces functioning nephron mass.

Pathophysiology

  • Podocytes cannot divide effectively to replace lost cells. When injury exceeds their ability to cover the glomerular capillary surface, bare segments adhere and scar, causing persistent protein leakage and a cycle of nephron loss.

  • Primary FSGS is defined clinically by nephrotic syndrome with an FSGS lesion after secondary and genetic causes are excluded. Adaptive forms arise when remaining nephrons hyperfilter under increased workload; suppressing immunity does not correct that mechanical driver.

Etiology

Primary FSGS is thought to involve an unidentified circulating or podocyte-toxic factor. Genetic forms arise from variants affecting podocyte or glomerular-basement-membrane proteins. Secondary forms follow adaptive hyperfiltration, reduced nephron mass, severe obesity, selected viral infections, or drugs. An FSGS lesion without nephrotic syndrome or a proven cause may be classified as FSGS of undetermined cause.

Risk Factors

  • Family history, syndromic findings, early onset, or treatment resistance suggesting a genetic podocytopathy

  • Reduced nephron mass, reflux injury, severe obesity, or another sustained hyperfiltration state

  • HIV or another selected infection and exposure to a known podocyte-toxic medication

  • APOL1 high-risk genotypes in people with relevant ancestry; ancestry is a clue for genetic counseling, not a substitute for testing

Signs Symptoms

  • Proteinuria ranging from subnephrotic loss to full nephrotic syndrome

  • Edema, rapid weight gain, foamy urine, ascites, or dyspnea from fluid retention

  • Hypoalbuminemia and hyperlipidemia when nephrotic syndrome is present

  • Hypertension, microscopic hematuria, and progressive decline in glomerular filtration

  • Thromboembolism or infection as a complication of severe nephrotic syndrome

Diagnostics

  • Quantify urine protein or albumin, assess urinalysis and sediment, measure serum albumin and kidney function, and evaluate blood pressure, edema, medication exposure, infection risk, and secondary causes.

  • Kidney biopsy with light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy establishes the lesion and evaluates competing glomerular disease. Sampling matters because a focal lesion can be missed in too small a specimen.

  • Diffuse podocyte foot-process effacement supports primary FSGS only in the correct nephrotic clinical context; pathology is not interpreted without the presentation and causal evaluation.

  • Selected patients need genetic testing and counseling, especially with family or syndromic features, early onset, steroid resistance, transplant planning, or living-related donor questions.

Labs

  • Urine protein-to-creatinine or albumin-to-creatinine ratio measures the leak and its response over time.

  • Serum creatinine and estimated GFR show nephron function, while potassium and bicarbonate reveal consequences of kidney impairment or therapy.

  • Serum albumin and lipid studies characterize nephrotic syndrome and help explain edema and vascular risk.

  • Serologic and infection testing is directed at secondary causes and competing glomerular diseases rather than used as one universal FSGS panel.

Assessment

Trend blood pressure, daily weight, edema, intake and output, respiratory status, urine protein, serum albumin, creatinine or eGFR, potassium, medication exposure, infection findings, and thromboembolic symptoms. Connect the biopsy pattern to the clinical subtype before anticipating immunosuppression, because primary and adaptive disease require fundamentally different treatment logic.

Differential

Differentiate primary, genetic, secondary or adaptive, and undetermined-cause FSGS from minimal-change disease, membranous nephropathy, diabetic or obesity-related glomerulosclerosis, HIV-associated nephropathy, reflux or reduced-nephron injury, and other nephrotic or podocyte disorders. A biopsy label alone does not identify the cause.

Treatments

  • All forms receive cause-directed supportive kidney care: appropriate blood-pressure control, reduction of proteinuria, sodium and edema management, cardiovascular-risk reduction, and avoidance of nephrotoxins.

  • Primary FSGS with nephrotic syndrome may receive high-dose glucocorticoid therapy under nephrology supervision; response and toxicity are monitored rather than assuming every biopsy lesion is steroid responsive.

  • A calcineurin inhibitor may be used when glucocorticoids are contraindicated or not tolerated and is recommended for steroid-resistant primary FSGS under a protocol that monitors kidney function, blood pressure, electrolytes, levels, and interactions.

  • Secondary, genetic, or undetermined-cause disease without nephrotic syndrome is generally managed by treating the driver and supportive care rather than automatic immunosuppression.

  • Advanced chronic kidney disease may require kidney-replacement planning; primary FSGS can recur after transplantation, so recurrence surveillance is part of transplant care.

Contraindications

  • Do not prescribe immunosuppression simply because a report contains the words FSGS; first classify the clinical syndrome and exclude a secondary or genetic driver.

  • ACE inhibitors and angiotensin-receptor blockers are contraindicated in pregnancy and require monitoring for hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, and hypotension.

  • Calcineurin inhibitors can cause nephrotoxicity, hypertension, electrolyte disturbance, infection, and major interactions; a rising creatinine is not automatically disease progression.

  • Glucocorticoids require infection, glucose, blood pressure, psychiatric, gastrointestinal, bone, ocular, and adrenal-risk planning.

  • Avoid NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins unless a prescriber has weighed kidney and volume risks.

Safety Considerations

  • Severe nephrotic syndrome increases venous-thromboembolism and infection risk because anticoagulant and immune proteins are lost in urine.

  • Edema can conceal intravascular depletion; aggressive diuresis without perfusion assessment can worsen kidney injury and hypotension.

  • Immunosuppression can mute fever and increase opportunistic infection risk, so subtle deterioration needs prompt evaluation.

  • Newer targeted medicines and SGLT2-based strategies are indication-, eGFR-, age-, pregnancy-, and regulatory-status specific rather than universal FSGS therapy.

Nursing Priorities

  • Measure daily weight under consistent conditions, strict intake and output, edema distribution, lung sounds, oxygenation, blood pressure, and functional tolerance to detect fluid movement earlier than appearance alone.

  • Trend urine protein, albumin, creatinine or eGFR, potassium, glucose, lipids, and drug-specific toxicities and report a worsening pattern rather than an isolated value.

  • Assess unilateral limb swelling, sudden chest pain or dyspnea, fever, skin breakdown, and respiratory fluid overload because nephrotic complications can become emergencies.

  • Reconcile OTC NSAIDs, supplements, infection exposures, and interacting medicines before RAAS blockade, glucocorticoid, or calcineurin-inhibitor therapy.

  • Teach sodium guidance, home blood pressure and weight tracking, adherence, pregnancy precautions, infection reporting, and avoidance of unapproved nephrotoxins.

Red Flags

  • Oliguria, rapidly rising creatinine, severe hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, or hypoxemia

  • Unilateral limb swelling, pleuritic chest pain, sudden dyspnea, hemoptysis, or syncope suggesting thrombosis or pulmonary embolism

  • Fever, sepsis signs, or rapidly worsening infection during immunosuppression

  • Severe hypertension, neurologic symptoms, or medication toxicity

  • Marked intravascular depletion, hypotension, or syncope during diuresis

Complications

  • Progressive chronic kidney disease and kidney failure

  • Venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism

  • Severe edema, pleural effusion, skin injury, infection, and malnutrition

  • Dyslipidemia and increased cardiovascular risk

  • Glucocorticoid or calcineurin-inhibitor toxicity and recurrent FSGS after kidney transplantation

Prognosis

Outcome varies substantially by cause, baseline kidney function, and proteinuria response. Complete or partial proteinuria remission is associated with better kidney survival, while persistent nephrotic-range loss and declining GFR carry a high risk of kidney failure. A genetic or adaptive lesion should not be judged by response expectations developed for primary disease.

Prevention

No strategy prevents every form. Protect nephron mass by controlling blood pressure and proteinuria, addressing obesity or other adaptive drivers, treating relevant infection, avoiding known offending drugs and nephrotoxins, and screening at-risk relatives when a pathogenic genetic variant is established.

Patient Education

  • FSGS describes a pattern of scarring; the cause determines whether immune treatment is helpful.

  • Record weight and blood pressure as instructed and report a rapid gain, reduced urine, worsening swelling, or breathlessness.

  • Do not start NSAIDs, supplements, or pregnancy without discussing kidney and medication risks with the care team.

  • Report fever, unilateral leg swelling, chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or signs of medication toxicity urgently.

Special Populations

  • Children require current pediatric nephrotic-syndrome guidance and careful growth, vaccine, infection, and steroid-toxicity monitoring.

  • Pregnancy requires nephrology and maternal-fetal medicine coordination and discontinuation of fetotoxic RAAS blockers before or as soon as pregnancy is recognized.

  • Genetic counseling is central for familial disease, living-related donor assessment, and recurrence-risk discussions.

  • Transplant recipients need early surveillance for recurrent proteinuria and prompt evaluation of graft dysfunction.

Nclex Traps

  • FSGS is a biopsy pattern with several causes, not one diagnosis that always receives steroids.

  • Edema does not prove intravascular volume excess; assess perfusion before intensifying diuresis.

  • Primary FSGS with nephrotic syndrome is the immunosuppression context; adaptive or genetic disease is not treated by copying that plan.

  • New chest pain or dyspnea in severe nephrotic syndrome raises concern for thromboembolism, not only fluid overload.
