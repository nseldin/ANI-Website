Pathology

Loss of blood flow deprives neurons and glia of oxygen and glucose. In the most poorly perfused core, energy failure disrupts membranes and causes irreversible injury. Collateral-fed penumbra loses normal electrical function first but may recover if reperfused. The vessel location determines which cortical, subcortical, brainstem, cerebellar, thalamic, or visual networks fail, while collateral quality determines how quickly damage spreads.

Etiology

Mechanisms include cardioembolism from atrial fibrillation or cardiac thrombus, artery-to-artery embolism from carotid or vertebral plaque, local large-artery atherosclerotic thrombosis, penetrating small-vessel disease, cervical or intracranial dissection, cancer-associated thrombosis, vasculopathy, and less common genetic or inflammatory causes. A cryptogenic label means no cause was found after an adequate evaluation, not that the event had no mechanism.

Signs Symptoms

  • Sudden unilateral face, arm, or leg weakness or numbness; aphasia or dysarthria; visual-field loss; gaze deviation; neglect; or loss of coordination suggests focal arterial ischemia.

  • Diplopia, dysphagia, severe imbalance, nystagmus, crossed findings, quadriparesis, or reduced consciousness suggests posterior circulation ischemia.

  • Symptoms may fluctuate or improve when collateral flow changes, but improvement does not prove durable recanalization or remove recurrence risk.

Diagnostics

  • Check glucose and document LKW, baseline function, disabling deficit, NIHSS items, BP, anticoagulants, and recent procedures while emergency imaging proceeds in parallel.

  • NCCT or appropriate MRI excludes hemorrhage and estimates ischemic burden; CTA or MRA identifies LVO, stenosis, tandem disease, and dissection.

  • CTP, DWI/ADC, DWI-FLAIR, or perfusion mismatch supports selected tissue-based pathways, while ECG, telemetry, echocardiography, vascular imaging, and targeted testing establish cause for prevention.

Treatments

  • Give eligible IV thrombolysis as rapidly as safely possible because each delay allows penumbra to become infarct core.

  • Activate mechanical thrombectomy for eligible LVO without delaying to watch for IVT improvement because a large clot may require direct removal.

  • After acute reperfusion decisions, use mechanism-specific antithrombotic, vascular-risk, carotid, cardiac, lifestyle, and rehabilitation strategies because cardioembolic, large-artery, and small-vessel strokes recur through different pathways.

Nursing Priorities

  • Identify sudden focal neurologic deficits, establish exact last-known-well and baseline function, check glucose, and activate emergency stroke transport because viable threatened brain is lost as arterial occlusion persists.

  • Prepare noncontrast brain and vascular imaging while obtaining anticoagulant, bleeding, surgery, medication, and comorbidity history because hemorrhage exclusion and vessel status determine safe thrombolysis and thrombectomy pathways.

  • Maintain nothing-by-mouth status until swallowing is screened, position and protect weak limbs, and prevent fever, hypoxia, and severe glucose disturbance because aspiration and physiologic stress expand secondary injury.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, blood pressure, rhythm, oxygenation, glucose, swallowing, temperature, urine, and reperfusion complications because edema, hemorrhagic transformation, recurrent ischemia, and atrial fibrillation can change care quickly.

  • Escalate for NIH Stroke Scale increase, declining consciousness, unequal pupils, severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, airway compromise, aspiration, or blood pressure outside the treatment-specific target because progression, swelling, hemorrhage, and loss of reperfusion eligibility are time critical.

Complications

  • Infarct expansion, hemorrhagic transformation, malignant edema, seizure, re-occlusion, and herniation can worsen the primary injury.

  • Aspiration pneumonia, DVT/PE, falls, pressure injury, malnutrition, depression, pain, spasticity, and contracture can add preventable disability.

  • Aphasia, neglect, hemianopia, weakness, dysphagia, cognitive change, fatigue, and recurrent stroke can remain after survival.

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for sudden neurologic symptoms even when they resolve because transient improvement does not exclude an unstable cerebral artery.

  • Do not drive to the hospital or eat, drink, or take aspirin first because emergency transport and imaging preserve treatment options safely.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal early NCCT does not rule out ischemic stroke or show that a large vessel is open.

  • IV thrombolysis and thrombectomy can be parallel, complementary treatments.

  • The penumbra is threatened and time-limited; the predicted core is a probabilistic imaging estimate, not a label for unquestionably dead tissue.

Definition

Ischemic stroke is sudden neurologic dysfunction caused by critical reduction or interruption of arterial blood flow to a region of brain, usually from embolus or thrombosis. The occluded artery creates an infarct core where injury is already severe and a surrounding penumbra that is dysfunctional but may remain viable through collateral circulation. Treatment aims to restore flow before the core expands, while simultaneously protecting airway, glucose, oxygenation, perfusion, temperature, and swallowing safety. Deficit pattern localizes the network; vascular and tissue imaging identifies the artery and remaining treatment opportunity.

Pathophysiology

ATP depletion stops sodium-potassium pumps, producing depolarization, glutamate release, calcium entry, free-radical injury, cytotoxic edema, inflammation, and blood-brain-barrier disruption. Reperfusion through alteplase, tenecteplase, spontaneous recanalization, or mechanical thrombectomy can rescue viable penumbra, but it cannot regenerate established core and may expose fragile microvessels to hemorrhagic transformation. This explains why rapid treatment helps, why advanced imaging can select some unknown-onset or later patients, and why neurologic and BP monitoring remains intensive after the artery is opened. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline supports alteplase or tenecteplase for eligible disabling deficits through 4.5 hours and selected advanced-imaging IVT or EVT pathways beyond that window; eligibility remains individualized.

Risk Factors

  • Atrial fibrillation and structural cardiac disease create emboli that can abruptly occlude a cerebral artery.

  • Hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and atherosclerosis injure large and small vessels and promote thrombosis.

  • Dissection, cancer, pregnancy or postpartum physiology, autoimmune vasculopathy, thrombophilia, stimulant exposure, and sickle cell disease become especially important when the ordinary risk profile does not fit.

Labs

  • Glucose below 60 mg/dL requires treatment because hypoglycemia both mimics and harms brain; intensive 80-to-130 mg/dL control is avoided because severe hypoglycemia rises without outcome benefit.

  • CBC, platelets, coagulation studies, renal function, oxygenation, and medication exposure inform bleeding, contrast, and reperfusion safety under the current protocol.

  • A1c, lipids, rhythm data, and mechanism-directed testing guide secondary prevention rather than acute diagnosis alone.

Contraindications

  • Do not give thrombolysis until intracranial hemorrhage is excluded and the current eligibility pathway is satisfied.

  • Do not equate low NIHSS with nondisabling stroke because isolated aphasia, hemianopia, dominant-hand weakness, or posterior gait failure can be devastating.

  • Do not delay standard-window IVT for advanced imaging that is not required, and do not withhold IVT solely because thrombectomy is planned.

Red Flags

  • Sudden aphasia, facial droop, unilateral weakness, visual loss, neglect, or severe ataxia

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, recurrent deficit, or rapid neurologic deterioration

  • Declining consciousness, unequal pupils, posturing, severe headache, or repeated vomiting

  • Aspiration, inability to protect the airway, severe hypoxemia, seizure, or unstable blood pressure
