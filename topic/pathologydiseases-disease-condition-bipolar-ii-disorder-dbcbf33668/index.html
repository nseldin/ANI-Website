Plain Language

Bipolar II disorder includes both major depressive episodes and hypomanic episodes. Hypomania is a clear change in mood, energy, activity, and sleep that other people can notice but that does not reach the marked impairment, hospitalization, or psychosis of mania; depressive episodes often cause the greatest disability.

Why It Matters

Bipolar II is not a mild form of bipolar disorder. Depression, mixed agitation, impulsivity, and suicide risk can be severe, and treating the depression as unipolar illness without reviewing the lifetime course can worsen activation or delay effective care.

Definition

DSM-5-TR Bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and one major depressive episode, with no lifetime manic episode. DSM hypomania lasts at least 4 consecutive days and produces an observable, unequivocal change without marked impairment, hospitalization, or psychosis; ICD-11 uses its own several-days framework. Psychosis during an elevated episode makes that episode manic, while psychosis confined to a depressive episode does not by itself convert the diagnosis to Bipolar I disorder. A screening score or brief energized period is insufficient.

Pathology

Bipolar II disorder is not defined by a structural lesion, serum marker, or one neurotransmitter abnormality. The clinically observable pathology is recurrent dysregulation of mood, reward, arousal, sleep, circadian timing, cognition, and activity across episodes. Reduced sleep can signal and amplify an upward shift, while recurrent depression can dominate disability; diagnosis depends on the longitudinal episode pattern and exclusion of substance, medication, and medical causes.

Pathophysiology

  • During hypomania, arousal, reward seeking, goal-directed activity, confidence, speech, and wakefulness rise together in a distinct change from baseline.

  • During bipolar depression, reward, energy, cognition, sleep, appetite, and psychomotor regulation may fall or become agitated; mixed activation can increase distress, impulsivity, and safety risk.

Etiology

Genetic and biologic vulnerability interacts with sleep loss, circadian disruption, stress, postpartum change, substances, stimulants or steroids, and medical illness. A family history or medication-associated activation raises clinical suspicion but does not independently establish Bipolar II disorder.

Risk Factors

  • A family or personal history of bipolar-spectrum illness, recurrent depression, early-onset depression, or prior self-harm or suicide attempt

  • Antidepressant-associated activation, mixed symptoms, postpartum episodes, or repeated episodes linked to sleep and circadian disruption

  • Alcohol or other substance use, stimulant or steroid exposure, major stress, and medical or neurologic illness that complicates the mood course

Signs Symptoms

  • Major depressive episodes with low mood or anhedonia, impaired function, sleep or appetite change, low energy, guilt, concentration difficulty, or suicidal thinking

  • Distinct periods of increased energy or activity with reduced need for sleep, increased talkativeness, confidence, sociability, productivity, distractibility, irritability, or impulsive decisions

  • Mixed depressive agitation, recurrent polarity changes, and observable changes from baseline reported by the patient or collateral sources

Diagnostics

  • Establish a lifetime timeline of depressive, hypomanic, manic, psychotic, substance-related, postpartum, and treatment-associated episodes, including duration and recovery between episodes.

  • Assess baseline behavior, functional consequences, hospitalization, collateral observations, medicines and substances, family history, medical mimics, and current suicide risk.

  • Mood Disorder Questionnaire or hypomania checklists can support case finding but do not independently diagnose Bipolar II disorder or distinguish it from other episodic conditions.

Labs

  • There is no laboratory test or imaging result that confirms Bipolar II disorder.

  • Use targeted thyroid, metabolic, toxicology, pregnancy, ECG, renal, hepatic, CBC, calcium, or neurologic testing according to the differential and selected treatment.

  • Medication concentrations and safety studies monitor a treatment such as lithium; they do not prove the psychiatric diagnosis.

Assessment

Ask about sleep need rather than hours alone; energy, activity, speech, spending, sexuality, conflict, risky behavior, irritability, psychosis, depression, mixed symptoms, suicide, substances, reproductive goals, organ disease, and prior medication response. Obtain patient-supported collateral and follow the pattern across time without treating enthusiasm, temperament, or one sleepless night as hypomania.

Differential

  • Bipolar I disorder, major depressive disorder, cyclothymic disorder, schizoaffective disorder, or another psychotic or mood disorder

  • Borderline personality disorder, ADHD, PTSD, anxiety, grief, or developmentally and culturally expected variation

  • Substance- or medication-induced mood disorder, sleep deprivation, hyperthyroidism, neurologic disease, delirium, and other medical causes

Treatments

  • Use structured psychoeducation, relapse planning, regular sleep and routine support, and bipolar-focused psychotherapy according to need, access, and preference.

  • Quetiapine is a guideline-supported bipolar-depression option; lithium or lamotrigine may be selected according to phase, prior response, recurrence pattern, organ function, pregnancy, adverse effects, and monitoring.

  • Evidence frequently combines Bipolar I and II populations, so do not invent a Bipolar-II-only treatment ranking. Antidepressants are not a reflex stand-alone treatment and require a specialist mood-state plan and monitoring when selected.

Contraindications

  • Do not continue to classify the illness as Bipolar II after a confirmed manic episode; reassess as Bipolar I disorder.

  • Check current agent-specific contraindications and labeling. Lithium requires renal, thyroid, calcium, fluid, interaction, and pregnancy review; lamotrigine requires prescribed titration and urgent rash assessment.

  • Lamotrigine is not an acute antimanic rescue drug, and an antidepressant should not be started reflexively without bipolar and activation assessment.

Safety Considerations

  • Track mood polarity, sleep need, energy, impulsivity, psychosis, suicide risk, adherence, substance use, and access to potentially lethal medication quantities.

  • Monitor lithium concentrations and toxicity context; weight, blood pressure, glucose, lipids, sedation, orthostasis, and movement effects with quetiapine; and rash or mucosal symptoms with lamotrigine.

  • Reproductive counseling and pregnancy planning must follow the actual medicine and jurisdiction-specific safety requirements.

Nursing Priorities

  • Maintain immediate safety and assess suicide, mixed agitation, psychosis, risky behavior, substance use, and ability to sleep, eat, hydrate, and complete essential care.

  • Reduce stimulation during escalation, provide consistent boundaries, support sleep and nutrition, reconcile interacting medicines, and obtain ordered baseline and follow-up studies.

  • Teach early-warning signs and toxicity cues, document change from baseline, and communicate emerging mania or medication harm promptly.

Red Flags

  • Suicide plan or intent, recent attempt, severe mixed agitation, or rapidly increasing impulsive behavior

  • Rapidly decreasing need for sleep with escalating activity, marked impairment, hospitalization-level severity, or psychosis suggesting mania

  • Lithium-associated vomiting, coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion, or seizure; or lamotrigine rash with blistering, mucosal lesions, fever, or systemic illness

Complications

  • Suicide, self-harm, substance-related injury, financial or relationship harm, unsafe driving or sexual behavior, and occupational loss

  • Treatment toxicity, metabolic disease, hospitalization, and delayed correct treatment after unipolar misclassification

  • Recurrent depressive disability, mixed episodes, and loss of social or daily function

Prognosis

Bipolar II disorder is recurrent but treatable. Course varies with episode burden, mixed symptoms, suicide risk, comorbidity, treatment response, sleep and substance patterns, physical health, and access to care. Recovery includes stable sleep and mood, reduced recurrence, safer decisions, and restored function rather than elimination of every ordinary mood change.

Prevention

  • Use a written early-warning and relapse plan that identifies individual sleep, energy, mood, and behavior changes and the next clinical contact.

  • Support medication adherence, regular sleep and routines, substance reduction, physical-health monitoring, and rapid review after antidepressant activation or polarity change.

  • Plan pregnancy and postpartum care before medication changes whenever possible and maintain close follow-up after hospital or crisis transitions.

Patient Education

  • Bipolar II disorder is not just depression or a mild form of bipolar illness; hypomania must be a distinct change from the person's usual state.

  • Chart sleep, mood, energy, and impulsive behavior and seek help early for psychosis, suicide risk, rapidly reduced sleep, or escalating activity.

  • Maintain stable salt and fluid intake if taking lithium, report rash promptly with lamotrigine, and never change prescribed treatment abruptly.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require specialist longitudinal assessment because ADHD, trauma, development, substances, and ordinary variation can resemble mood activation.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum periods require preconception planning and urgent response to mania, psychosis, severe depression, or suicide risk.

  • Older adults need renal, thyroid, falls, interaction, cognitive, and medical-mimic review; shift work and substance use can obscure the episode pattern in any age group.

Nclex Traps

  • Hypomania still represents illness and Bipolar II disorder is not automatically mild.

  • A manic episode changes the classification to Bipolar I; psychosis during depression alone does not make that change.

  • Lithium may be used for mood stabilization, but current suicide-risk guidance does not establish it as a stand-alone suicide-prevention treatment.
