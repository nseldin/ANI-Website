Definition

Rhonchi are low-pitched continuous breath sounds caused by air moving through secretions or narrowed larger airways. The sound may resemble snoring, rattling, or gurgling.

Plain Language

Rhonchi are low rattling or snoring lung sounds, often related to mucus in larger breathing tubes.

Why It Matters

Rhonchi can reveal retained secretions or larger-airway narrowing and may change after coughing, but they do not identify one disease. Poor cough, falling oxygen, severe breathing effort, or decreasing air movement requires prompt escalation.

Associated Conditions

  • secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways

Assessment Method

Auscultate paired lung fields through inspiration and expiration, document pitch, timing, distribution, symmetry, air movement, cough and sputum, and whether the sound changes after a safe spontaneous cough or airway-clearance intervention.

Positive Finding

A low-pitched continuous snoring, rattling, or gurgling sound is heard during inspiration, expiration, or both and may change after coughing.

Mechanism

Air moving through secretions or an irregular narrowed larger airway makes the airway contents and walls vibrate continuously.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Rhonchi response raises concern for secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways because air moving through secretions or an irregular narrowed larger airway makes the airway contents and walls vibrate continuously. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Rhonchi does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Rhonchi to focus evaluation of secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways and the reviewed alternatives: coarse crackles, wheezing, pleural friction rub, transmitted upper-airway noise. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Auscultate paired lung fields through inspiration and expiration, document pitch, timing, distribution, symmetry, air movement, cough and sputum, and whether the sound changes after a safe spontaneous cough or airway-clearance intervention. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Rhonchi as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Different clinicians use rhonchi for low-pitched wheeze or secretion-related gurgling, and transmitted upper-airway noise can mimic the finding. Therefore, Rhonchi cannot confirm or exclude secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • coarse crackles

  • wheezing

  • pleural friction rub

  • transmitted upper-airway noise

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from coarse crackles, wheezing, pleural friction rub, transmitted upper-airway noise, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Rhonchi may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current descriptive breath sound with inconsistent historical terminology; document the acoustic quality and response to coughing instead of relying on the word rhonchi alone. Rhonchi remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways. Apply the reviewed limitation for Rhonchi: Different clinicians use rhonchi for low-pitched wheeze or secretion-related gurgling, and transmitted upper-airway noise can mimic the finding.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Rhonchi, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Auscultate paired lung fields through inspiration and expiration, document pitch, timing, distribution, symmetry, air movement, cough and sputum, and whether the sound changes after a safe spontaneous cough or airway-clearance intervention. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Escalate rhonchi with severe work of breathing, hypoxemia, exhaustion, altered mental status, ineffective cough, copious secretions, or rapidly decreasing air movement; a temporary change after coughing does not exclude pneumonia, obstruction, or respiratory failure.

Communicate the finding with its suspected association, secretions, edema, inflammation, or dynamic narrowing in larger airways, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

For Rhonchi, apply this exact authored caution when deciding whom to notify and how quickly: Escalate rhonchi with severe work of breathing, hypoxemia, exhaustion, altered mental status, ineffective cough, copious secretions, or rapidly decreasing air movement; a temporary change after coughing does not exclude pneumonia, obstruction, or respiratory failure.
