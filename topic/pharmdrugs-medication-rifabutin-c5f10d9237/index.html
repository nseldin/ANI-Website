Class

Rifamycin antimycobacterial RNA-polymerase inhibitor with less CYP induction than rifampin

Used To Treat

Prevention of disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex in patients with advanced HIV under U.S. labeling and guideline-based treatment of MAC or tuberculosis as part of multidrug regimens, particularly when rifampin interactions are problematic. It must never be used alone for active TB or MAC disease.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Never use rifabutin alone for active TB or MAC disease because rapid rpoB resistance can emerge. Screen for active TB before MAC prophylaxis. CYP3A interactions can cause underexposure and resistance or overexposure with uveitis and neutropenia. Stop and urgently evaluate eye pain, photophobia, blurred vision, marked neutropenia, jaundice, severe rash, or systemic hypersensitivity. Orange-red urine, sweat, saliva, and tears are expected and can permanently stain soft contact lenses.

Mechanism

Rifabutin diffuses into mycobacteria and binds the rpoB-encoded beta subunit of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase near the RNA/DNA channel. The bound drug sterically blocks extension of nascent RNA after a few nucleotides, suppressing transcription and protein synthesis. Human nuclear RNA polymerases have lower affinity, creating microbial selectivity. A single rpoB mutation can produce high-level resistance, which is why active infection requires combination therapy and why inadvertent rifamycin monotherapy can compromise the entire class. Rifabutin activates pregnane-X receptor and induces CYP3A and transporters less strongly than rifampin, but CYP3A also metabolizes rifabutin; inhibitors can raise parent drug and 25-O-desacetyl metabolite concentrations enough to inflame the uveal tract and suppress marrow.

Nursing Essentials

  • Reconcile antiretrovirals, azoles, macrolides, immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, contraceptives, and antiseizure drugs before dosing. Interaction management often requires changing both dose and monitoring.

  • Teach the color effect before treatment, but distinguish benign orange tears from uveitis: pain, photophobia, floaters, or blurred vision need urgent eye assessment.

Contraindications

  • Serious rifamycin hypersensitivity and medication combinations that cannot be safely adjusted.

  • Do not start prophylaxis until active TB is reasonably excluded; suppressing with one rifamycin can select resistance.

Interactions

  • Strong CYP3A inhibitors such as clarithromycin or azoles can raise rifabutin and uveitis/neutropenia risk; inducers can reduce exposure.

  • Rifabutin can lower concentrations of CYP3A substrates including hormonal contraceptives and selected antiretrovirals, though less than rifampin.

Key Labs

  • CBC with differential, liver tests, renal function, HIV/immune status, mycobacterial cultures/susceptibility and interaction-specific drug levels when indicated

  • Visual symptoms/ophthalmic exam, secretion color teaching, adherence to the complete regimen, fever/diarrhea, treatment response, and resistance

Nclex Traps

  • Rifabutin has fewer CYP interactions than rifampin, not zero interactions.

  • Orange-red secretions are expected; eye pain or blurred vision suggests uveitis and is not expected discoloration.

Description

Rifabutin is a rifamycin that binds the beta subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase and prevents RNA-chain elongation, shutting down mycobacterial protein synthesis. It has a long tissue half-life and activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and MAC. Compared with rifampin, rifabutin generally induces CYP3A less strongly, which can make it useful with selected antiretroviral or transplant regimens. Less induction does not mean no interaction: rifabutin is itself a CYP3A substrate, so inhibitors can raise exposure and cause uveitis, neutropenia, arthralgia, or hepatitis, while inducers can lower drug and permit failure. Orange-red secretions are expected, but painful red eyes or blurred vision are not.

Adverse Effects

  • Orange-red secretions, GI upset, rash, arthralgia, taste change, and liver-enzyme elevation

  • Uveitis, neutropenia/leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, severe hypersensitivity, C. difficile infection, and resistance from inadequate combination therapy
