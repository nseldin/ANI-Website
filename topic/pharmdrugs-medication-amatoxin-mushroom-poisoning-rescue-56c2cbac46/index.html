Used To Treat

Recognition and coordinated emergency management of suspected amatoxin-containing mushroom ingestion. No standardized FDA-approved antidote exists; early poison-center consultation, serial liver and coagulation studies, supportive care, consideration of off-label or investigational therapies, and early transplant-center involvement are the core pathway.

Description

Amatoxin poisoning often begins with delayed vomiting and diarrhea, appears to improve, and then progresses to hepatocellular failure, coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, encephalopathy, kidney injury, and death. The symptom-free interval is not recovery: toxin has entered hepatocytes before liver failure is clinically obvious. Management therefore begins from exposure timing and species suspicion rather than waiting for jaundice. Activated charcoal, N-acetylcysteine, intravenous silibinin through emergency access, and other therapies have been used, but none removes the need for early transplant evaluation.

Mechanism

Amatoxins such as alpha-amanitin enter hepatocytes and inhibit RNA polymerase II, shutting down messenger-RNA synthesis. Protein production then fails, rapidly turning over intestinal cells are injured first, and metabolically active hepatocytes later undergo apoptosis and necrosis. Biliary excretion and intestinal reabsorption can prolong exposure, while apparent gastrointestinal recovery occurs before hepatic synthetic failure peaks. This sequence explains delayed aminotransferase rise, falling glucose and clotting factors, recurrent toxicity, and why early charcoal or uptake-directed investigational therapy is considered before organ failure is obvious.

Administration Timing

  • Call Poison Help or medical toxicology immediately, obtain a detailed mushroom and meal history, preserve specimens or photographs safely, and do not wait for species confirmation when delayed gastrointestinal illness follows a wild-mushroom meal.

  • Begin serial glucose, liver, coagulation, renal, acid-base, and neurologic assessment; use specialist-directed charcoal or metabolic support when appropriate and evaluate emergency access to IV silibinin without delaying standard care.

  • Contact a liver-transplant center early when exposure is credible or laboratory injury begins because transfer after refractory shock, cerebral edema, or advanced multiorgan failure may be too late.

Nursing Essentials

  • Treat the symptom-free interval as a dangerous phase and maintain observation because RNA and protein-synthesis failure continues after diarrhea improves.

  • Record every co-diner, portion, onset time, symptom pattern, specimen, and source location because others may need evaluation before symptoms and public-health identification can prevent additional meals.

  • Trend glucose frequently and institute bleeding, aspiration, encephalopathy, and fall precautions because hepatic synthetic and metabolic failure can deteriorate abruptly.

  • Coordinate early transport to a transplant-capable center because no medication reliably reverses established fulminant hepatic failure.

Key Labs

  • Serial AST, ALT, bilirubin, PT/INR, fibrinogen, factor V when used by the transplant center, glucose, lactate, blood gas, ammonia, creatinine, electrolytes, phosphorus, CBC, and urinalysis; trend direction and rate rather than one normal early panel.

  • Obtain type and screen, pregnancy testing when relevant, an acetaminophen level and other coexposure studies, continuous glucose and neurologic assessment, urine output, and transplant-prognostic variables because occult coexposure, hypoglycemia, renal decline, or worsening prognostic trends can change rescue and transfer decisions before jaundice appears.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning complications include dehydration, electrolyte loss, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, coagulopathy, hemorrhage, encephalopathy, cerebral edema, acute kidney injury, shock, and multiorgan failure.

  • Rescue therapies add aspiration risk from charcoal, anaphylactoid reactions from acetylcysteine, infusion uncertainty from investigational silibinin, and procedural or immunologic risks from transplantation.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce vomiting or use household mushroom remedies; emesis adds aspiration risk and cannot retrieve toxin already absorbed.

  • Do not discharge or stop monitoring because gastrointestinal symptoms improve or early aminotransferases are normal; the latent hepatic phase is a defining feature.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Rising INR, hypoglycemia, lactate, bilirubin or ammonia; falling factor V or fibrinogen; oliguria; acidosis; confusion; bleeding; or hemodynamic instability requires immediate transplant-center and intensive-care escalation.

  • A co-diner who remains asymptomatic still needs poison-center-directed assessment because latency varies and early treatment opportunities precede organ failure.

Interactions

  • Multiple-dose activated charcoal may interrupt enterohepatic recycling in selected protected-airway patients, but aspiration, ileus, or bowel obstruction can make it harmful and it must follow toxicology guidance.

  • N-acetylcysteine, IV silibinin, and historical high-dose penicillin regimens are not proven interchangeable or cumulatively curative because timing, exposure uncertainty, and concurrent therapies confound outcome data; combining treatments can also obscure adverse effects and must not delay transfer or transplant evaluation.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A shared mushroom portion creates a larger weight-adjusted toxin exposure, while dehydration and hypoglycemia develop quickly; evaluate exposed children early even if adults initially appear sicker.

  • Population

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Maternal hepatic failure threatens both patient and fetus, and therapy evidence is sparse; stabilize and involve toxicology, maternal-fetal medicine, and transplant teams without delaying maternal rescue.

  • Population

    Late presenters or patients with chronic liver or kidney disease

    Note

    Reduced organ reserve and missed early decontamination narrow the rescue window, so transfer and transplant assessment should accelerate rather than waiting for standard thresholds to worsen.

Evidence Limitations

  • CDC notes that no FDA-approved standardized treatment exists. Evidence for silibinin, acetylcysteine, penicillin, and charcoal comes mainly from observational reports, case series, and confounded combinations.

  • Mushroom identification is often uncertain, and amatoxin concentrations are not rapidly available; management is therefore syndrome-, timing-, and trend-based.

Nclex Traps

  • Improvement after delayed severe diarrhea can be the deceptive latent phase, not recovery.

  • A normal early liver panel does not exclude amatoxin poisoning.

  • No candidate antidote replaces early transplant-center involvement.

Regulatory Status

Emergency toxicology and transplant-support pathway with no FDA-approved standardized amatoxin antidote. IV silibinin remains investigational in the United States; other pharmacologic uses are off label.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): delayed gastrointestinal illness after a death-cap or destroying-angel exposure can progress to fatal liver failure after apparent improvement. Contact Poison Help and a transplant-capable center early; do not wait for jaundice or a high INR.

Boxed Warning Specificity

Specific educational safety warning; verify whether the current product carries an FDA boxed warning.
