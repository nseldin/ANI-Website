Plain Language

Pica is persistent eating of substances that are not food and have no nutritional value, such as soil, paint, paper, clay, hair, or metal. It is diagnosed only when the behavior is not expected for the person's developmental level and is not a culturally accepted practice.

Why It Matters

The danger depends on what was eaten: pica can cause lead or other poisoning, choking, intestinal blockage or perforation, infection, dental injury, electrolyte problems, and death. It may also signal iron or another nutrient deficiency, pregnancy-related change, developmental disability, stress, or an unsafe environment that needs direct action.

Definition

Pica is the persistent eating of one or more nonnutritive, nonfood substances for at least one month under DSM-5-TR when the behavior is inappropriate to developmental level, not part of a culturally supported practice, and clinically important enough for separate attention if it occurs with pregnancy, another mental disorder, or a medical condition. The diagnosis describes repeated behavior; one accidental ingestion or developmentally normal mouthing does not establish pica.

Pathology

There is no single pica lesion. Injury is substance-specific: lead-containing paint or dust can cause neurotoxicity; soil can carry parasites or contaminants; sharp, caustic, magnetic, or metallic objects can burn, perforate, obstruct, or poison; hair can form a bezoar; ice chewing may accompany iron deficiency; and repeated abrasion can injure teeth.

Pathophysiology

  • Mechanisms vary. Iron or zinc deficiency may be associated with pica, but deficiency is not present in every case and the direction of causation is not always clear.

  • Developmental disability, autism, sensory reinforcement, compulsive or stress-related behavior, pregnancy, neglect, and environmental access can contribute or maintain ingestion.

  • Toxicity and obstruction can progress without obvious early symptoms, especially in a person who cannot describe what or how much was swallowed.

Etiology

Pica is heterogeneous. Nutrient deficiency, pregnancy, developmental or intellectual disability, autism, psychosis or obsessive-compulsive symptoms, stress, neglect, food insecurity, cultural practice, and easy access to a preferred substance may be relevant. Clinicians must assess rather than assume a cause from the substance or the patient's identity.

Risk Factors

  • Young age with behavior beyond expected mouthing, pregnancy, iron deficiency or other nutrient deficiency

  • Intellectual disability, autism, developmental delay, limited communication, or sensory-seeking behavior

  • Psychiatric illness, severe stress, neglect, institutional environment, food insecurity, or limited supervision

  • Older housing, renovation dust, contaminated soil, imported products, occupational take-home exposure, or access to magnets, batteries, sharp objects, caustics, medications, or other toxins

Signs Symptoms

  • Repeated eating of soil, clay, paint, paper, hair, cloth, soap, starch, ice, metal, feces, ash, or another nonfood material

  • Cravings, hidden collections, damaged household items, unexplained missing objects, or ingestion observed by caregivers

  • Abdominal pain or distention, constipation, vomiting, blood in stool, reduced appetite, dental wear, choking, cough, fever, or weight and nutrition change

  • Fatigue, pallor, headache, developmental or behavior change, neuropathic symptoms, or other clues to iron deficiency, lead exposure, or another toxin

  • The patient may be asymptomatic despite clinically important exposure

Diagnostics

  • Identify the exact substance, source, amount, frequency, duration, last ingestion, co-ingestants, preparation, accessibility, witnesses, developmental level, cultural meaning, pregnancy, symptoms, and intent. Contact poison control or emergency services for a potentially toxic, sharp, magnetic, battery, caustic, or unknown ingestion.

  • Examine airway and breathing, vital signs, neurologic and abdominal status, hydration, oral and dental injury, development, growth, pallor, and signs of poisoning, obstruction, perforation, infection, or neglect.

  • Apply current DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 requirements only after distinguishing persistent pica from accidental ingestion, developmentally expected mouthing, food consumption, culturally accepted practice, self-harm or poisoning, psychosis, obsessive-compulsive behavior, and substance-specific medical causes.

  • Assess environmental and safeguarding risk. A diagnosis of pica does not replace investigation of lead, abuse, food deprivation, unsafe housing, or caregiver support.

Labs

  • Testing is substance- and symptom-specific. Common considerations include complete blood count and iron studies, blood lead level for credible lead exposure, electrolytes and renal or liver measures for relevant substances, and zinc or other nutrient testing when clinically supported.

  • Use abdominal or other imaging, endoscopy, stool or parasite testing, toxicology, ECG, or surgical evaluation when the ingested material, symptoms, or exposure pattern supports it; not every material is visible on plain radiography.

  • Do not wait for symptoms or a pica diagnosis before obtaining time-sensitive poison guidance for a dangerous ingestion.

Assessment

First treat the event as an ingestion emergency when the substance could be toxic, caustic, sharp, magnetic, obstructing, perforating, or unknown, or when choking, respiratory symptoms, severe abdominal findings, altered mental status, seizure, or shock is present. Once acute danger is addressed, assess persistent behavior, nutrient deficiency, development, psychiatric and pregnancy context, environment, supervision, and function.

Differential

Differentiate pica from developmentally normal mouthing, culturally accepted ingestion, food craving, accidental foreign-body ingestion, deliberate self-harm or poisoning, psychosis, obsessive-compulsive disorder, autism-related repetitive behavior without ingestion, factitious behavior, substance intoxication, and hunger related to food scarcity. Identify coexisting iron deficiency, lead exposure, parasites, gastrointestinal disease, pregnancy, or developmental disability without assuming they fully explain the behavior.

Treatments

  • Stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, poisoning, obstruction, perforation, infection, and dental injury according to the exact substance and current condition. Use poison-control, toxicology, gastroenterology, surgery, dentistry, or public-health expertise when indicated.

  • Remove or secure hazardous materials, repair environmental exposure when possible, improve supervision and safe alternatives, and use a written behavior and emergency plan that fits the person's developmental and communication needs.

  • Treat confirmed iron, zinc, or other deficiency with the appropriate replacement and investigate its cause; supplementation is not a universal cure when deficiency is absent.

  • Use individualized behavioral assessment and intervention for persistent pica, including reinforcement of safe competing behavior and caregiver training. Treat coexisting developmental, psychiatric, pregnancy, dental, or social needs.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce vomiting or give food, drink, neutralizing agents, laxatives, or activated charcoal unless directed for the exact ingestion by poison-control or emergency clinicians.

  • Do not assume all pica is iron deficiency or give prolonged supplementation without confirming need and monitoring toxicity risk.

  • Do not use punishment, humiliation, unsafe restraint, or deprivation as behavior management.

  • Do not rely on a normal abdominal radiograph to exclude every foreign body or toxin; imaging choice depends on the material and symptoms.

Safety Considerations

  • Button batteries, multiple magnets, sharp objects, caustics, lead, medications, and unknown substances require urgent substance-specific guidance because injury can occur before symptoms.

  • Children and people with communication limitations may not report ingestion, pain, neurologic change, or amount accurately; use observation, collateral history, environmental inspection, and a low threshold for urgent evaluation.

  • Assess neglect, food deprivation, unsafe housing, abuse, and caregiver capacity without presuming blame. Use mandatory safeguarding pathways when indicated.

  • A blood lead test is required to determine lead exposure; absence of symptoms does not exclude lead harm.

Nursing Priorities

  • Identify and preserve information about the exact material, container or photo, amount, time, route, symptoms, co-ingestants, and prehospital actions; contact poison-control or emergency resources promptly when indicated.

  • Monitor airway, breathing, neurologic status, vital signs, pain, abdomen, stool or emesis, hydration, ECG and laboratory trends according to the ingestion.

  • Use nonjudgmental questions and developmentally appropriate observation to map pica behavior, triggers, access, consequences, nutrient symptoms, pregnancy, communication needs, and home safety.

  • Teach caregivers environmental control, safe substitutes, the written behavior plan, follow-up testing, and the exact symptoms or ingestions that require emergency care.

Red Flags

  • Choking, drooling, inability to swallow secretions, respiratory distress, cyanosis, stridor, or suspected aspiration

  • Severe or localized abdominal pain, rigid or distended abdomen, persistent vomiting, hematemesis, bloody stool, fever, absent stool or gas, or shock

  • Altered mental status, seizure, weakness, neuropathic symptoms, severe headache, arrhythmia, or other signs of poisoning

  • Known or possible button battery, multiple-magnet, sharp-object, caustic, lead, medication, or unknown-substance ingestion

  • Suspected abuse, neglect, food deprivation, unsafe housing exposure, deliberate self-harm, or caregiver inability to maintain immediate safety

Complications

  • Lead or other heavy-metal toxicity, medication or chemical poisoning, electrolyte disturbance, infection, parasites, and nutrient deficiency

  • Choking, aspiration, dental injury, bezoar, intestinal obstruction, ischemia, perforation, bleeding, peritonitis, and sepsis

  • Neurodevelopmental harm from toxic exposure, anemia, impaired growth, repeated procedures, and death

  • Family stress, school exclusion, stigma, unsafe restraint, and recurrence when environmental and behavioral drivers remain

Prognosis

Outcome depends on the substance, complications, developmental and psychiatric context, environmental control, and response to deficiency or behavioral treatment. Some childhood or pregnancy-related presentations remit, while pica associated with developmental disability or persistent reinforcement may require long-term prevention and monitoring. Prior improvement does not make a new hazardous ingestion safe.

Prevention

  • Keep batteries, magnets, medications, chemicals, lead-containing dust or paint, sharp objects, and preferred nonfood substances secured; correct environmental lead and housing hazards through appropriate public-health channels.

  • Identify and treat nutrient deficiency and food insecurity, provide safe sensory alternatives, and teach caregivers and schools a consistent behavior and emergency plan.

  • Continue exposure-specific follow-up, including blood lead monitoring when indicated, because toxicity may be silent.

Patient Education

  • Pica is repeated eating of nonfood material, not a moral failure. Tell the clinician exactly what was eaten even if it feels embarrassing; the material determines the danger and tests.

  • Do not make yourself vomit or try a home antidote. Call poison control or emergency services for a battery, magnets, sharp or caustic material, medicine, lead-containing item, unknown substance, or any concerning symptom.

  • Iron or another deficiency may contribute, but supplements should match confirmed need and cannot replace removal of a toxic exposure.

  • Seek emergency help for choking, trouble breathing or swallowing, severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, blood, fever, confusion, seizure, weakness, or suspected dangerous ingestion.

Special Populations

  • Young children require developmental interpretation, lead and home-safety assessment, close supervision, and safeguarding; ordinary infant mouthing is not automatically pica.

  • Pregnancy can be associated with pica and changes toxicity and nutrition implications. Ask without shame and coordinate obstetric, nutritional, and exposure care.

  • Autistic people and those with intellectual, developmental, sensory, or communication disability need accessible behavior plans, safe alternatives, environmental controls, and careful pain or toxicity observation.

  • Older adults or people with dementia, psychosis, incarceration, institutional care, or severe food insecurity need review of cognition, environment, access, neglect, and decision-specific safety.

Nclex Traps

  • One accidental ingestion or developmentally normal mouthing is not pica, but a dangerous ingestion is still an emergency without the diagnosis.

  • Do not assume pica equals iron deficiency; test and treat the actual cause and complication.

  • Do not induce vomiting after an unknown, caustic, battery, magnet, or sharp-object ingestion.

  • A child can have clinically important lead exposure without symptoms; use a blood lead test when exposure is credible.

  • Substance-specific emergency management comes before behavioral treatment.
