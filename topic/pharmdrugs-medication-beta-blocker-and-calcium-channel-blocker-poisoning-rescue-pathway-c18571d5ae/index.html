Used To Treat

Emergency management of symptomatic beta-blocker or calcium-channel-blocker poisoning with bradycardia, conduction block, hypotension, cardiogenic shock, or arrest.

Description

This pathway recognizes that beta blockers and calcium-channel blockers can both cause bradycardia and shock, but their additional features and rescue responses differ. Support airway and circulation, obtain ECG and bedside glucose, use vasopressors and agent-directed calcium or glucagon when appropriate, and begin high-dose insulin early for severe cardiogenic shock. AHA toxicology guidance describes regular insulin 1 unit/kg IV bolus followed by 1 to 10 units/kg/hour with dextrose and intensive glucose and potassium monitoring because these supraphysiologic insulin doses can rapidly lower glucose and shift potassium into cells. Glucagon 2 to 10 mg can be reasonable in beta-blocker poisoning, while calcium can be reasonable in calcium-channel-blocker poisoning; neither should delay vasopressor, insulin, or VA-ECMO escalation.

Mechanism

Beta-blockade reduces cyclic AMP, heart rate, conduction, and contractility; propranolol also blocks sodium channels and can cause seizure or QRS widening. Calcium-channel blockade reduces calcium entry into myocardium, conduction tissue, vascular smooth muscle, and pancreatic beta cells, causing bradycardia, vasodilation, weak contraction, and hyperglycemia. High-dose insulin improves myocardial carbohydrate uptake and inotropy when poisoned myocardium cannot use substrate efficiently. Dextrose prevents treatment-induced hypoglycemia, while potassium monitoring detects intracellular shift rather than assuming total-body depletion.

Administration Timing

  • Immediately stabilize airway and circulation, obtain ECG and glucose, identify exact agent and formulation, give activated charcoal only when safe and indicated, and call Poison Help.

  • For severe shock, initiate high-dose insulin under protocol, commonly 1 unit/kg IV regular insulin then 1-10 units/kg/hour with dextrose, frequent glucose, and potassium monitoring; titrate to perfusion rather than glucose alone.

  • Use agent-directed calcium, glucagon, vasopressors, atropine, pacing, or sodium bicarbonate as indicated and activate VA-ECMO early for refractory cardiogenic shock.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify product, immediate- versus extended-release formulation, amount, time, co-ingestants, weight, glucose, ECG, and access to extracorporeal support because delayed collapse can follow sustained-release exposure.

  • Check bedside glucose every 15-30 minutes during initiation and after changes, then at protocol intervals, and trend potassium, fluid balance, perfusion, lactate, and ejection because insulin doses are far above diabetes dosing.

  • Use concentrated dextrose and insulin through reliable lines with independent double checks because medication error, hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, and fluid overload are major preventable hazards.

Key Labs

  • Serial glucose, potassium, magnesium, calcium, creatinine, blood gas, lactate, ECG, bedside echocardiography, and agent concentrations when clinically useful guide mechanism and response.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning causes bradycardia, block, hypotension, pulmonary edema, shock, seizure, dysrhythmia, and arrest; high-dose insulin causes hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, and volume burden, while glucagon commonly causes vomiting.

Contraindications

  • Do not withhold high-dose insulin solely because glucose is initially normal; provide dextrose and monitoring. Do not route routine diabetes insulin or therapeutic beta-blocker questions into this emergency pathway.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Refractory shock, rising lactate, recurrent bradycardia, seizure, wide QRS, or arrest requires immediate toxicology, critical care, and VA-ECMO; atenolol or sotalol poisoning may be dialyzable.

Interactions

  • Combined beta blocker, calcium-channel blocker, digoxin, clonidine, or other cardiodepressant exposure can produce deeper and longer shock.

  • Sulfonylureas, insulin, poor nutrition, or kidney failure increase hypoglycemia, while beta blockade can blunt adrenergic warning signs and beta-agonist response.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    One or a few sustained-release tablets can cause severe delayed toxicity, and insulin, dextrose, calcium, and glucagon require independent weight-based calculation.

  • Population

    Heart failure or conduction disease

    Note

    Limited cardiac reserve and baseline block make deterioration faster and pacing less effective when myocardial contractility is profoundly depressed.

  • Population

    Kidney failure

    Note

    Atenolol or sotalol can accumulate and may be dialyzable, while insulin and glucose kinetics make prolonged monitoring necessary.

Evidence Limitations

  • Most antidotal comparisons rely on observational, animal, and case evidence; shock phenotype, exact drug, and local expertise determine combination and sequence.

Nclex Traps

  • High-dose insulin is an inotropic metabolic therapy, not ordinary diabetes correction.

  • Glucagon should not delay high-dose insulin, vasopressors, or extracorporeal escalation for persistent shock because it can cause vomiting and its hemodynamic response is inconsistent and often brief.

  • Pacing electrical capture may not restore perfusion when myocardial contractility is profoundly impaired.

Regulatory Status

This is an educational emergency pathway. High-dose insulin, glucagon, calcium, and several adjuncts are guideline-supported toxicology uses that may be off-label for the specific overdose.

Boxed Warning

CARDIODEPRESSANT OVERDOSE WARNING: bradycardia, hypotension, altered consciousness, or shock after a beta blocker or calcium-channel blocker requires 911 and Poison Help. High-dose insulin needs ICU-level glucose, potassium, fluid, and perfusion monitoring and must never be attempted as home treatment.
