Risk Factors

  • Hypertension is central because chronic pressure injures penetrating arterioles.

  • Diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, age, chronic kidney disease, and prior small-vessel disease amplify endothelial and arteriolar injury.

  • Atrial fibrillation and large-artery disease still require assessment because a lacunar-appearing infarct can have another source.

Signs Symptoms

  • Pure motor face-arm-leg weakness without cortical signs suggests internal capsule or pontine lacune.

  • Pure hemisensory loss suggests thalamic involvement, while ataxic hemiparesis combines weakness and ipsilateral limb incoordination.

  • Dysarthria-clumsy hand causes slurred speech and impaired fine hand movement without aphasia.

Diagnostics

  • Perform a complete cortical and brainstem examination because finding aphasia, neglect, field loss, or gaze deviation argues against a simple lacunar syndrome.

  • MRI DWI detects small acute deep infarcts better than early NCCT, which may be normal.

  • Evaluate cardiac rhythm and extracranial/intracranial vessels as indicated because appearance does not prove mechanism.

Labs

  • Glucose excludes mimic and identifies an important vascular risk factor.

  • A1c, lipids, renal function, and blood-pressure history guide long-term risk reduction because chronic small-vessel injury accumulates.

  • Platelet, coagulation, and anticoagulant data support acute reperfusion safety when the deficit is disabling.

Treatments

  • Use acute reperfusion criteria based on disability, timing, imaging, and bleeding risk because lesion size cannot be known reliably from symptoms alone.

  • Control blood pressure and diabetes over time, stop smoking, and use mechanism-appropriate antithrombotic and lipid therapy because recurrence prevention targets vessel injury.

  • Start task-specific motor, sensory, speech, and gait rehabilitation because a compact lesion can produce major practical disability.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess pure motor weakness, pure sensory loss, sensorimotor deficit, dysarthria-clumsy hand, ataxic hemiparesis, and absence of cortical signs because small penetrating-artery infarcts often produce recognizable deep syndromes.

  • Activate the same emergency stroke pathway used for larger infarcts and document last-known-well because lacunar appearance cannot exclude hemorrhage or large-vessel disease before imaging.

  • Review chronic blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, lipids, sleep apnea, antithrombotic use, and medication adherence because small-vessel injury reflects cumulative vascular exposure and secondary prevention must address its drivers.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, strength, sensation, speech, coordination, swallowing, blood pressure, glucose, and mobility because early neurologic deterioration, aspiration, and falls may occur despite a small imaging lesion.

  • Escalate for progressive weakness, recurrent deficits, NIH Stroke Scale increase, new aphasia or neglect, reduced consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, or seizure because evolving infarction, an alternative localization, or hemorrhage requires urgent reassessment.

Complications

  • Recurrent lacunes can accumulate into gait impairment, pseudobulbar features, urinary dysfunction, and vascular cognitive impairment.

  • Falls, aspiration, contracture, shoulder pain, and loss of fine-motor independence can follow a single strategically placed infarct.

  • Depression and slowed processing may be overlooked because speech and basic strength can recover.

Contraindications

  • Do not call a stroke minor because the MRI lesion is small.

  • Do not assume lacunar mechanism from a pure motor syndrome until hemorrhage, cortical ischemia, embolism, and mimic are evaluated.

  • Do not use absence of aphasia as evidence that the patient is safe to swallow or ambulate.

Red Flags

  • Sudden pure motor or sensory deficit affecting the face, arm, or leg

  • Progressive weakness, recurrent fluctuation, or NIH Stroke Scale increase after presentation

  • New aphasia, neglect, gaze deviation, visual loss, or other unexpected cortical sign

  • Reduced consciousness, severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, or aspiration

Patient Education

  • A small deep infarct can cause major disability, so sudden weakness or numbness still requires immediate emergency care.

  • Control blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, and lipids consistently because repeated penetrating-artery injury can accumulate into gait and cognitive impairment.

Nclex Traps

  • Lacunar means small deep infarct, not trivial stroke.

  • Classic lacunes lack cortical signs, but real patients can have mixed disease.

  • MRI is more sensitive than early CT for a small deep acute infarct.

Name

Lacunar stroke

Category

Cerebral small-vessel disease

Definition

Lacunar stroke is a small, deep infarct caused by occlusion of a penetrating artery supplying structures such as the internal capsule, thalamus, pons, or basal ganglia. The lesion may be physically small yet functionally important because motor, sensory, coordination, or speech fibers are densely packed. Classic syndromes include pure motor hemiparesis, pure sensory stroke, sensorimotor stroke, ataxic hemiparesis, and dysarthria-clumsy hand, usually without cortical signs such as aphasia, neglect, or a homonymous field cut.

Etiology

Chronic hypertension and diabetes promote lipohyalinosis and microatheroma in small penetrating arteries, but not every small deep infarct is intrinsic small-vessel disease. Emboli, parent-artery plaque, dissection, vasculitis, and other mechanisms can mimic a lacunar pattern, so cause is determined by the complete evaluation rather than lesion size alone.

Pathology

Penetrating arteries are end vessels with limited collateral supply. When one closes, a compact deep territory infarcts. Internal-capsule lesions interrupt motor fibers, thalamic lesions interrupt sensory relay, pontine lesions affect descending motor and cerebellar connections, and basal-ganglia lesions can alter motor integration.

Pathophysiology

Because cortex is spared in a classic lacune, language, spatial attention, visual fields, and gaze preference may remain intact. This absence of cortical signs helps localization but does not make the event benign. Repeated lacunes and diffuse white-matter injury disconnect frontal-subcortical circuits, contributing over time to slowed processing, gait disorder, urinary symptoms, mood change, and vascular cognitive impairment.
