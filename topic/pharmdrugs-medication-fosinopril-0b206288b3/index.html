Class

Phosphinate ester-prodrug ACE inhibitor with balanced renal and hepatobiliary elimination

Used To Treat

Hypertension and adjunctive treatment of heart failure according to labeling. Its clinically memorable distinction is balanced renal and hepatobiliary elimination of active fosinoprilat.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. Fosinopril's dual elimination does not protect the fetus. The danger is pathway blockade in fetal kidneys, not maternal drug accumulation alone.

Mechanism

Esterases convert fosinopril to fosinoprilat, whose phosphinate group binds the zinc-containing ACE active site. Reduced angiotensin-II formation lowers AT1-mediated vasoconstriction, aldosterone, sodium retention, sympathetic activity, and ventricular remodeling; reduced bradykinin breakdown adds vasodilation and cough or angioedema risk. Efferent-arteriole dilation can reduce intraglomerular pressure and albumin leakage but can also lower GFR during poor inflow. Fosinoprilat is cleared through both kidneys and bile. In renal impairment, hepatobiliary elimination rises enough that total clearance changes less than with many renally dependent ACE inhibitors. This explains dose flexibility, not renal invulnerability: potassium handling and glomerular pressure still respond to RAAS blockade.

Nursing Essentials

  • Teach the dual-elimination distinction accurately: it may reduce the need for renal dose adjustment, but pressure, potassium, and creatinine monitoring remain mandatory.

  • In heart failure, assess volume and diuretic intensity before initiation because excessive RAAS dependence predicts first-dose hypotension.

  • Check pressure and orthostasis, volume, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, pregnancy status, cough, facial or tongue swelling, and every NSAID, diuretic, potassium product, lithium drug, or duplicate RAAS blocker.

  • Teach that persistent dry cough should be evaluated, while lip, tongue, throat, voice, or swallowing change is an emergency because bradykinin swelling can obstruct the airway.

Contraindications

  • Previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, hereditary or idiopathic angioedema, pregnancy, and hypersensitivity.

  • Do not combine with sacubitril/valsartan or within 36 hours, or with aliskiren in diabetes.

  • Pregnancy, previous ACE-inhibitor angioedema, and hereditary or idiopathic angioedema where specified by labeling.

  • Do not combine with sacubitril/valsartan or administer within 36 hours; do not combine aliskiren with an ACE inhibitor in diabetes.

Interactions

  • Antacids can reduce fosinopril absorption and should be separated according to labeling; the usual potassium, NSAID, diuretic, lithium, and duplicate RAAS interactions remain.

  • Dialysis membrane reactions and anaphylactoid reactions during selected extracorporeal procedures are class concerns.

  • Potassium supplements or salt substitutes, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, diuretics, lithium, and duplicate RAAS blockers can produce hyperkalemia, AKI, hypotension, or lithium toxicity.

  • ACE inhibitor plus sacubitril/valsartan is contraindicated because overlapping peptide-degradation blockade increases angioedema risk.

Key Labs

  • Pressure, orthostasis, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, volume, and hepatic context when severe disease affects compensatory clearance.

  • No serum fosinoprilat level is routinely used; clinical response and safety labs guide dosing.

  • Baseline and follow-up potassium and creatinine/eGFR, blood pressure/orthostasis, volume, and urine albumin when kidney protection is the goal.

  • Investigate a major or progressive renal change for dehydration, NSAIDs, diuretics, sepsis, obstruction, hypotension, or renal-artery disease.

Nclex Traps

  • Dual renal and hepatobiliary elimination is a pharmacokinetic distinction. It does not erase the pharmacodynamic creatinine rise caused by efferent dilation.

  • Fosinopril contains phosphorus in its ACE-binding group; it is not a phosphate binder or phosphorus supplement.

  • ACE inhibition lowers angiotensin II and raises bradykinin; those are separate consequences of inhibiting the same enzyme.

  • A modest early creatinine rise can reflect lower intraglomerular pressure, but oliguria, severe hyperkalemia, symptoms, or a large progressive decline is not routine adaptation.

Description

Fosinopril is a phosphinate-containing ACE-inhibitor prodrug converted to active fosinoprilat. It lowers angiotensin II and aldosterone and raises bradykinin like the rest of the class, but its elimination adapts differently: when renal clearance falls, hepatobiliary elimination partially compensates, and when hepatic clearance falls, renal elimination contributes. Older labeling therefore permits the usual dosage range across renal impairment more readily than with exclusively renal ACE inhibitors. That pharmacokinetic resilience does not remove hemodynamic kidney risk, hyperkalemia, or the need to monitor CKD. A patient with renal-artery stenosis or severe volume depletion can still lose GFR because the efferent arteriole dilates, regardless of how the drug itself leaves the body. Fosinopril is used for hypertension and as adjunctive heart-failure therapy, not because it is a diuretic but because it reduces afterload and maladaptive RAAS signaling.

Adverse Effects

  • Cough, dizziness, hypotension, fatigue, GI symptoms, hyperkalemia, and creatinine change follow the ACE-inhibitor pattern.

  • Hepatic or renal impairment can shift the route of elimination, but severe disease in both systems may still prolong exposure.

  • Class effects include dry cough, dizziness, hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine increase, and less commonly rash or taste change.

  • Airway angioedema, severe AKI, dangerous potassium elevation, neutropenia in susceptible patients, hepatic injury, and fetal toxicity are high-consequence events.
