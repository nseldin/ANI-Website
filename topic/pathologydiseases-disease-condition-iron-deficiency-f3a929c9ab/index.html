Definition

Iron deficiency develops when available iron is insufficient for hemoglobin synthesis and iron-dependent tissues because of blood loss, low intake, malabsorption, or increased demand. The characteristic pattern is fatigue, pica, restless legs, glossitis, brittle nails, low ferritin, low transferrin saturation, and eventually microcytic hypochromic anemia. Confirm or stage it with CBC, reticulocyte indices, ferritin, iron, transferrin saturation, and inflammation context; distinguish it from anemia of inflammation, thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, and mixed deficiency by ferritin, saturation, red-cell indices, smear, and hemoglobin studies. The priority is to replace iron orally when absorption and tolerance permit because symptomatic anemia can complicate Iron deficiency. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Weakness, sweating, tremor, palpitations, mental-status change, weight change, thirst/polyuria, muscle cramps/spasm, paresthesias, fatigue, temperature intolerance, or abnormal growth/nutrition cues can reveal metabolic instability.

  • Escalate severe hypo/hyperglycemia, seizure, dysrhythmia, tetany/laryngospasm, adrenal crisis, thyroid storm, refeeding electrolyte collapse, or DKA/HHS signs.

Diagnostics

  • CBC, reticulocyte indices, ferritin, iron, transferrin saturation, and inflammation context

  • age- and sex-appropriate evaluation for gastrointestinal, menstrual, urinary, pregnancy, dietary, or malabsorptive loss

  • response to replacement when diagnosis and adherence remain uncertain

  • Distinguish Iron deficiency from anemia of inflammation, thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, and mixed deficiency by ferritin, saturation, red-cell indices, smear, and hemoglobin studies.

Labs

  • glucose may be high or low depending on condition

  • sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate changes can become life-threatening

  • anion gap and ketones matter in DKA

Treatments

  • replace iron orally when absorption and tolerance permit

  • use intravenous iron when oral therapy is ineffective, unsafe, or too slow

  • identify and stop the bleeding or absorption cause rather than treating the number alone

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess fatigue, exercise intolerance, pica, restless legs, hair or nail change, diet, menstrual and gastrointestinal loss, pregnancy, donation, surgery, and malabsorption history, and trend complete blood count, ferritin, transferrin saturation, and reticulocytes because depleted stores may cause symptoms before hemoglobin falls and replacement without finding the cause permits recurrence.

  • Administer oral iron on the prescribed daily or alternate-day schedule and review antacids, calcium, tea, food timing, nausea, and constipation because absorption and tolerability determine whether enough elemental iron reaches the marrow to replenish stores.

  • Use intravenous iron when ordered after verifying dose, product, access, and baseline vital signs, and monitor flushing, chest or back pressure, wheeze, hypotension, and extravasation because parenteral repletion bypasses poor absorption but can cause infusion reactions and tissue staining.

  • Coordinate evaluation for menstrual, gastrointestinal, urinary, dietary, celiac, inflammatory, or bariatric causes and reassess ferritin and hemoglobin at the planned interval because a weak or transient response points to ongoing loss, inflammation, malabsorption, nonadherence, or another diagnosis.

  • Escalate for syncope, chest pain, resting dyspnea, hemodynamic instability, brisk bleeding, black tarry stool unrelated to iron, severe infusion reaction, or failure of values to improve because major blood loss, severe anemia, or an alternate disorder requires urgent care.

Complications

  • symptomatic anemia can complicate Iron deficiency.

  • pregnancy and developmental effects

  • impaired exercise and cognition

  • missed gastrointestinal malignancy or chronic bleeding

  • infusion reaction or iron overload from unneeded parenteral treatment

Red Flags

  • Syncope, chest pain, dyspnea at rest, marked tachycardia, or hemodynamic instability

  • Vomiting blood, black sticky stool with weakness, heavy uncontrolled vaginal bleeding, or other brisk loss

  • Wheeze, facial swelling, hypotension, severe chest or back pain, or collapse during infusion

  • Falling hemoglobin or ferritin, weight loss, dysphagia, or no response despite appropriate replacement

Patient Education

  • Expect possible dark stool and constipation from oral iron, but report sticky tarry stool with weakness or abdominal symptoms because gastrointestinal bleeding can look similar.

  • Keep iron locked away from children and complete follow-up testing because overdose is dangerous and feeling better does not prove that iron stores or the source of loss have been corrected.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal or high ferritin during inflammation does not always exclude iron-restricted erythropoiesis

  • Continue long enough to replete stores, not only normalize hemoglobin
