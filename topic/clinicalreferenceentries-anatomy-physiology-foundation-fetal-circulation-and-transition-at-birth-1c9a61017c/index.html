Summary

Fetal circulation uses the placenta for gas exchange and three shunts—the ductus venosus, foramen ovale, and ductus arteriosus—to direct the most oxygenated returning blood toward the heart and brain while bypassing much of the liver and unexpanded lungs. At birth, cord clamping removes the low-resistance placenta and the first effective breaths lower pulmonary vascular resistance. Those pressure and chemical changes redirect flow through the lungs and begin functional closure of the shunts.

Quick Answer

Oxygenated placental blood travels in the umbilical vein; much passes through the ductus venosus to the inferior vena cava, enters the right atrium, and is preferentially directed through the foramen ovale to the left atrium, left ventricle, and ascending aorta. Blood returning from the upper body enters the right ventricle and pulmonary artery, but high fetal pulmonary resistance sends most through the ductus arteriosus to the descending aorta. It returns to the placenta through two umbilical arteries. With breathing, lung expansion and oxygen lower pulmonary resistance, pulmonary blood flow raises left-atrial pressure, and the foramen ovale flap closes functionally. Cord clamping raises systemic resistance. Increased oxygen and reduced placental prostaglandin influence constrict the ductus arteriosus; the ductus venosus and umbilical vessels also close. Transition is a process, so persistent shunting or congenital disease can present after initial adaptation.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and fetal route

    Text

    The umbilical vein carries the fetus's most oxygenated blood from placenta toward liver. Some perfuses hepatic tissue and much uses the ductus venosus to reach the inferior vena cava. Streaming within the right atrium favors this relatively oxygen-rich blood across the foramen ovale into left atrium, left ventricle, and ascending aorta, supplying coronary and cerebral circulations. Superior vena-caval blood preferentially enters right ventricle and pulmonary trunk. Because fetal lungs are fluid-filled and pulmonary arterioles constricted, only a minority reaches pulmonary capillaries; most crosses the ductus arteriosus into descending aorta. Two umbilical arteries arise from the fetal arterial circulation and return lower-oxygen blood to placenta.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: why fetal shunts exist

    Text

    The placenta, not the fetal lung, exchanges gases, so sending the full cardiac output through high-resistance unexpanded lungs would waste pressure and reduce systemic delivery. The foramen ovale provides a right-to-left atrial route for better-oxygenated inferior vena-caval blood. The ductus arteriosus connects pulmonary trunk to aorta, bypassing pulmonary resistance. The ductus venosus permits a portion of umbilical blood to bypass hepatic sinusoids and reach the heart quickly, though the liver still receives important flow. Parallel rather than adult series circulation allows both ventricles to contribute to systemic output. Oxygen content is lower than in postnatal arterial blood, but fetal hemoglobin affinity and high cardiac output support tissue delivery.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: birth transition

    Text

    With effective lung inflation, fluid clearance and rising alveolar oxygen relax pulmonary vessels, sharply lowering pulmonary vascular resistance. Pulmonary blood flow and venous return to the left atrium increase. Cord clamping removes the low-resistance placental circuit, raising systemic vascular resistance and reducing venous return to the right atrium. When left-atrial pressure exceeds right-atrial pressure, the flexible septal flap closes the foramen ovale functionally. Higher oxygen tension and loss of placental prostaglandin supply promote ductus-arteriosus constriction. Umbilical flow cessation closes the ductus venosus. Anatomic sealing takes longer than the initial functional changes, explaining why transitional flow may be detectable without always representing permanent disease.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Ventilation, oxygenation, temperature, pH, pulmonary vascular tone, systemic resistance, myocardial function, and shunt patency interact. Hypoxia, acidosis, cold stress, pain, or lung disease can keep pulmonary resistance high, preserving right-to-left flow across fetal channels and worsening hypoxemia. A newborn spends extra oxygen and glucose when cold, which can destabilize transition. Some congenital heart lesions depend on a patent ductus to supply pulmonary or systemic blood; closure that is normal for most newborns can cause shock in those infants. The fetal circulation card links placental exchange to postnatal lung and cardiac physiology rather than assuming an instant switch at the moment of delivery.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn keeps pulmonary resistance abnormally high, so blood bypasses lungs through the foramen ovale or ductus arteriosus and causes hypoxemia. A persistent ductus arteriosus after resistance falls usually shunts aortic blood toward pulmonary artery, increasing pulmonary flow and left-heart volume; significance depends on size and maturity. Prematurity delays ductal and lung adaptation. Respiratory distress, sepsis, meconium aspiration, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, hypothermia, and acidosis can impair transition. Ductal-dependent congenital lesions may appear stable while the ductus remains open, then deteriorate with poor feeding, cyanosis, tachypnea, weak pulses, acidosis, or shock as it constricts.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Prostaglandin E1 can maintain or reopen ductal patency in suspected ductal-dependent congenital heart disease while definitive diagnosis and treatment proceed; apnea and hypotension are important monitoring risks. In selected preterm infants with a hemodynamically significant PDA, cyclooxygenase-inhibiting medicines reduce prostaglandin synthesis and may promote closure, but renal, gastrointestinal, platelet, and perfusion risks matter. Oxygen and ventilation can lower pulmonary resistance when hypoxia and acidosis drive it, but oxygen is titrated because excessive exposure can also injure premature infants. Inhaled pulmonary vasodilator therapy and other support address selected PPHN. These opposing uses show why the same ductus can be lifesaving or harmful depending on anatomy and postnatal physiology.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: assessment and diagnostics

    Text

    Prenatal ultrasound and fetal echocardiography assess anatomy and flow before birth. After birth, examine color, work of breathing, heart rate, pulses in upper and lower extremities, capillary refill, blood pressure patterns, temperature, feeding tolerance, urine output, and liver size. Pulse oximetry screening can detect some critical congenital heart disease but does not exclude all lesions. Comparing right-hand preductal saturation with a lower-extremity postductal value can reveal differential shunting in selected cases. Blood gas, lactate, glucose, chest imaging, ECG, and echocardiography answer ventilation, perfusion, metabolism, structure, and shunt questions. A murmur may be absent in severe ductal-dependent disease.

  • Label

    Nursing actions with rationale

    Text

    At delivery, support warmth, airway positioning, effective ventilation when needed, heart-rate reassessment, and oxygen titration according to neonatal resuscitation guidance. Ventilation is pivotal because lung inflation drives the fall in pulmonary resistance. Maintain neutral temperature and monitor glucose because cold stress and hypoglycemia increase oxygen demand. When transition is abnormal, trend preductal and postductal saturations as ordered, pulses, perfusion, respiratory effort, blood pressure, urine output, feeding, and acid-base status. With prostaglandin infusion, ensure reliable access and readiness for apnea management. Escalate a deteriorating infant even if the prenatal study was reassuring or a pulse-ox screen was previously passed.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Central cyanosis, persistent low saturation, apnea, grunting, severe retractions, weak or unequal pulses, gray color, prolonged capillary refill, temperature instability, lethargy, poor feeding, diaphoresis with feeds, oliguria, metabolic acidosis, or shock requires immediate neonatal assessment. Sudden decline during the first days can reflect ductal closure in critical congenital heart disease. Marked preductal-postductal saturation difference, differential cyanosis, or hypoxemia that does not improve as expected needs urgent evaluation. Do not assume every blue newborn has primary lung disease or that every congenital heart lesion causes a loud murmur. Stabilize ventilation, oxygenation, temperature, glucose, and circulation while obtaining expert help.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    The umbilical vein is relatively oxygen-rich and the two umbilical arteries relatively oxygen-poor because names describe direction toward or away from the fetal heart. The fetus does receive blood flow through the lungs, just not the adult proportion. The three shunts are ductus venosus, foramen ovale, and ductus arteriosus; they have different locations and purposes. Foramen-ovale closure is initiated by an atrial pressure reversal, while ductal constriction responds importantly to oxygen and prostaglandin change. Shunts do not all become permanent ligaments instantly. A PDA is not universally something to close: ductal patency can be essential in specific congenital lesions.
