Summary

A neuron is a polarized signaling cell whose dendrites and soma receive information, axon hillock integrates it, axon conducts an all-or-none action potential, and terminal releases chemical signals. Ion gradients, selective channels, myelin, and supporting glia make fast communication possible; the action potential does not carry a substance down the axon but propagates a regenerative change in membrane voltage.

Quick Answer

At rest, potassium leak and maintained ion gradients leave the inside relatively negative. Sufficient depolarization reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open, and sodium entry produces the upstroke. Sodium channels inactivate while potassium channels open, potassium exits, and the membrane repolarizes. Refractory periods enforce direction and limit firing frequency.

Sections

    • Neuron structure

    • Dendrites receive synaptic input, the soma contains nucleus and protein-making machinery, and the axon hillock or initial segment sums excitatory and inhibitory influences. A single axon may branch into collaterals and terminals. Axonal transport moves proteins, vesicles, mitochondria, and recycled material between soma and distant terminals; disruption can injure long neurons even when the cell body survives. Myelin wraps selected axons: oligodendrocytes myelinate multiple central nervous system segments, while each Schwann cell myelinates a peripheral segment. Nodes of Ranvier interrupt myelin and concentrate channels needed for saltatory conduction.

    • Resting membrane potential

    • The neuronal membrane separates unequal ion distributions. Potassium is relatively concentrated inside and sodium outside; large intracellular anions contribute negative charge. Potassium leak channels allow more resting potassium movement than sodium movement, making the inside negative relative to outside. The sodium-potassium ATPase maintains gradients over time by moving three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in, but rapid voltage changes occur mainly through channels. The resting potential is a dynamic steady state, not an inactive battery. Severe hypoxia or ATP depletion eventually collapses gradients, causing swelling, calcium injury, and loss of signaling.

    • Threshold and depolarization

    • Graded potentials arise when synaptic or sensory channels change local voltage. They vary in size, decay with distance, and sum across time and space. At the initial segment, enough net depolarization reaches threshold and opens voltage-gated sodium channels. Sodium entry depolarizes adjacent membrane, opening more channels in a regenerative sequence. Once threshold is reached, the action potential is all-or-none; stronger stimuli increase firing frequency or recruit more neurons rather than making each spike taller. Local anesthetics and several antiseizure or antiarrhythmic drugs reduce excitable signaling by stabilizing or blocking voltage-gated sodium-channel states.

    • Repolarization and refractory periods

    • Near the peak, sodium channels inactivate and delayed voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium efflux brings voltage back toward negative values and can briefly hyperpolarize the membrane because potassium channels close slowly. During the absolute refractory period, inactivated sodium channels cannot immediately reopen, so another action potential is impossible. During the relative refractory period, a stronger stimulus is required. These properties prevent backward re-excitation and cap firing frequency. The sodium-potassium pump restores long-term concentration balance, but it is not responsible for each rapid downstroke.

    • Propagation and myelin

    • In unmyelinated axons, each adjacent segment depolarizes sequentially. In myelinated axons, current spreads rapidly under insulating myelin and action potentials regenerate mainly at nodes, creating saltatory conduction. Larger axon diameter lowers internal resistance and speeds conduction. Demyelination leaks current, slows or blocks conduction, and can make function worsen with heat. Peripheral nerves may regenerate slowly if the soma survives and connective pathways remain aligned; central axons face inhibitory and scar environments. Compression can first impair myelin and conduction, while severe ischemia or trauma causes axonal loss and slower recovery.

    • Failure consequences and disease links

    • Multiple sclerosis damages central myelin; Guillain-Barre syndrome and related neuropathies affect peripheral myelin or axons; diabetes, toxins, nutritional deficiency, ischemia, and compression injure peripheral nerves; stroke interrupts networks or their blood supply. Hyperkalemia initially depolarizes membranes but persistent depolarization inactivates sodium channels and causes weakness or conduction failure. Hypocalcemia increases excitability by lowering effective threshold, while severe abnormalities can produce tetany or seizure. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity can cause neurologic excitation followed by depression and malignant cardiac effects because sodium channels are essential in both tissues.

    • Clinical connections, labs, and diagnostics

    • Neurologic examination localizes dysfunction through mental status, cranial nerves, strength, reflexes, sensation, coordination, gait, and autonomic findings. Nerve-conduction studies measure response speed and amplitude; EMG evaluates muscle electrical activity; MRI identifies many central lesions; lumbar puncture and antibody, infectious, genetic, toxic, vitamin, glucose, thyroid, or electrolyte testing address selected causes. Antiseizure drugs reduce excitability through sodium channels, calcium channels, GABA, glutamate, vesicle proteins, or other targets. Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, glucose, renal and liver function, and drug levels matter because metabolic or medication states can mimic structural neurologic disease.

    • Nursing assessment and actions

    • Establish a detailed baseline and trend side, distribution, onset, progression, pain, numbness, weakness, coordination, reflexes, speech, swallowing, cognition, bowel and bladder function, and respiratory effort. Protect insensate skin from pressure and temperature injury. Use fall and seizure precautions when indicated, administer neurologic medications on time, and avoid abrupt interruption of selected antiseizure or Parkinson therapies. Review renal and hepatic clearance, interactions, sedation, and electrolyte changes. New focal findings require a last-known-well time and urgent stroke pathway rather than waiting to see whether the neuron 'recovers.'

    • Urgent red flags

    • Sudden unilateral weakness, facial droop, speech or visual change, severe abrupt headache, seizure without recovery, rapidly ascending weakness, respiratory decline, new urinary retention with saddle anesthesia, or acute loss of limb perfusion is time-sensitive. Fever, neck stiffness, altered consciousness, or petechiae can signal meningitis. Severe agitation, seizure, widened QRS, ventricular dysrhythmia, or hypotension after local anesthetic exposure suggests systemic toxicity. Progressive weakness with reduced cough or vital capacity can precede obvious oxygen desaturation because ventilation failure first raises carbon dioxide.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • The action potential is all-or-none, but the nervous system still codes intensity through frequency and recruitment. The sodium-potassium pump does not directly create each spike. Depolarization does not always mean successful firing; persistent depolarization can inactivate sodium channels. Myelin speeds conduction but does not generate the signal itself. A normal CT does not exclude early ischemic stroke, peripheral neuropathy, or many functional network disorders. Numbness and weakness are not interchangeable. When localization suggests central, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction, or muscle disease, choose assessments and precautions that fit that level.
