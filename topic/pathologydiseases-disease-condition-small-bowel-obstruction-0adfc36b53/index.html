Definition

Small bowel obstruction develops when adhesion, hernia, tumor, inflammation, or volvulus blocks small-intestinal transit and may compromise mesenteric blood flow. The characteristic pattern is colicky pain, vomiting, distention, obstipation, high-pitched or changing sounds, and dilated small bowel with a transition point. Confirm or stage it with CT abdomen and pelvis for site, cause, closed loop, ischemia, and perforation; distinguish it from ileus, gastroenteritis, large-bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischemia, and pseudo-obstruction by transition point, pain, bowel pattern, and imaging. Signs Symptoms

  • Abdominal pain pattern, distention, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea or constipation, stool/emesis blood, jaundice, dysphagia, poor intake, dehydration, or guarding are the useful GI clues.

  • Escalate rigid abdomen, peritonitis signs, GI bleeding, shock, severe dehydration, fever with jaundice, or worsening mental status.

Diagnostics

  • CT abdomen and pelvis for site, cause, closed loop, ischemia, and perforation

  • serial abdominal examination, CBC, electrolytes, lactate, renal function, and urine output

  • early surgical evaluation when strangulation or complete obstruction is possible

  • Distinguish Small bowel obstruction from ileus, gastroenteritis, large-bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischemia, and pseudo-obstruction by transition point, pain, bowel pattern, and imaging.

Labs

  • hemoglobin may fall with bleeding

  • AST/ALT and bilirubin may rise with hepatic/biliary injury

  • lipase rises in pancreatitis

  • albumin and INR reflect liver synthetic function

Treatments

  • resuscitate, correct electrolytes, rest and decompress the bowel

  • perform urgent surgery for peritonitis, ischemia, closed loop, perforation, or failed conservative care

  • use selected water-soluble contrast protocols only in appropriate stable adhesive disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for continuous severe pain, guarding, fever, tachycardia, rising lactate, bloody stool, or shock because strangulation or perforation requires urgent surgery.

  • Keep the patient nothing by mouth and establish intravenous access because vomiting and third spacing cause dehydration while oral intake increases distention.

  • Insert and maintain ordered nasogastric decompression while measuring output because removing accumulated fluid and gas can reduce vomiting and aspiration risk.

  • Assess pain pattern, distention, bowel sounds, vomiting, prior surgery, and hernia sites because strangulation may initially resemble uncomplicated obstruction.

  • Monitor fluid balance, urine output, sodium, potassium, lactate, abdominal tenderness, and nasogastric output to detect ischemia and replacement needs.

Complications

  • strangulation can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

  • necrosis can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

  • perforation can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

  • peritonitis can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

  • aspiration can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

  • hypovolemic kidney injury can complicate Small bowel obstruction.

Red Flags

  • Continuous severe pain rather than intermittent cramping

  • Guarding, rebound tenderness, or rigid abdomen

  • Fever, tachycardia, rising lactate, or shock

  • Bloody stool or abrupt clinical deterioration

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient not to take laxatives or eat through a suspected obstruction unless specifically directed because pressure and vomiting may worsen.

  • Explain the role of bowel rest, decompression, fluid replacement, and the warning findings that make surgery urgent.

Nclex Traps

  • Temporary diarrhea or stool passage does not exclude partial obstruction

  • Continuous severe pain and rising lactate are not routine uncomplicated adhesive obstruction
