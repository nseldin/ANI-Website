Definition

Jaundice is yellow discoloration of the skin, mucous membranes, or sclerae (the whites of the eyes) caused by too much bilirubin in the blood. It is an observed sign, not a diagnosis.

Plain Language

Jaundice means the skin or whites of the eyes look yellow because bilirubin has built up in the body.

Why It Matters

Jaundice can result from red-blood-cell breakdown, liver injury, impaired bilirubin processing, or blocked bile flow. The color alone cannot identify the cause or bilirubin level; fever with right upper abdominal pain, confusion, low blood pressure, bleeding, or rapid deterioration requires urgent evaluation.

Assessment Method

Inspect the sclerae, meaning the whites of the eyes, oral mucosa, and skin in natural or neutral light and compare with baseline; assess onset, dark urine, pale stools, itching, abdominal pain, fever, nausea, weight loss, medications, toxins, alcohol, transfusion and exposure history, mental status, bruising or bleeding, vital signs, and ordered total and direct bilirubin and liver tests.

Positive Finding

The sclerae, skin, or mucous membranes have a yellow discoloration that is new or greater than baseline.

Mechanism

Bilirubin accumulates in blood and deposits in tissues when production exceeds clearance or when liver uptake, conjugation, hepatocyte function, bile formation, or bile drainage is impaired.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Jaundice finding raises concern for excess bilirubin from increased heme breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow because bilirubin accumulates in blood and deposits in tissues when production exceeds clearance or when liver uptake, conjugation, hepatocyte function, bile formation, or bile drainage is impaired. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Jaundice does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Evidence Limitations

Visibility varies with bilirubin concentration, lighting, tissue examined, and skin pigmentation; carotenemia can yellow skin while sparing the sclerae, and visual examination cannot quantify bilirubin or identify the cause. Therefore, Jaundice cannot confirm or exclude excess bilirubin from increased heme breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • carotenemia

  • medication-related skin discoloration

  • normal pigmentation variation

  • yellow lighting artifact

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as carotenemia, medication-related skin discoloration, normal pigmentation variation, yellow lighting artifact.

False Negative Situations

Jaundice may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of excess bilirubin from increased heme breakdown, impaired hepatic uptake or conjugation, hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, or obstruction of bile flow.

Modern Status

Current clinical sign of hyperbilirubinemia, not a diagnosis; the bilirubin fraction, liver-test pattern, history, examination, and imaging when indicated determine cause and urgency.
