Range

Methylmalonic acid (MMA) may be measured in serum, plasma, or urine, and the specimen, units, method, age, and performing laboratory determine the applicable adult reference interval. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements cites serum MMA above 0.271 micromol/L as one value that suggests vitamin B12 deficiency, but it also notes that renal insufficiency raises MMA and that values tend to be higher in older adults, so this is not a universal diagnostic cutoff. Interpret MMA with symptoms, prior vitamin B12 use, the vitamin B12 result, CBC and indices, folate or homocysteine when relevant, and creatinine/eGFR rather than comparing blood and urine values or different laboratories as interchangeable.

Why

An elevated Methylmalonic acid result signals reduced processing through a vitamin B12-dependent metabolic pathway and can support vitamin B12 deficiency when the clinical picture or initial B12 result is uncertain. Elevation is associated with inadequate intake, autoimmune gastritis or another absorption disorder, gastric or terminal-ileal surgery, and medicine-related risk such as metformin use, but reduced kidney function and older age can elevate MMA without proving tissue B12 deficiency. Promptly assess fatigue, pallor, glossitis, paresthesias (tingling or numbness), vibration or position-sense change, gait or balance difficulty, weakness, cognitive or mood change, diet, alcohol use, bowel or bariatric surgery, malabsorption, nitrous-oxide exposure, medicines, supplements, and prior B12 replacement. Cause-directed evaluation can include CBC with MCV and reticulocytes, total or active vitamin B12, folate, homocysteine, creatinine/eGFR, and clinician-selected intrinsic-factor antibodies or other testing for the suspected cause. Notify or escalate promptly when suspected megaloblastic anemia accompanies an acute neurologic change such as new ataxia or another new neurologic deficit, or rapid deterioration; NICE advises not delaying clinician-directed vitamin B12 replacement while awaiting results in that neurologic pattern. One elevated MMA result does not diagnose vitamin B12 deficiency or methylmalonic acidemia, grade neurologic injury, identify the cause, or by itself select a replacement dose, route, or duration; biomarker normalization also does not prove clinical recovery.

High Causes

  • Vitamin B12 deficiency from inadequate intake or impaired absorption

  • Autoimmune gastritis, celiac disease, Crohn disease, gastric or terminal-ileal surgery, or some bariatric procedures

  • Medicine or exposure context that increases B12 risk, including metformin or nitrous oxide

  • Reduced kidney function, which can raise MMA independently of B12 status

  • Older age, assay, specimen, or laboratory variation

  • Methylmalonic acidemia in a separate newborn or inherited-metabolic evaluation

Low Causes

  • A low MMA value is usually not clinically concerning and does not establish adequate vitamin B12 status when symptoms or prior supplementation make the picture discordant.

Critical Concerns

  • Ataxia, a new neurologic deficit, rapidly worsening gait or sensation, or suspected megaloblastic anemia with neurologic symptoms needs prompt clinician evaluation because delay can worsen or leave persistent injury.

  • The MMA number itself has no adult emergency threshold; escalate for the neurologic, hematologic, or clinical deterioration pattern.

  • Reduced kidney function can elevate MMA, so a discordant result must be interpreted with creatinine/eGFR before labeling vitamin B12 deficiency.

Nursing Considerations

  • Obtain diagnostic blood samples before replacement when feasible, document all vitamin B12-containing tablets, injections, patches, and multivitamins, and do not delay the ordered treatment pathway when urgent neurologic criteria apply.

  • Assess anemia and neurologic findings plus diet, bowel or bariatric surgery, malabsorption, alcohol, metformin, acid-suppressing therapy, nitrous oxide, pregnancy or breastfeeding, kidney function, and prior treatment.

  • Trend symptoms and the cause-specific plan rather than using MMA normalization alone as proof of recovery or independently choosing oral versus intramuscular therapy.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal hemoglobin or MCV does not exclude clinically important vitamin B12 deficiency when neurologic symptoms are present.

  • MMA is more specific to B12-dependent metabolism than homocysteine, but kidney dysfunction can make MMA falsely persuasive.

  • Folate can improve the blood-count clue while untreated vitamin B12-related neurologic injury continues; evaluate B12 before assuming isolated folate deficiency.
