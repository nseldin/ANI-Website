Definition

Microscopic colitis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the colon that causes watery, usually nonbloody diarrhea even though the bowel lining may look normal during colonoscopy. Diagnosis requires mucosal biopsies because the defining inflammation is visible under the microscope rather than as the deep ulcers typical of Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis.

Pathology

Lymphocytic colitis shows increased intraepithelial lymphocytes with mucosal inflammation. Collagenous colitis has a thickened subepithelial collagen band plus inflammation. The two patterns share symptoms and treatment and can overlap or change over time.

Pathophysiology

  • A susceptible mucosal immune system reacts abnormally to luminal antigens, medications, smoking-related exposures, microbiome signals, or bile acids.

  • Inflammation impairs sodium and water absorption and increases secretion, producing high-volume watery diarrhea without requiring visible ulceration. Excess bile acids reaching the colon can amplify secretion in a subset of patients.

Etiology

The exact cause is usually not identifiable and likely reflects immune, genetic, microbiome, medication, and environmental interactions. Smoking and several medication groups are associated with microscopic colitis, including NSAIDs, proton-pump inhibitors, and selected serotonergic or other drugs; association does not prove that one medicine caused an individual case. Celiac disease, autoimmune thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and type 1 diabetes occur more often in affected patients.

Risk Factors

  • Older age and female sex, especially for collagenous colitis

  • Current smoking

  • Celiac disease or another autoimmune disorder

  • Exposure to a medication temporally associated with new or worsening diarrhea

Signs Symptoms

  • Chronic or recurrent watery, nonbloody diarrhea, including nocturnal stools

  • Urgency, fecal incontinence, abdominal cramping or pain, bloating, fatigue, and weight loss

  • Relapsing episodes separated by partial or complete remission

  • Dehydration, orthostasis, skin irritation, and electrolyte loss when stool volume is high

Diagnostics

  • Exclude infection and other common causes of chronic watery diarrhea, then perform colonoscopy with multiple biopsies from both right and left colon even when the mucosa looks normal.

  • Ask pathology to distinguish collagenous and lymphocytic patterns and review biopsy orientation and sampling when suspicion remains high.

  • Screen for celiac disease and assess bile-acid diarrhea, thyroid disease, medication exposure, and malabsorption according to the clinical context.

  • Do not use a normal fecal calprotectin or visually normal colonoscopy to exclude microscopic colitis.

Labs

  • No blood or stool biomarker confirms microscopic colitis.

  • CBC, electrolytes, creatinine, magnesium, albumin, and inflammatory markers assess dehydration, nutrition, anemia, and alternative inflammation.

  • Stool pathogen testing is selected by duration, exposure, immune status, and local epidemiology.

  • Celiac serology is recommended because coexisting celiac disease changes dietary management.

Assessment

Assess stool onset, frequency, volume, nocturnal episodes, blood, urgency, incontinence, pain, fever, weight trend, intake, urine output, orthostatic symptoms, travel and infection exposure, autoimmune disease, smoking, and a complete medication timeline. Examine hydration and abdomen and assess perianal skin and fall risk. A normal visual colonoscopy does not close the assessment unless adequate regional biopsies were obtained.

Differential

Differentiate microscopic colitis from infectious diarrhea including Clostridioides difficile, celiac disease, bile-acid diarrhea, irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhea, medication-induced diarrhea without colitis, Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis, hyperthyroidism, pancreatic insufficiency, small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and uncommon secretory disorders.

Treatments

  • Review and, when clinically safe, withdraw a plausible medication trigger because exposure may be sustaining mucosal inflammation, rather than stopping essential therapy indiscriminately; support smoking cessation.

  • Oral budesonide is the preferred induction treatment for active collagenous or lymphocytic colitis because it acts strongly in the gut with less systemic exposure than conventional corticosteroids.

  • Recurrent disease may require the lowest effective budesonide maintenance strategy with monitoring for steroid effects.

  • Use hydration and electrolyte replacement to correct stool losses, then add dietary adjustment and selected antidiarrheal or bile-acid-binding therapy according to severity and mechanism.

  • Refractory disease requires gastroenterology confirmation of the diagnosis and consideration of immunomodulator or biologic therapy; surgery is rarely needed.

Contraindications

  • Do not label chronic watery diarrhea irritable bowel syndrome without biopsy when age, nocturnal symptoms, medication exposure, or autoimmune context raises microscopic colitis.

  • Do not stop anticoagulants, psychiatric drugs, or acid-suppression therapy abruptly solely because a drug class has an association; assess timing, indication, and alternatives with the prescriber.

  • Avoid antidiarrheal treatment alone when fever, blood, severe pain, ileus, toxic colitis, or infection is suspected.

  • Budesonide still has glucocorticoid effects and important CYP3A interactions; lower systemic bioavailability does not make it risk-free.

Safety Considerations

  • Older adults can become volume depleted and develop kidney injury before vital signs appear dramatic.

  • Check orthostatic symptoms, intake, urine output, renal function, and electrolytes when stool frequency rises.

  • Persistent blood, fever, marked inflammatory findings, or severe focal pain is atypical and should reopen the differential.

  • During longer steroid exposure, assess bone, eye, glucose, infection, adrenal, and interaction risks according to the regimen.

Nursing Priorities

  • Measure stool frequency, nighttime episodes, appearance, urgency, incontinence, intake, weight, orthostatic symptoms, and functional effect.

  • Protect perianal skin with gentle cleansing and barrier care and provide prompt access to toileting to preserve dignity and reduce falls.

  • Reconcile recent and chronic medicines, including OTC NSAIDs and supplements, and document the timing relative to diarrhea.

  • Monitor hydration, creatinine, potassium, magnesium, nutrition, and response after budesonide or trigger withdrawal to detect ongoing high-volume losses and confirm that treatment is controlling the diarrhea.

  • Use teach-back for relapse signs and the difference between symptom control and histologic diagnosis.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, confusion, oliguria, severe weakness, tachycardia, or hypotension from dehydration

  • Bloody stool, high fever, peritoneal signs, severe focal pain, ileus, or rapidly worsening illness

  • Major unintentional weight loss, profound anemia, or persistent symptoms despite appropriate therapy

  • Steroid-associated infection, adrenal symptoms, severe hyperglycemia, or acute medication interaction

Complications

  • Dehydration, acute kidney injury, electrolyte disturbance, weight loss, malnutrition, falls, and impaired quality of life

  • Fecal incontinence and skin breakdown

  • Medication and corticosteroid adverse effects

  • Rare ulceration or perforation; unlike Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis, microscopic colitis does not appear to increase colorectal-cancer risk

Prognosis

Most patients improve with appropriate therapy, but relapse after treatment withdrawal is common and does not mean the original diagnosis was wrong. The disease generally does not progress to destructive Crohn-type colitis or raise colorectal-cancer risk. The practical goal is durable symptom control with the least treatment toxicity.

Prevention

No strategy prevents every case. Avoid smoking and unnecessary chronic exposure to medications associated with microscopic colitis while preserving essential therapy through prescriber review. Treat celiac disease and bile-acid diarrhea when present and use follow-up to catch dehydration or relapse early.

Patient Education

  • A normal-looking colonoscopy does not mean the diarrhea is imaginary; the inflammation is found in biopsy tissue.

  • Take budesonide exactly as prescribed and do not stop a prolonged course abruptly without guidance.

  • Keep a stool and medication timeline and report nighttime diarrhea, reduced urine, dizziness, blood, fever, or marked weight loss.

  • Replace fluids as directed, protect skin, and ask before using OTC NSAIDs or antidiarrheal medicines.

Special Populations

  • Older adults need early hydration and fall-risk assessment and careful review of polypharmacy.

  • Pregnancy and lactation require individualized selection of antidiarrheal, bile-acid-binding, and steroid therapy.

  • Pediatric microscopic colitis is uncommon and should prompt careful reassessment for immune, celiac, infectious, and medication-related disease.

Nclex Traps

  • Microscopic colitis can have a normal colonoscopic appearance; biopsy from multiple colonic regions is the key test.

  • The hallmark stool is watery and nonbloody. Blood or systemic toxicity points to another or additional process.

  • Budesonide is a corticosteroid with fewer systemic effects, not zero systemic effects.

  • Medication associations require a structured prescriber review, not abrupt withdrawal of every listed drug.
