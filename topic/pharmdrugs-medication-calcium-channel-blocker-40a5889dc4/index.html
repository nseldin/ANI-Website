Used To Treat

Hypertension and angina; selected IV agents for closely titrated acute blood-pressure control; diltiazem or verapamil for selected supraventricular tachyarrhythmias; and nimodipine to improve neurologic outcome after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. The indication follows the subclass and formulation, not the class name alone.

Description

Calcium channel blockers inhibit L-type voltage-gated calcium channels, but the clinically decisive fact is where each drug exerts its strongest effect. Dihydropyridines such as amlodipine, nifedipine, nicardipine, and clevidipine act predominantly in arterial smooth muscle, so they lower systemic vascular resistance and afterload while commonly causing headache, flushing, reflex tachycardia, and dependent edema. Non-dihydropyridines diltiazem and verapamil act more strongly in the SA node, AV node, and myocardium, so they slow heart rate and conduction and reduce contractility. Nimodipine is a vascular dihydropyridine used for a unique neurologic outcome indication after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Calling every member simply a blood-pressure drug hides the distinction that determines both benefit and danger.

Mechanism

Cav1.2 L-type channels open during membrane depolarization and admit calcium. In arterial smooth muscle, calcium binds calmodulin, activates myosin light-chain kinase, and permits actin-myosin contraction. Channel blockade therefore relaxes arterioles, lowers systemic vascular resistance, lowers afterload, and can improve coronary flow. In SA- and AV-nodal cells, the action-potential upstroke and conduction depend substantially on inward calcium current; diltiazem and verapamil therefore slow pacemaker firing, prolong AV-node refractoriness, and interrupt AV-node-dependent reentry. In ventricular myocytes, less trigger calcium enters through L-type channels and less calcium is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, reducing contractility. Dihydropyridines bind preferentially to channel states common in depolarized vascular smooth muscle, whereas verapamil and diltiazem show stronger use-dependent cardiac-channel effects. That tissue selectivity explains why amlodipine does not substitute for diltiazem in rapid atrial fibrillation and why verapamil can worsen systolic heart failure or heart block.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: The class has no single interchangeable warning profile. Dihydropyridines can cause excessive hypotension, reflex tachycardia, angina worsening during rapid vasodilation, and peripheral edema. Diltiazem and verapamil can cause bradycardia, high-grade AV block, hypotension, and worsening systolic failure, especially when combined with beta blockers, digoxin, or other conduction-slowing drugs. Never give AV-node blockers for pre-excited atrial fibrillation through an accessory pathway. Nimodipine has its own boxed warning against IV or other parenteral administration because fatal cardiovascular collapse has occurred. Severe overdose can produce prolonged vasoplegic and cardiogenic shock, hyperglycemia, bradycardia, and conduction block and requires immediate poison-center or toxicology-directed care.

Adverse Effects

  • Dihydropyridines: peripheral edema, headache, flushing, dizziness, palpitations, reflex tachycardia, gingival overgrowth, and dose-related hypotension.

  • Diltiazem/verapamil: bradycardia, PR prolongation or AV block, hypotension, worsening heart failure, edema, dizziness, and especially constipation with verapamil.

Contraindications

  • Agent-specific hypersensitivity and severe hypotension matter across the class; advanced or severe aortic stenosis is a major contraindication or high-risk state for several potent arterial vasodilators because coronary perfusion can fall across a fixed outflow obstruction.

  • Avoid diltiazem or verapamil in cardiogenic shock, acute decompensated systolic heart failure, unpaced high-grade AV block or sick sinus syndrome, ventricular tachycardia, and atrial fibrillation/flutter with an accessory bypass tract.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the subclass before giving the drug. For a dihydropyridine, prioritize blood pressure, perfusion, edema, headache, and reflex tachycardia. For diltiazem or verapamil, also verify heart rate, rhythm, PR interval, ventricular function, and every other AV-node-slowing medicine.

  • Verify the formulation and route. Immediate-release versus extended-release products and oral versus IV products are not casual substitutions; clevidipine is a lipid emulsion, nicardipine is an irritant infusion, and nimodipine must never be injected.

Interactions

  • CYP3A inhibitors, inducers, and grapefruit can markedly alter exposure to several agents, but the magnitude differs by drug. Diltiazem and verapamil are also clinically important CYP3A/P-glycoprotein inhibitors that can raise concentrations of selected statins, digoxin, or other substrates.

  • Beta blockers, digoxin, ivabradine, clonidine, antiarrhythmics, anesthetics, and other hypotensive or conduction-slowing drugs can produce additive bradycardia, AV block, myocardial depression, or shock with non-dihydropyridines.

Key Labs

  • Blood pressure, orthostatic symptoms, heart rate, ECG and PR interval for cardiac agents, ventricular function when relevant, edema and daily weight context, renal/hepatic function, and medication-interaction review.

  • In overdose: continuous ECG and hemodynamics, bedside glucose, potassium, magnesium, calcium, acid-base status, lactate, renal/hepatic function, urine output, echocardiographic contractility, and frequent glucose/potassium checks during high-dose insulin therapy.

Nclex Traps

  • Dihydropyridines mainly dilate arteries; diltiazem and verapamil slow the AV node. The suffix or class label never replaces the tissue-effect distinction.

  • Dihydropyridine ankle edema is usually capillary fluid redistribution from precapillary arteriolar dilation, not automatic total-body fluid overload; a loop diuretic may not correct its mechanism.
