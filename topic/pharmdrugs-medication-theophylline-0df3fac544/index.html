Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity to theophylline or formulation; uncontrolled seizure or tachyarrhythmia risk requires specialist evaluation

  • Do not use unsupervised when concentration monitoring, interaction review, or adherence to extended-release instructions is unreliable

Nursing Essentials

  • Record exact product, dose and sample times, meal timing, smoking or vaping combustion exposure, fever, infection, heart/liver status, and every new medication when interpreting a level.

  • Do not crush extended-release products unless the exact label permits it. Assess early GI or CNS symptoms before assuming they are minor.

Interactions

  • Fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin, cimetidine, and selected macrolides can raise levels; rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, and smoke can lower them.

  • Stopping smoking can reduce clearance within days. Caffeine and sympathomimetics add stimulation, while seizure-threshold-lowering drugs magnify toxicity.

Key Labs

  • Correctly timed serum theophylline level interpreted with indication and product, ECG, potassium, magnesium, glucose, pH/bicarbonate, renal and liver function

  • Temperature and fever duration, smoking change, heart-failure status, vomiting, rhythm, neurologic status, and seizure activity

Nclex Traps

  • Combustion smoke, not nicotine itself, induces theophylline clearance; smoking cessation can raise the level.

  • A number inside a traditional range does not exclude toxicity in a susceptible patient, and a level without sample timing is incomplete.

Class

Oral methylxanthine bronchodilator with uncertain mixed PDE/adenosine mechanisms and a narrow therapeutic index

Used To Treat

Selected chronic symptoms and reversible airflow obstruction when safer preferred inhaled therapies are inadequate or unsuitable. It is not first-line asthma or COPD treatment and is not a rapid rescue drug. Dose must be individualized by formulation, clinical response, interacting factors, and correctly timed serum concentration.

Mechanism

Theophylline inhibits PDE3 and to a lesser extent PDE4 at airway-relevant effects, reducing cyclic AMP breakdown and supporting smooth-muscle relaxation. Adenosine A1/A2 receptor antagonism and other mechanisms contribute to respiratory drive, diaphragmatic contractility, tachycardia, gastric secretion, tremor, and seizures; anti-inflammatory actions at lower concentrations are proposed but not a single proven clinical target. Hepatic CYP1A2, CYP3A4, and CYP2E1 metabolism is capacity-limited, so elimination can become nonlinear and a small dose increase can cause a disproportionate level rise. Smoke hydrocarbons induce CYP1A2, whereas fever, liver dysfunction, heart failure, and inhibitors reduce clearance. This is why the 'why' behind every new symptom starts with what changed since the last level.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index. New nausea, repeated vomiting, insomnia, tremor, agitation, rapid or irregular pulse, hypotension, or seizure may be toxicity and can precede catastrophic arrhythmia or status epilepticus. Hold and obtain a correctly timed level, ECG, electrolytes, glucose, and acid-base assessment; severe toxicity needs continuous monitoring and poison-control/toxicology guidance. Do not increase a dose for symptoms before reviewing interactions and clearance changes.

Description

Theophylline is an oral methylxanthine that can relax airway smooth muscle and reduce airway responsiveness, but its helpful concentration lies close to concentrations causing persistent vomiting, arrhythmias, and seizures. Its mechanism is mixed and not fully certain: phosphodiesterase inhibition supports cyclic-AMP-mediated bronchodilation, while adenosine-receptor antagonism contributes to CNS and cardiac stimulation. The defining lesson is dynamic clearance. Cigarette or marijuana smoke induces metabolism; stopping smoking can make a stable dose toxic. Sustained fever, liver disease, heart failure, sepsis, age, and CYP inhibitors can also reduce clearance. A dose is never 'stable' unless the patient's physiology and exposures are stable.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, reflux, diarrhea, headache, insomnia, irritability, tremor, diuresis, and tachycardia

  • Atrial or ventricular arrhythmia, hypotension, seizures, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and death in severe toxicity
