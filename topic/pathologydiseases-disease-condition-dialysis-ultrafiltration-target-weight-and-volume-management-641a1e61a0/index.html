Definition

Dialysis ultrafiltration removes plasma water across a membrane, while target weight is the individualized post-treatment weight at which extracellular volume is judged acceptable without signs of overload or underfilling. Target weight is a clinical estimate that changes with nutrition, illness, pregnancy, amputation, edema, and residual kidney function; it is not the same as an ideal body-weight goal.

Etiology

Water accumulates between treatments when intake and sodium-driven thirst exceed residual urine. During HD, the requested net removal must account for weight gain and fluids given during treatment; during PD, osmotic solution and dwell determine ultrafiltration.

Pathology

Ultrafiltration first removes water from the vascular compartment. Interstitial fluid must refill plasma through Starling forces; low albumin, impaired venous tone, cardiac dysfunction, heat, meals, autonomic disease, and high removal rates can make refill or compensation inadequate.

Pathophysiology

Sodium excess increases thirst and interdialytic gain, forcing more removal over limited treatment time. If ultrafiltration exceeds plasma refill, venous return and cardiac output fall, producing hypotension, cramps, nausea, myocardial or cerebral ischemia, and access thrombosis risk. If target weight is set too high or insufficient fluid is removed, chronic hypertension, edema, pulmonary congestion, and cardiac remodeling persist. If set too low, recurrent underfilling causes dizziness, falls, residual-kidney injury, and organ stunning.

Risk Factors

  • High sodium or fluid intake and loss of residual urine

  • Shortened or missed treatments

  • Heart disease, autonomic neuropathy, low albumin, frailty, infection, or vasodilating medicines

  • Inaccurate target weight after hospitalization, tissue loss, nutrition change, or pregnancy

Signs Symptoms

  • Overload: edema, JVD, crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea, hypertension, and rapid weight gain

  • Underfilling: dizziness, yawning, nausea, cramps, cool skin, hypotension, and syncope

  • Persistent thirst and large interdialytic gain

  • Post-dialysis fatigue or slow recovery after aggressive removal

Diagnostics

  • Trend serial pre/post weights and symptoms rather than one target number

  • Assess lungs, edema, JVP, pressure pattern, orthostasis, residual urine, intake, sodium, and treatment completion

  • Use adjunctive lung ultrasound or bioimpedance when available and clinically useful

  • Investigate heart failure, infection, bleeding, arrhythmia, or access problems when hypotension is not explained by volume removal

Labs

  • Sodium pattern influences thirst but serum sodium does not directly quantify total excess water

  • Albumin affects plasma refill but is also a marker of inflammation and nutrition

  • BNP can be chronically elevated in kidney failure and requires trend/context

  • Residual urine and delivered treatment affect total weekly balance

Treatments

  • Individualize target weight and adjust gradually using serial examination and tolerance

  • Reduce sodium burden and align fluid plan with residual urine and treatment schedule

  • Lengthen or increase treatment opportunities when large removal cannot be tolerated safely

  • Treat heart failure, infection, arrhythmia, autonomic dysfunction, or other causes of poor tolerance

Nursing Priorities

  • Compare pre-dialysis weight with the individualized post-dialysis target, recent intake, edema, lung sounds, blood pressure, and symptoms because prescribed ultrafiltration should remove accumulated fluid without depleting circulating volume.

  • Verify treatment duration, ultrafiltration goal and rate, dialysate prescription, access function, and interval weight gain because shortened treatment or an unrealistic fluid goal increases both overload and intradialytic instability.

  • Monitor blood pressure, pulse, cramps, nausea, dizziness, chest symptoms, consciousness, access pressures, and machine alarms throughout treatment because rapid plasma-volume decline can cause hypotension, ischemia, and inadequate dialysis.

  • Respond to symptomatic hypotension according to the dialysis protocol, reassess the ultrafiltration goal, and position safely because continuing fluid removal despite poor perfusion can injure the heart, brain, and residual kidney function.

  • Escalate for systolic pressure below the unit threshold with symptoms, chest pain, severe dyspnea, new hypoxemia, altered consciousness, dysrhythmia, absent access thrill, or refractory cramping because shock, pulmonary edema, cardiac ischemia, or access failure needs immediate intervention.

Complications

  • Intradialytic hypotension, cramps, syncope, falls, and access thrombosis

  • Myocardial, cerebral, mesenteric, and residual-kidney ischemia

  • Chronic pulmonary edema, hypertension, and ventricular remodeling

  • Treatment intolerance, shortened sessions, and underdialysis

Contraindications

  • Do not chase a target weight through symptomatic hypoperfusion

  • Do not assume scale gain is all fluid or scale loss is all successful decongestion

  • Avoid reflex fluid administration for every low BP without checking the broader cause

Red Flags

  • Symptomatic hypotension below the dialysis unit's treatment threshold or recurrent syncope

  • Severe dyspnea, new hypoxemia, frothy sputum, crackles, or hypertensive pulmonary edema

  • Chest pain, new dysrhythmia, altered consciousness, seizure, or focal neurologic deficit

  • Absent access thrill, uncontrolled access bleeding, machine blood leak, or suspected air embolism

Patient Education

  • Follow the individualized sodium and fluid plan between treatments because large interval gains require faster removal and increase cramps, hypotension, and cardiac strain.

  • Record daily weights under similar conditions and report a persistent change because true target weight changes with nutrition, illness, and residual kidney function.

Nclex Traps

  • Ultrafiltration removes plasma water first; interstitial refill takes time

  • Edema and intravascular underfilling can coexist

  • Target weight changes with body composition

  • Large interdialytic gain creates an ultrafiltration-rate problem, not only a scale problem

Causal Links

  • Sodium intake -> thirst and water intake -> interdialytic gain -> higher required ultrafiltration

  • Ultrafiltration faster than plasma refill -> lower venous return -> hypotension and ischemia

  • Target weight too high -> persistent extracellular overload -> hypertension and pulmonary congestion

  • Target weight too low -> recurrent underfilling -> cramps, falls, and organ stunning
