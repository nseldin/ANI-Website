Definition

Aphasia is an acquired language problem caused by brain dysfunction. It can impair speaking, understanding speech, naming, repetition, reading, or writing even when the speech muscles can move.

Plain Language

The person may know what they want to communicate but cannot find or form the words, may not understand language, or may have both problems.

Why It Matters

Sudden aphasia is a stroke emergency. Aphasia is not loss of intelligence and should not be mislabeled as confusion or noncooperation; use supported communication while urgently evaluating the cause.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

Establish the patient's usual language and communication method, then assess spontaneous speech, naming, comprehension of spoken and written commands, repetition, reading, and writing while checking hearing, vision, attention, speech-motor control, and focal neurologic findings.

Positive Finding

There is a new acquired impairment of spoken or written language expression, comprehension, naming, repetition, reading, or writing that cannot be explained only by weakness of the speech muscles.

Mechanism

Injury to the dominant hemisphere's distributed language network disrupts formation, selection, understanding, or transmission of words and meaning.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Aphasia finding raises concern for damage or dysfunction of dominant-hemisphere language networks from stroke, traumatic brain injury, tumor, seizure, infection, or neurodegenerative disease because injury to the dominant hemisphere's distributed language network disrupts formation, selection, understanding, or transmission of words and meaning. Evidence Limitations

Limited shared language, low literacy, hearing or vision loss, delirium, fatigue, developmental language disorder, and poor testing conditions can mimic or hide aphasia. Therefore, Aphasia cannot confirm or exclude damage or dysfunction of dominant-hemisphere language networks from stroke, traumatic brain injury, tumor, seizure, infection, or neurodegenerative disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • dysarthria

  • apraxia of speech

  • delirium

  • hearing impairment

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as dysarthria, apraxia of speech, delirium, hearing impairment.

False Negative Situations

Aphasia may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of damage or dysfunction of dominant-hemisphere language networks from stroke, traumatic brain injury, tumor, seizure, infection, or neurodegenerative disease.

Clinical Significance

Aphasia impairs language expression or understanding, reading, or writing; it does not itself diagnose impaired swallowing. After stroke, aphasia may complicate communication about a swallowing plan, but the person may have aphasia, dysphagia, both, or neither.
