Definition

Expiratory grunting is an observed and audible respiratory sign. It is a clue to alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema. A positive finding means the patient partially closes the glottis and produces a grunt.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Expiratory grunting means the patient partially closes the glottis and produces a grunt.

Why It Matters

The observed Expiratory grunting finding can signal alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema. Glottic braking raises end-expiratory pressure to preserve functional residual capacity and keep alveoli open. Expiratory grunting cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema

Assessment Method

Listen for a short low sound at end-expiration.

Positive Finding

The patient partially closes the glottis and produces a grunt.

Mechanism

Glottic braking raises end-expiratory pressure to preserve functional residual capacity and keep alveoli open.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Expiratory grunting finding raises concern for alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema because glottic braking raises end-expiratory pressure to preserve functional residual capacity and keep alveoli open. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Expiratory grunting does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Expiratory grunting to focus evaluation of alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema and the reviewed alternatives: breath holding, moaning, upper-airway sound. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Listen for a short low sound at end-expiration. Evidence Limitations

Pain and vocalization can mimic grunting. Therefore, Expiratory grunting cannot confirm or exclude alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • breath holding

  • upper-airway sound

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as breath holding, moaning, upper-airway sound.

False Negative Situations

Expiratory grunting may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of alveolar collapse risk in infants or severe pulmonary edema.
