Definition

Cluster headache is a primary headache disorder in the trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia family. It causes repeated attacks of excruciating, strictly one-sided orbital, supraorbital, or temporal pain with same-side autonomic findings such as tearing, red eye, nasal symptoms, eyelid swelling, ptosis, or miosis and often intense restlessness.

Pathology

Cluster headache usually produces no destructive brain lesion. The attack reflects abnormal activation of trigeminal pain pathways together with a cranial parasympathetic reflex, while hypothalamic and circadian networks help explain why attacks can recur at remarkably similar times of day and in seasonal bouts.

Pathophysiology

  • Trigeminal nociceptive activation generates the severe orbital or temporal pain. Coupling to the trigeminal-autonomic reflex activates parasympathetic outflow, producing lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, facial sweating, and eyelid edema on the painful side.

  • Hypothalamic and biological-clock networks appear to organize the rhythmic attack pattern. That relationship explains predictable nocturnal attacks and clustered active periods, but it does not mean that one sleep habit or one trigger is the root cause.

Etiology

No single initiating cause has been proved. Genetic susceptibility contributes in a minority, and alcohol or nitroglycerin can provoke attacks during an active cluster period. These exposures are triggers rather than the disease itself; outside an active bout, the same exposure may not trigger pain.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of cluster headache

  • Male sex, although the disorder occurs in every sex

  • Tobacco exposure as an epidemiologic association rather than a diagnostic requirement

  • An active cluster bout during which alcohol or vasodilator exposure can precipitate an attack

Signs Symptoms

  • Very severe unilateral orbital, supraorbital, or temporal pain lasting 15 to 180 minutes when untreated

  • Attack frequency from one every other day to as many as eight per day during an active period

  • Ipsilateral tearing, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion or rhinorrhea, eyelid edema, facial sweating, ptosis, or miosis

  • Pacing, rocking, or inability to remain still, which contrasts with the quiet immobility many patients prefer during migraine

  • Attacks that recur at a similar hour, frequently wake the patient from sleep, and cluster into episodic or chronic patterns

Diagnostics

  • Diagnosis is clinical and requires a characteristic attack pattern under current International Classification of Headache Disorders criteria; there is no confirmatory blood test.

  • Document duration, frequency, laterality, autonomic findings, restlessness, bout length, remission periods, triggers, and response to oxygen or triptan because these details separate cluster headache from other short-lasting headaches.

  • A first bout, abnormal neurologic or ocular examination, atypical timing, persistent Horner findings between attacks, or another secondary-headache red flag warrants specialist assessment and often neuroimaging.

Labs

  • No laboratory value confirms cluster headache.

  • Laboratory testing is directed by the differential, such as inflammatory studies for suspected giant cell arteritis or metabolic and toxicology testing when systemic illness or exposure is plausible.

  • Normal laboratory results do not remove the need for imaging or emergency assessment when thunderclap onset, focal deficits, fever, glaucoma findings, or altered consciousness are present.

Assessment

Establish the exact onset, attack duration and frequency, side of pain, same-side eye and nasal findings, pacing or agitation, prior bout pattern, medication use, oxygen response, vital signs, visual symptoms, pupils, extraocular movements, and full neurologic examination. The first priority is distinguishing a verified recurrent pattern from hemorrhage, dissection, glaucoma, infection, or a sellar or cavernous-sinus lesion.

Differential

Differentiate cluster headache from migraine, paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT or SUNA, trigeminal neuralgia, acute angle-closure glaucoma, sinus or dental disease, carotid dissection, and cavernous-sinus or pituitary disease. A thunderclap or persistently abnormal examination demands evaluation for a secondary cause rather than treatment based only on attack location.

Treatments

  • Acute treatment uses high-flow 100% oxygen through a nonrebreather mask with reservoir and/or a rapidly absorbed subcutaneous or intranasal triptan when safe; the attack is often too brief for oral medication to act in time.

  • Verapamil is commonly used as preventive treatment during a bout, with baseline and follow-up ECG monitoring because conduction slowing can develop during dose escalation.

  • A greater occipital nerve block or a short specialist-directed corticosteroid bridge may suppress attacks while preventive therapy takes effect.

  • Lithium, topiramate, selected neuromodulation, or other preventive options are specialist-led when first-line therapy is unsuitable or ineffective.

Contraindications

  • Do not present opioids, oral triptans, routine acetaminophen, or routine NSAIDs as preferred acute therapy; slow onset and medication-related harm make them poor matches for a short, rapidly peaking attack.

  • Triptans require product-label screening for ischemic coronary, cerebrovascular, or peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, and relevant interactions.

  • Verapamil should not be escalated without ECG and clinical monitoring because bradycardia, PR prolongation, or atrioventricular block can occur.

  • Oxygen must never be used near smoking, flame, heat, or sparking equipment.

Safety Considerations

  • Severe distress, sleep loss, and suicidal thinking require direct assessment; the pain burden can be extreme even though the disorder does not progressively destroy brain tissue.

  • Oxygen effectiveness depends on rapid access, adequate prescribed flow, a reservoir nonrebreather setup, and a good mask seal.

  • A first or changed attack pattern is not assumed to be cluster headache because aneurysm, dissection, glaucoma, infection, and pituitary disease can mimic it.

  • Preventive choice and licensing vary for children, pregnancy, lactation, and cardiac disease and require current specialist and product guidance.

Nursing Priorities

  • Set up prescribed high-flow oxygen promptly, verify the nonrebreather reservoir remains inflated, maintain fire precautions, and document time to meaningful relief.

  • Record onset, laterality, duration, autonomic signs, activity or restlessness, vital signs, neurologic and ocular findings, treatment timing, response, and adverse effects.

  • Screen cardiovascular history before triptan administration and monitor pulse, blood pressure, and symptoms during verapamil therapy according to the treatment plan.

  • Teach oxygen fire safety, correct mask use, a simple attack diary, prescribed triptan limits, and avoidance of known personal triggers during an active bout.

  • Assess sleep disruption, coping, depression, and suicidal ideation and escalate urgent psychological risk immediately.

Red Flags

  • First or worst headache, thunderclap onset, new focal deficit, seizure, syncope, confusion, fever, meningismus, or reduced consciousness

  • Painful red eye with fixed or poorly reactive pupil, visual loss, or marked ocular pressure symptoms

  • Persistent ptosis, miosis, ophthalmoplegia, or other neurologic finding between attacks

  • New headache during pregnancy or postpartum, after trauma, with cancer or immunocompromise, or in a substantially changed pattern

  • Active suicidal thinking or inability to remain safe because of pain

Complications

  • Profound sleep disruption, impaired work or school function, anxiety, depression, and suicidal crisis

  • Medication adverse effects or medication-overuse headache when ineffective rescue drugs are repeatedly used

  • Injury or unsafe behavior during severe agitation

  • Delayed diagnosis of a secondary structural or vascular mimic when the pattern is assumed rather than examined

Prognosis

Cluster headache is highly disabling but does not ordinarily cause progressive structural brain injury. Episodic bouts can recur after long remissions, while chronic cluster headache has little or no sustained remission. Fast acute access and correctly monitored preventive therapy can substantially reduce attack burden.

Prevention

Preventive medication is used during the active period and individualized for episodic or chronic disease. Avoid personal triggers such as alcohol during a bout, maintain ready access to prescribed oxygen and rescue treatment, and use follow-up to adjust prevention rather than relying on repeated emergency visits.

Patient Education

  • Cluster headache is a neurologic pain disorder with an autonomic reflex; eye and nasal findings are part of the attack and not proof of a sinus infection.

  • Use oxygen exactly as prescribed and keep all smoking, flames, candles, and heat sources away from the equipment.

  • Take the prescribed rapid rescue treatment at attack onset and do not substitute repeated opioids or slow oral remedies.

  • Seek emergency care for thunderclap onset, persistent weakness or speech change, visual loss, fever with neck stiffness, confusion, fainting, or a pattern unlike prior verified attacks.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need pediatric neurologic assessment because presentation, medication approval, and weight-based administration differ.

  • Pregnancy and lactation require individualized review of oxygen, triptans, preventive drugs, and secondary-headache risks.

  • Patients with cardiovascular disease need nonvasoconstrictive rescue planning and careful preventive selection.

Nclex Traps

  • Cluster headache causes restlessness and pacing; many patients with migraine prefer stillness in a dark room.

  • A nonrebreather mask with an adequate prescribed flow is the acute oxygen setup; low-flow nasal cannula is not equivalent.

  • Same-side tearing and rhinorrhea do not automatically mean sinusitis.

  • Complete the neurologic and ocular red-flag assessment before treating a first attack as a familiar primary headache.
