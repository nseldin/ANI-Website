Definition

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a motile, nonfermenting Gram-negative bacillus found in water, soil, and moist built environments. It rarely causes disease in healthy intact tissue but becomes a formidable opportunistic pathogen when burns, devices, neutropenia, cystic fibrosis, prolonged hospitalization, or broad antibiotic exposure weaken normal barriers. Its environmental persistence and intrinsic and acquired resistance make precise culture and susceptibility testing especially important.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    P. aeruginosa is one species within the Pseudomonas genus; the shortened word Pseudomonas is therefore a search term rather than a species-level identity alias. Multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa describes a resistance phenotype, not a different organism. Keeping species and phenotype separate prevents a susceptible isolate from inheriting warnings or therapy assumptions that apply only to a resistant strain.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The organism is a slender Gram-negative rod with polar motility, no spores, and respiratory rather than fermentative metabolism. It grows well with oxygen but can use nitrate or nitrite as alternative electron acceptors under anoxic conditions, including oxygen-limited biofilms. Its low-permeability outer membrane, efflux pumps, and inducible or acquired resistance mechanisms can reduce antimicrobial activity. It forms biofilms in airways and on devices, and some strains produce blue-green pigments, but pigment is not required for identification.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Reservoirs include water, moist environmental sites, sinks, respiratory equipment, and contaminated devices. In healthcare, transfer can occur through contaminated hands, equipment, solutions, or environmental sources; endogenous colonization can also precede infection. Moisture and disrupted barriers explain the organism's association with ventilators, urinary catheters, burns, wounds, and chronically damaged airways.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Attachment and extracellular-polysaccharide production support persistent biofilm growth, while secreted enzymes and toxins can injure epithelium and impair immune defenses. In the lung, inflammation, mucus retention, and bacterial persistence worsen ventilation and tissue damage. In neutropenia or severe burns, weak containment permits bloodstream invasion, ecthyma gangrenosum, shock, and rapid systemic deterioration.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    P. aeruginosa can cause ventilator-associated or hospital-acquired pneumonia, bloodstream infection, catheter-associated UTI, burn and wound infection, malignant otitis externa, keratitis, and chronic infection in cystic fibrosis or bronchiectasis. Findings are syndrome-specific rather than species-specific. New hypotension, escalating oxygen needs, rapidly spreading wound injury, neutropenic fever, or eye pain after contact-lens exposure needs urgent evaluation.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Culture from the clinically involved site establishes the organism and permits susceptibility testing. A Gram stain can show Gram-negative rods and inflammatory cells but cannot identify P. aeruginosa or its resistance profile. Specimen quality and source control matter: growth from a nonsterile respiratory specimen may represent colonization, while the same organism in blood carries very different significance.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Standard Precautions, meticulous hand hygiene, correct device care, water-management practices, environmental cleaning, and prompt removal of unnecessary invasive devices reduce risk. Treatment must never be chosen from the species name alone. Site, severity, prior antibiotics, local antibiogram, source control, and isolate susceptibility determine whether one or more antipseudomonal agents are appropriate.
