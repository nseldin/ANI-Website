Range

Ceruloplasmin is usually reported in mg/dL or g/L, but the reference interval depends on the laboratory, assay, age, and population. The 2025 EASL-ERN Wilson-disease guideline lists 20-40 mg/dL as a general reference example and <10 mg/dL as a high-suspicion finding in the correct context; these are not stand-alone diagnostic cutoffs. Immunologic assays may measure inactive apoceruloplasmin as well as copper-carrying holoceruloplasmin, and inflammation, liver function, protein loss, nutrition, pregnancy, and estrogen exposure can change interpretation. Follow the performing laboratory's preparation instructions; MedlinePlus lists no special preparation for the test itself.

Why

A low ceruloplasmin result signals reduced circulating copper-carrying protein and is associated with Wilson disease, severe liver disease, kidney or other protein loss, malnutrition or malabsorption, copper deficiency, and excess zinc exposure; a high result may reflect an acute-phase response, meaning a rise during inflammation, infection, or tissue injury, or pregnancy or estrogen exposure. Promptly assess jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain or swelling, edema, bruising or bleeding, tremor, rigidity or abnormal movement, speech or swallowing change, gait or coordination, psychiatric change, anemia or hemolysis clues, nutrition and malabsorption risk, kidney or urine-protein findings, zinc or copper supplements, medicines, family history, and prior copper studies. Cause-directed evaluation for suspected Wilson disease includes liver biochemistries, CBC and INR, serum copper, a correctly collected 24-hour urinary copper, slit-lamp eye examination for Kayser-Fleischer rings, and neurologic and hepatology review; ATP7B genetic testing, brain imaging, or liver copper measurement may be used when the combined evidence remains uncertain. Escalate urgently for acute liver failure or rapid deterioration with jaundice, coagulopathy (impaired clotting) or bleeding, encephalopathy, or Coombs-negative hemolysis (red-cell destruction without antibodies detected on the direct antiglobulin test); severe new neurologic or psychiatric instability also needs urgent clinical evaluation. Ceruloplasmin alone does not diagnose Wilson disease or copper deficiency, and a normal result does not exclude Wilson disease; interpret the value with the clinical pattern and the full copper evaluation.

High Causes

  • Inflammation, infection, or tissue injury causing an acute-phase response

  • Pregnancy, combined oral contraceptives, or estrogen therapy

  • Selected inflammatory or malignant conditions

  • Assay and population variation

Low Causes

  • Wilson disease, although some affected people have a normal result

  • Severe or decompensated liver disease with reduced protein synthesis

  • Kidney or gastrointestinal protein loss

  • Malnutrition or malabsorption

  • Copper deficiency, including excess zinc exposure as one possible cause

  • Menkes disease or another rare copper-transport disorder

Critical Concerns

  • Acute liver failure or rapid deterioration with jaundice, rising INR or bleeding, encephalopathy, or Coombs-negative hemolysis requires emergency liver and Wilson-disease evaluation.

  • A low value alone is not a Wilson-disease diagnosis, and a normal value does not exclude Wilson disease.

  • Inflammation can raise ceruloplasmin, while liver synthetic failure, protein loss, and malnutrition can lower it; the direction of the result is not specific to copper overload.

Nursing Considerations

  • Verify the performing assay and interval, pregnancy or estrogen context, inflammatory illness, liver and kidney history, nutrition and malabsorption risks, zinc or copper supplements, medicines, family history, and prior copper studies.

  • Assess liver, neurologic, psychiatric, hemolysis, edema and protein-loss findings; coordinate CBC/INR, liver biochemistries, serum copper, correctly collected 24-hour urinary copper, slit-lamp examination, and specialist evaluation when indicated.

  • Do not start or stop copper or zinc therapy from one result; trend symptoms and the clinician-directed copper evaluation.

Nclex Traps

  • Low ceruloplasmin is supportive evidence, not proof of Wilson disease; normal ceruloplasmin does not rule it out.

  • High ceruloplasmin often reflects inflammation, pregnancy, or estrogen rather than copper excess.

  • Protein loss, malnutrition, and severe liver dysfunction can mimic a low Wilson-disease pattern.
