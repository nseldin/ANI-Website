Risk Factors

  • Dominant superior-division MCA occlusion can injure frontal language and nearby face-arm motor cortex.

  • Prior language lateralization, bilingual organization, and old brain injury can alter the expected pattern.

  • Seizure or postictal dysfunction can imitate acute aphasia, but vascular onset still demands emergency imaging.

Signs Symptoms

  • Speech is nonfluent, effortful, and short, with omitted connecting words or telegraphic phrases.

  • The patient may follow simple commands and recognize errors but struggle to name, repeat, write, or explain needs.

  • Right face-arm weakness or apraxia may accompany the aphasia because adjacent dominant frontal networks are involved.

Diagnostics

  • Test spontaneous speech, naming, repetition, comprehension, reading, writing, and command following because one conversational answer cannot define the syndrome.

  • Separate aphasia from dysarthria, apraxia of speech, delirium, hearing loss, and reduced consciousness.

  • Activate NCCT and vascular imaging for sudden onset because a disabling aphasia may represent treatable LVO even with a low NIHSS total.

Labs

  • Check glucose immediately because hypoglycemia can cause focal language dysfunction.

  • Use acute stroke safety labs and anticoagulant history for reperfusion decisions without treating aphasia as mere confusion.

  • No blood test diagnoses aphasia; serial language examination tracks the network deficit.

Treatments

  • Evaluate sudden disabling aphasia for reperfusion because inability to communicate is functionally important even when motor findings are mild.

  • Use speech-language therapy and supported communication because repeated meaningful practice promotes recovery and participation.

  • Treat the vascular cause and mood consequences because recurrence prevention and emotional recovery both influence long-term function.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess nonfluent effortful speech, naming, repetition, comprehension, writing, frustration, right face and arm strength, and exact onset because dominant frontal language injury often preserves awareness while limiting expression.

  • Activate the stroke pathway, document last-known-well, check glucose, and prepare emergency brain and vascular imaging because sudden expressive aphasia is a time-sensitive cortical deficit even without severe weakness.

  • Use yes-or-no questions, communication boards, writing or gestures, sufficient response time, and one speaker because language output failure does not mean loss of intelligence or inability to understand.

  • Monitor neurologic examination, blood pressure, rhythm, oxygenation, swallowing, aspiration, mood, frustration, and treatment complications because edema, hemorrhage, recurrent ischemia, and depression can worsen recovery.

  • Escalate for new or worsening aphasia, weakness, reduced consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, seizure, or airway compromise because recurrent stroke, edema, and hemorrhagic transformation need immediate reassessment.

Complications

  • Communication failure can lead to untreated pain, unsafe consent, isolation, depression, and reduced rehabilitation participation.

  • Dysphagia may coexist because dominant MCA stroke can involve nearby swallowing networks.

  • Family members may mistake impaired output for impaired intelligence or memory, damaging autonomy.

Contraindications

  • Do not call expressive aphasia confusion without testing comprehension.

  • Do not assume comprehension is completely normal; complex syntax and rapid speech may be difficult.

  • Do not equate slurred speech with Broca aphasia because dysarthria is a motor-speech disorder.

Red Flags

  • Sudden inability to produce words, effortful speech, facial droop, or arm weakness

  • Worsening language or motor deficit, reduced consciousness, or recurrent symptoms

  • Severe headache, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, or seizure

  • Coughing with swallowing, wet voice, aspiration, hypoxemia, or airway compromise

Patient Education

  • Give extra response time and use writing, pictures, gestures, or yes-or-no choices because difficulty speaking does not equal lack of understanding.

  • Call emergency services for sudden speech difficulty even if the person understands everything because expressive aphasia may be an acute stroke.

Nclex Traps

  • Broca aphasia is nonfluent language impairment, not simply weak speech muscles.

  • Preserved simple comprehension does not mean every complex statement is understood.

  • A low NIHSS with disabling aphasia is not automatically a nondisabling stroke.

Name

Broca aphasia

Category

Stroke language syndrome

Definition

Broca aphasia is a nonfluent language disorder in which speech output is effortful, sparse, and grammatically simplified, while basic comprehension is relatively better preserved. Naming, repetition, writing, and complex comprehension can also be impaired, so it is more than a problem moving the mouth. It usually follows dominant inferior-frontal or connected-network injury, often in the superior division of the middle cerebral artery, and may occur with contralateral face-arm weakness.

Etiology

Acute dominant MCA ischemia is a major cause, but intracranial hemorrhage, tumor, trauma, infection, seizure, or neurodegenerative disease can injure the same network. Sudden onset is treated as stroke until proven otherwise because reperfusion is time-sensitive.

Pathology

Language production depends on a distributed dominant frontal, insular, premotor, temporal, parietal, and white-matter network. A Broca-pattern syndrome emerges when frontal language planning and its connections are disrupted. The patient knows the intended message but cannot efficiently encode it into fluent grammatical speech.

Pathophysiology

Output becomes slow and effortful because lexical retrieval, grammar, and speech-language sequencing are impaired. Comprehension for simple statements may be good, but complex syntax can fail because the same network helps process grammatical relationships. Awareness is often preserved, which can produce frustration or depression. Dysarthria can coexist, but dysarthria changes articulation while language content, naming, reading, and writing remain conceptually intact.
