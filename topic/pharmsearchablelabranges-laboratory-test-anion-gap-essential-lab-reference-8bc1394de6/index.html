Category

Acid-base labs

Name

Anion gap

Range

The anion gap is calculated from measured electrolytes, commonly sodium minus the sum of chloride and bicarbonate; some laboratories also include potassium in the calculation. A range around 8-12 mEq/L without potassium is a common example, but the formula, analyzer, albumin concentration, and reporting laboratory change the expected interval, so use the printed range rather than one universal cutoff.

Why

An increased anion-gap result signals unmeasured negatively charged particles (anions) and raises concern for high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Important causes include diabetic, alcoholic, or starvation ketoacidosis; lactic acidosis; reduced acid excretion in kidney failure; and toxic alcohol or salicylate poisoning. Promptly assess vital signs, perfusion, mental status, breathing pattern, hydration, glucose and ketone risk, kidney function, medicines, alcohol use, and possible exposure. Cause-directed evaluation can include repeat electrolytes and bicarbonate, a blood gas, direct beta-hydroxybutyrate, lactate, creatinine, measured osmolality or an osmolar gap, and toxin testing as ordered for the presentation. Escalate immediately when a high gap accompanies shock, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, seizure, severe acidemia, visual symptoms, suspected poisoning, or rapid deterioration. The anion gap alone does not diagnose the cause or establish or resolve DKA; a normal gap does not exclude a serious or mixed acid-base disorder, hypoalbuminemia can lower the gap, and hyperchloremic non-anion-gap acidosis may appear during DKA treatment. The 2024 adult hyperglycemic-crises consensus advises against using anion gap as a first-line DKA diagnostic or resolution criterion when direct beta-hydroxybutyrate measurement is available.

High Causes

  • Diabetic, alcoholic, or starvation ketoacidosis

  • Lactic acidosis

  • Kidney failure with reduced acid excretion

  • Toxic alcohol ingestion

  • Salicylate toxicity

  • More than one acid-base process occurring together

Low Causes

  • Hypoalbuminemia

  • Laboratory or calculation variation

  • Less commonly paraproteinemia or selected intoxications

Critical Concerns

  • A high anion gap with low bicarbonate or acidemia raises concern for dangerous metabolic acidosis, but urgency is determined by the full clinical and acid-base pattern.

  • Shock, respiratory distress, altered consciousness, seizure, severe acidemia, visual symptoms, suspected toxic alcohol or salicylate exposure, or rapid deterioration requires immediate escalation.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess vital signs, perfusion, neurologic and respiratory status, hydration, glucose and ketone risk, kidney function, medicines, alcohol use, and possible exposure.

  • Trend the gap only with bicarbonate, blood gas, direct beta-hydroxybutyrate, lactate, renal function, albumin, and cause-directed toxin studies as ordered; do not use the gap alone to declare DKA resolved.

Nclex Traps

  • A high gap is a clue to unmeasured acids, not the name of the diagnosis.

  • Do not use a persistent or normalized anion gap alone to judge DKA resolution when direct beta-hydroxybutyrate is available; saline-related hyperchloremic acidosis can confuse the recovery pattern.
