Used To Treat

Known or suspected carbon-monoxide exposure. Immediate high-concentration oxygen is the specific displacement therapy; hyperbaric oxygen is a referral decision based on clinical severity, pregnancy, cardiac and neurologic findings, acidosis, exposure context, and local expertise rather than one universal carboxyhemoglobin number.

Description

Carbon monoxide is an inhaled gas that binds hemoglobin far more avidly than oxygen, reducing oxygen carriage and release to tissues, so suspected exposure is a diagnostic emergency requiring immediate source removal and high-concentration oxygen even when ordinary pulse oximetry looks normal. Several people with headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, syncope, or flu-like symptoms in the same space are a diagnostic alarm because carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. Remove the patient and rescuers from exposure, call emergency services, give high-concentration oxygen immediately, and draw co-oximetry without delaying treatment. The carboxyhemoglobin level falls after fresh air or oxygen and correlates imperfectly with organ injury, so a low measured value does not erase a convincing history, syncope, ischemia, neurologic findings, pregnancy risk, or severe acidosis. Delayed cognitive, mood, gait, and neurologic symptoms can emerge after apparent recovery.

Mechanism

Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with far greater affinity than oxygen, reduces available oxygen-carrying sites, and left-shifts remaining oxyhemoglobin so oxygen unloads poorly in tissue. It also binds myoglobin and mitochondrial cytochromes and promotes oxidative, inflammatory, and lipid-peroxidation injury, explaining cardiac and neurologic damage beyond the blood level. High alveolar oxygen creates a gradient that accelerates carbon-monoxide dissociation. Hyperbaric pressure further increases dissolved plasma oxygen and accelerates elimination, but trial results and selection thresholds are heterogeneous, making expert clinical referral more appropriate than a number-only rule.

Administration Timing

  • Do not enter an unsafe scene. Call 911, remove exposed people to fresh air, and give 100% oxygen by an effective high-flow device immediately; intubate and ventilate when airway, smoke injury, or consciousness requires it. Draw co-oximetry without delaying oxygen.

  • Obtain ECG, troponin, neurologic examination, pregnancy status, blood gas and lactate as indicated, and contact a hyperbaric center early for loss of consciousness, persistent neurologic deficit, cardiac ischemia or dysfunction, severe acidosis, high-risk pregnancy, or other severe features. Transfer risk and local capability are part of the decision.

  • Continue observation and oxygen until symptoms and clinical risk are resolved under protocol. Provide explicit follow-up for delayed neurocognitive or psychiatric symptoms and correct the environmental source before anyone returns.

Nursing Essentials

  • Ask about generators, furnaces, fireplaces, vehicles, boats, fires, solvents, faulty vents, alarms, and whether others or pets are ill. Notify fire or utility authorities as appropriate so the location is made safe.

  • Use co-oximetry; record oxygen device and start time before interpreting the level. Standard pulse oximetry, calculated arterial saturation, and cherry-red skin are unreliable screening tools.

  • Assess headache, cognition, gait, focal findings, syncope, chest pain, pregnancy, trauma, burns, airway soot, and cyanide risk because neurologic, cardiac, fetal, and co-toxicant findings determine urgency and hyperbaric consultation. Trend rhythm, blood pressure, neurologic status, and oxygen-delivery markers to detect deterioration that a falling carboxyhemoglobin value can miss.

Key Labs

  • Carboxyhemoglobin by co-oximetry, ECG, troponin, glucose, blood gas, bicarbonate, lactate, and pregnancy testing when relevant. A normal PaO2 measures dissolved oxygen and does not exclude impaired hemoglobin carriage.

  • Smokers can have a higher baseline carboxyhemoglobin, while prior oxygen can lower the measured value; interpret the result with time, symptoms, and exposure rather than a universal cutoff.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause headache, vomiting, confusion, syncope, seizure, myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia, shock, coma, fetal injury, delayed neurologic sequelae, and death.

  • High-concentration oxygen can cause absorption atelectasis or oxygen toxicity with prolonged exposure. Hyperbaric treatment can cause ear or sinus barotrauma, pulmonary barotrauma, claustrophobia, oxygen seizure, and transfer delay.

Contraindications

  • Do not withhold immediate high-concentration oxygen while waiting for a carboxyhemoglobin result, because oxygen accelerates carbon-monoxide dissociation and treatment delay prolongs tissue hypoxia. Do not use ordinary pulse oximetry or a normal PaO2 to rule out poisoning, because pulse oximetry may misclassify carboxyhemoglobin and PaO2 reflects dissolved rather than hemoglobin-bound oxygen.

  • Untreated pneumothorax is a major hyperbaric contraindication and unstable airway or equipment can make transfer unsafe, but these issues require expert stabilization rather than abandoning oxygen therapy.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Syncope, persistent confusion or focal findings, seizure, chest pain, ischemic ECG or troponin, dysrhythmia, shock, severe acidosis, pregnancy with significant exposure, or smoke inhalation requires emergency and hyperbaric consultation. New cognitive, mood, gait, or neurologic symptoms days to weeks later require reassessment.

Interactions

  • Cyanide can coexist in enclosed-space smoke inhalation and causes cellular oxygen-use failure despite oxygen therapy; treat each suspected mechanism without waiting for delayed levels.

  • Alcohol, sedatives, trauma, hypoglycemia, and infection can mimic or compound altered mental status; improvement on oxygen does not exclude them.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Fetal hemoglobin binds carbon monoxide and fetal elimination is slower; seek early hyperbaric and obstetric input even when maternal symptoms or levels seem modest.

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Nonspecific vomiting, sleepiness, or behavior change and higher metabolic demand can obscure severe exposure; assess every household member.

  • Population

    Older adults or cardiovascular disease

    Note

    Limited coronary reserve raises ischemia and arrhythmia risk at a burden tolerated poorly by a healthy adult.

Evidence Limitations

  • Hyperbaric oxygen trials and thresholds are heterogeneous, and carboxyhemoglobin correlates imperfectly with outcome. CDC recommends considering clinical severity and consulting a hyperbaric facility rather than using a single universal cutoff.

Nclex Traps

  • Pulse oximetry can look normal because it cannot reliably distinguish carboxyhemoglobin.

  • A normal PaO2 does not measure oxygen carried by functional hemoglobin and cannot rule out carbon-monoxide poisoning, because PaO2 reflects only dissolved plasma oxygen.

  • The measured carboxyhemoglobin can be low after oxygen and still represent a serious earlier exposure.

Regulatory Status

Oxygen and hyperbaric oxygen are established treatments, but hyperbaric referral is a clinical and facility decision rather than an FDA-labeled antidote threshold. Current CDC guidance and regional hyperbaric expertise govern selection.

Boxed Warning

CARBON-MONOXIDE WARNING: call 911, leave the source safely, and give high-concentration oxygen immediately. A normal pulse oximeter or PaO2 does not rule out poisoning; use co-oximetry and clinical severity, and contact a hyperbaric center early for neurologic, cardiac, acidotic, or pregnancy risk.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
