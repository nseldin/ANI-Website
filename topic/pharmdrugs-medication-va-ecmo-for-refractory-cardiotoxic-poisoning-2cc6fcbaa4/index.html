Used To Treat

Selected severe, potentially reversible cardiotoxic poisonings with refractory cardiogenic shock, malignant dysrhythmia, or cardiac arrest despite optimized toxin-specific resuscitation. Evidence is strongest when pump failure is dominant; VA-ECMO is not established as routine rescue for isolated vasoplegia or noncardiogenic collapse.

Description

Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is temporary mechanical circulation and gas exchange used as a bridge while a reversible poison is metabolized, redistributed, neutralized, or removed. It can preserve brain, heart, kidney, and liver perfusion when poisoned myocardium cannot generate adequate output despite high-dose insulin, vasopressors, antidotes, and ventilation. The key concept is time: many cardiovascular drugs are profoundly toxic for hours but do not permanently destroy the heart. VA-ECMO can carry the patient through that interval. It is not itself an antidote, is available only in specialized systems, and exposes the patient to major bleeding, thrombosis, limb, neurologic, and infection risks. The AHA 2025 guidance supports extracorporeal life support in selected refractory cardiotoxic poisonings, including severe calcium-channel-blocker shock.

Mechanism

Venous blood is drained to an extracorporeal circuit, oxygenated, cleared of carbon dioxide, and returned under pressure to the arterial system. This bypasses failing cardiac output and maintains systemic perfusion independent of receptor blockade or sodium/calcium-channel dysfunction. Restored flow reduces the vicious cycle in which shock causes acidosis, acidosis worsens drug binding and myocardial performance, and organ failure slows toxin clearance. VA rather than VV support is required when circulation is failing because venovenous ECMO supports gas exchange without directly generating systemic arterial flow. Mechanical support does not remove most toxins by itself and can increase left-ventricular afterload, so venting, toxin-specific therapy, and dialysis decisions remain separate.

Administration Timing

  • Activate a medical toxicologist and ECMO-capable shock team early when a potentially reversible cardiotoxic exposure shows rising lactate, escalating vasoactive needs, worsening ventricular function, recurrent dysrhythmia, or peri-arrest physiology despite optimized therapy. Referral before prolonged arrest improves the chance that cannulation occurs before irreversible injury.

  • Continue toxin-specific treatment during evaluation: high-dose insulin and dextrose for CCB/beta-blocker shock, vasopressors, calcium where appropriate, sodium bicarbonate for sodium-channel blockade, antidotes, lipid emulsion only for selected cases, ventilation, temperature and acid-base control.

  • Use multidisciplinary candidacy criteria rather than a drug name alone. Dominant cardiogenic shock, reversibility, neurologic prognosis, bleeding risk, no-flow/low-flow time, access anatomy, and center capability determine whether expected benefit exceeds harm.

Nursing Essentials

  • Trend perfusion before and during cannulation using mental status, skin, urine output, lactate clearance, arterial pressure, echocardiography, vasoactive dose, and end-organ tests because a normal circuit flow number does not guarantee adequate tissue delivery.

  • Perform frequent distal-limb pulse/Doppler, color, temperature, capillary refill, motor/sensory, and compartment checks around arterial cannulation because limb ischemia can progress rapidly and needs a distal perfusion strategy.

  • Monitor circuit pressures, flows, oxygenator function, gas exchange, anticoagulation, bleeding, hemolysis, platelet count, temperature, and cannula position, and use strict line/circuit safety because air or clot can be catastrophic.

  • Continue serial ECG, echocardiography, toxin concentrations when available, glucose/potassium during high-dose insulin, and dialysis or adsorptive therapy when separately indicated. ECMO buys time but does not end toxicokinetics.

Key Labs

  • Frequent arterial/venous blood gases, lactate, CBC/platelets, PT/INR, aPTT or center-specific anti-Xa/viscoelastic testing, fibrinogen, electrolytes, ionized calcium, glucose, renal/hepatic tests, CK, plasma-free hemoglobin/LDH and other hemolysis markers.

  • Serial echocardiography for ventricular function, filling and distension; ECG; urine output; cerebral and limb perfusion measures; toxin concentrations and acid-base markers specific to the exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, thrombosis, embolic stroke, limb ischemia, compartment syndrome, hemolysis, infection, cannula injury, air embolus, oxygenator failure, and transfusion complications can occur.

  • Retrograde arterial flow can increase left-ventricular afterload and cause distension or pulmonary edema when the ventricle does not eject; differential hypoxemia and inadequate distal perfusion require specialized correction.

Contraindications

  • Irreversible underlying disease, devastating neurologic injury, prolonged unwitnessed no-flow time, uncontrollable bleeding, inability to anticoagulate when the circuit requires it, or nonrecoverable organ failure may make burden exceed benefit; contraindications are center- and case-specific.

  • Do not equate every poisoned shock state with pump failure. Evidence is less established when profound vasoplegia without recoverable cardiac dysfunction is dominant, and VV-ECMO alone does not restore arterial circulation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Failure to achieve perfusion despite adequate circuit flow requires immediate evaluation for cannula malposition, tamponade, bleeding, vasoplegia, ventricular distension, pneumothorax, circuit failure, or an uncorrected metabolic/toxic mechanism.

  • Rebound shock during weaning can occur when the toxin remains active. Demonstrate sustained cardiac recovery and stable toxicokinetics before decannulation, then continue surveillance for bleeding, thrombosis, renal injury, and neurologic sequelae.

Evidence Limitations

  • AHA 2025 support is based on selected observational cohorts, case series, and the reversibility rationale rather than randomized trials. Benefit depends strongly on patient selection, center experience, and time to flow.

  • The evidence does not establish VA-ECMO as routine treatment for all overdoses or for primarily noncardiogenic/vasoplegic collapse. Transfer decisions require real-time toxicology and ECMO-team judgment.

Nclex Traps

  • VA-ECMO supports circulation; VV-ECMO supports gas exchange but does not replace a failing cardiac output.

  • Mechanical support is a bridge, not detoxification. Continue antidotes, high-dose insulin, vasopressors, and toxin removal as indicated.

  • Call early in a deteriorating reversible poisoning; waiting for prolonged arrest can eliminate the window for meaningful recovery.

Regulatory Status

Specialized rescue modality supported by contemporary resuscitation guidance for selected refractory toxic cardiogenic shock. It is not a drug approval or a routine indication and requires an ECMO-capable multidisciplinary team.

Boxed Warning
