Definition

Tonsillitis develops when viral or bacterial infection inflames palatine tonsils and surrounding pharyngeal tissue. The characteristic pattern is sore throat, fever, erythematous or exudative tonsils, tender nodes, odynophagia, and usually no airway compromise. Confirm or stage it with clinical scoring followed by rapid group A streptococcal testing and culture strategy appropriate to age; distinguish it from infectious mononucleosis, peritonsillar abscess, epiglottitis, diphtheria, gonococcal infection, and malignancy by testing and red flags. The priority is to provide hydration, analgesia, and antipyresis because peritonsillar abscess can complicate Tonsillitis. can follow if the process advances.

Mechanistically, viral or bacterial infection inflames palatine tonsils and surrounding pharyngeal tissue.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • clinical scoring followed by rapid group A streptococcal testing and culture strategy appropriate to age

  • viral testing when epidemiology or treatment changes

  • airway, hydration, asymmetry, trismus, rash, and systemic examination for complications

  • Distinguish Tonsillitis from infectious mononucleosis, peritonsillar abscess, epiglottitis, diphtheria, gonococcal infection, and malignancy by testing and red flags.

Labs

  • labs are often not primary unless infection/systemic disease is suspected

Treatments

  • provide hydration, analgesia, and antipyresis

  • give antibiotics only for confirmed or strongly indicated bacterial disease

  • drain abscess or secure the airway when disease extends beyond uncomplicated tonsillitis

Nursing Priorities

  • Provide fluids, soft foods, salt-water gargles when age-appropriate, and prescribed analgesic or antipyretic because reducing throat pain supports hydration and prevents avoidable emergency visits for poor intake.

  • Assess airway, voice, drooling, swallowing, hydration, fever, tonsil size and symmetry, exudate, cervical nodes, rash, cough, viral features, and age because airway obstruction, abscess, mononucleosis, viral illness, and group A streptococcal infection require different care.

  • Obtain rapid antigen testing and backup throat culture for eligible symptomatic children as ordered before antibiotics because examination alone cannot reliably distinguish group A strep when clear viral features are absent.

  • Administer the full prescribed antibiotic for confirmed bacterial infection and monitor allergy, gastrointestinal effects, fever, urine change, joint symptoms, and adherence because eradication limits transmission and reduces suppurative and rheumatic complications.

  • Escalate immediately for stridor, tripod positioning, inability to handle secretions, muffled voice with unilateral swelling or uvular deviation, neck stiffness or swelling, severe dehydration, hypotension, or toxic appearance because airway obstruction, peritonsillar or retropharyngeal abscess, or sepsis may be present.

Complications

  • peritonsillar abscess can complicate Tonsillitis.

  • dehydration can complicate Tonsillitis.

  • airway obstruction can complicate Tonsillitis.

  • rheumatic fever can complicate Tonsillitis.

  • poststreptococcal nephritis can complicate Tonsillitis.

  • invasive infection can complicate Tonsillitis.

Red Flags

  • Stridor, drooling, tripod position, cyanosis, or inability to swallow secretions

  • Muffled voice, trismus, unilateral bulge, uvular deviation, or severe one-sided pain

  • Neck swelling or stiffness, limited extension, respiratory distress, or chest pain

  • Markedly reduced urine, poor perfusion, hypotension, persistent fever, or toxic appearance

Patient Education

  • Teach hand hygiene and that confirmed strep patients should remain home until afebrile and at least twelve to twenty-four hours after appropriate antibiotics begin.

  • Explain why leftover antibiotics should not be used for a future sore throat because most episodes are viral and incomplete or mismatched treatment can cause harm.

Nclex Traps

  • Tonsillar exudate does not prove streptococcal infection

  • Drooling, muffled voice, trismus, or unilateral bulge is not uncomplicated tonsillitis
