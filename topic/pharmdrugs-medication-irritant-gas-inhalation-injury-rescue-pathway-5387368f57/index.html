Used To Treat

Eye, airway, and lung injury after chlorine, chloramine, ammonia, phosgene, mixed household cleaners, pool chemicals, industrial releases, or another primarily irritant gas.

Description

Irritant gases are reactive inhaled toxicants that damage airway epithelium and alveolar surfactant, causing immediate bronchospasm or delayed permeability edema according to their water solubility and dose. Highly water-soluble ammonia and many chloramines react quickly in the eyes and upper airway, producing burning, tearing, cough, laryngeal edema, and bronchospasm. Chlorine has intermediate solubility and can injure both upper and lower airways. Poorly water-soluble phosgene may cause little early warning while penetrating to distal lung tissue, followed hours later by noncardiogenic pulmonary edema and respiratory failure. CDC advises monitoring exposed patients during the delayed-risk period that can extend to 48 hours. Mixing bleach with ammonia generates chloramines, while mixing bleach with acid can release chlorine. Rescue begins with responder safety and fresh air, not entry into a contaminated room. Remove contaminated clothing and irrigate eyes or skin, then support airway, oxygenation, bronchospasm, and ventilation. There is no general antidote, and prophylactic antibiotics or corticosteroids are not routine substitutes for serial respiratory assessment.

Mechanism

When these gases dissolve in airway surface water, they generate acids, bases, oxidants, or reactive intermediates that denature proteins, disrupt epithelial membranes, trigger inflammation, and increase capillary permeability. Solubility determines where injury is concentrated: rapid dissolution produces intense upper-airway warning, while lower-solubility phosgene reaches bronchioles and alveoli before symptoms become obvious. Epithelial damage causes bronchospasm and sloughing; endothelial and alveolar injury leak protein-rich fluid, reduce lung compliance, and impair gas exchange. Fresh air stops additional dose, irrigation removes residual surface chemical, bronchodilators reduce reversible smooth-muscle constriction, and lung-protective ventilation supports oxygenation while the barrier heals. Because inflammation and permeability can worsen after exposure ends, an early normal pulse oximeter or chest image cannot always rule out later acute lung injury.

Administration Timing

  • Do not enter a contaminated space or create a second casualty. Call 911 or hazmat, follow evacuation or shelter instructions, move to fresh air only when safe, and never mix bleach with ammonia, acids, or other cleaners.

  • Remove contaminated clothing, bag it safely, irrigate painful eyes and exposed skin with lukewarm water, and assess voice, stridor, wheeze, oxygenation, work of breathing, and mental status. Give oxygen and inhaled bronchodilator for hypoxemia or bronchospasm and secure a progressively edematous airway early with expert help.

  • Observe according to agent, dose, symptoms, lung disease, and course. Phosgene can produce delayed pulmonary edema up to 48 hours; worsening cough, dyspnea, pink or frothy sputum, hypoxemia, or hypotension requires immediate ICU and lung-protective respiratory support.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify every product mixed, concentration, enclosed-space duration, industrial agent, cloud behavior, PPE, decontamination already completed, exertion after exposure, co-victims, and baseline asthma or lung disease. Bring labels or safety data without contaminated containers entering clean care areas.

  • Trend voice, secretions, stridor, wheeze, respiratory rate, oxygen need, end-tidal carbon dioxide when useful, auscultation, peak flow in stable cooperative patients, mental status, temperature, and fluid balance. Minimize unnecessary exertion after phosgene exposure.

  • Keep staff in appropriate PPE until decontamination is confirmed, ventilate treatment areas, and coordinate occupational or public-health reporting. Provide explicit return precautions for delayed cough, dyspnea, chest tightness, fever, or exercise intolerance.

Key Labs

  • Pulse oximetry, serial respiratory examination, blood gas for significant distress, chest imaging when symptoms or high-risk exposure warrant it, ECG and lactate in severe illness, and agent-specific studies for mixed exposure. Initial chest imaging can be normal before edema develops.

  • No routine blood chlorine, ammonia, chloramine, or phosgene concentration guides bedside treatment. Exposure history, latency, airway findings, oxygen trend, imaging, and physiology determine severity.

Adverse Effects

  • Complications include corneal injury, laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, chemical pneumonitis, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, ARDS, secondary infection, reactive-airways dysfunction syndrome, chronic bronchitis, hypoxic injury, and death.

  • Excessive fluid can worsen lung edema, delayed intubation can make an edematous airway impossible, and indiscriminate antibiotics or corticosteroids add adverse effects without proven routine prophylactic benefit.

Contraindications

  • Do not enter the source without trained respiratory protection, and do not attempt household neutralization or create another chemical mixture. Do not make an exposed patient vomit.

  • Do not discharge a significant phosgene exposure solely because early symptoms, oxygen saturation, or radiograph are mild. Do not use routine prophylactic antibiotics or corticosteroids without a defined complication or specialist rationale.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Stridor, increasing oxygen need, bronchospasm unresponsive to initial treatment, pink frothy sputum, pulmonary edema, fatigue, hypercapnia, hypotension, altered consciousness, or delayed decline requires ICU, toxicology or poison-center, pulmonology, and early advanced-airway support.

Interactions

  • Mixing bleach with ammonia produces chloramines; mixing bleach with acids releases chlorine. Pool chemicals and industrial releases may combine oxidants or acids, so exact product identification matters.

  • Asthma, smoking, COPD, recent respiratory infection, and exertion after phosgene exposure can magnify symptoms. Cyanide, carbon monoxide, or thermal injury in a fire requires separate rescue pathways.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Smaller airways, higher minute ventilation per kilogram, and inability to leave a source independently increase edema and respiratory-failure risk.

  • Population

    Asthma or chronic lung disease

    Note

    Baseline airway hyperreactivity and reduced reserve can turn a modest irritant exposure into severe bronchospasm or prolonged symptoms.

  • Population

    Workers and responders

    Note

    Confined-space or industrial releases require hazmat control, respiratory protection, decontamination, occupational documentation, and co-worker evaluation.

Evidence Limitations

  • Treatment is largely supportive and evidence for corticosteroids or other adjuncts is inconsistent. Agent solubility and CDC latency guidance help risk-stratify, but observation and admission decisions remain exposure- and physiology-specific.

Nclex Traps

  • Never mix bleach with ammonia or acid because chloramine or chlorine gas can form.

  • Phosgene may cause little early irritation yet produce delayed pulmonary edema and respiratory failure.

  • No general irritant-gas antidote replaces fresh air, decontamination, airway support, bronchodilation, and observation.

Regulatory Status

No universal FDA-approved antidote exists. Oxygen, bronchodilators, airway care, ventilation, irrigation, corticosteroids, and antibiotics are selected by clinical findings and local chemical-emergency protocols.

Boxed Warning

IRRITANT-GAS WARNING: do not enter a contaminated area or mix cleaners; call 911 or hazmat and move to fresh air only when safe. Hoarseness, stridor, wheeze, hypoxemia, or worsening cough needs emergency care. Continue phosgene surveillance for up to 48 hours when specialists advise because alveolar-capillary injury can worsen after a symptom-light latency and produce delayed pulmonary edema.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
