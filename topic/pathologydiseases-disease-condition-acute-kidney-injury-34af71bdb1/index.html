Pathology

Reduced functioning filtration lowers clearance of water, electrolytes, acids, wastes, and medications. The structural lesion may be absent early in hemodynamic AKI, centered in tubules in acute tubular injury, inflammatory in glomerular or interstitial disease, vascular, or caused by pressure upstream from obstruction.

Etiology

Prerenal AKI results from inadequate effective renal perfusion; intrinsic AKI results from tubular, interstitial, glomerular, or vascular injury; and postrenal AKI results from obstructed urine flow. Sepsis, surgery, shock, decompensated heart or liver disease, nephrotoxins, urinary obstruction, and interacting hemodynamic medicines commonly overlap.

Signs Symptoms

  • Oliguria, anuria, or a nonoliguric creatinine rise

  • Edema, hypertension, rapid weight gain, pulmonary crackles, or dyspnea

  • Weakness, paresthesias, dysrhythmia, nausea, pruritus, confusion, or asterixis as complications accumulate

  • Symptoms of the cause such as vomiting, bleeding, infection, rash, flank pain, retention, or systemic inflammation

Diagnostics

  • Establish baseline and trend creatinine and urine output against KDIGO timing criteria

  • Check catheter patency, bladder retention, urinalysis, microscopy, electrolytes, acid-base status, and medication exposures

  • Use renal ultrasound when obstruction is possible and targeted serology or biopsy when glomerular, interstitial, or vascular disease is suspected

  • Assess perfusion and congestion because both low inflow and high venous pressure can lower GFR

Treatments

  • Reverse shock, infection, volume loss, congestion, obstruction, or inflammatory disease driving the injury

  • Stop or adjust nephrotoxins and renally cleared medicines while preserving necessary therapies when benefits outweigh risks

  • Manage potassium, acid-base, oxygenation, nutrition, glucose, and fluid balance

  • Use kidney replacement therapy for refractory dangerous complications or selected dialyzable toxins based on the whole clinical context, not a creatinine number alone

Nursing Priorities

  • Compare current creatinine and urine output with baseline, review timing of hypotension, sepsis, surgery, contrast, obstruction, fluid loss, and every medicine, and assess bladder retention because AKI is a syndrome whose cause determines whether fluid, drainage, or restraint is safest.

  • Measure strict intake and output, daily weight, pressure, perfusion, edema, lung sounds, mental status, and trend creatinine, urea, potassium, bicarbonate, sodium, phosphate, magnesium, urinalysis, and electrocardiography when indicated because life-threatening complications may develop before creatinine peaks.

  • Optimize individualized perfusion and fluid status, obtain cultures or imaging, relieve obstruction, and treat sepsis promptly while reassessing after each intervention because both persistent underfilling and indiscriminate fluid loading can extend kidney injury.

  • Stop or clarify nephrotoxins, adjust renally cleared medicines, verify contrast necessity, and avoid potassium or magnesium accumulation because reduced filtration changes drug exposure and electrolyte elimination immediately.

  • Escalate for anuria, refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremic pericarditis or encephalopathy, seizure, rapidly rising toxin level, or worsening shock because urgent nephrology and kidney-replacement therapy may be needed.

Complications

  • Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary edema, and dysrhythmia

  • Uremic encephalopathy, pericarditis, platelet dysfunction, or severe nausea and catabolism

  • Medication accumulation and toxicity

  • CKD, kidney failure, recurrent AKI, cardiovascular events, and death

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to avoid NSAIDs and to obtain a medication review after AKI because doses that were safe before injury may accumulate during recovery.

  • Explain the need for follow-up creatinine, urine protein, pressure, and medication review even after discharge because AKI raises later chronic kidney disease risk.

Nclex Traps

  • A patient can have nonoliguric AKI

  • Check the catheter before assuming anuria

  • Creatinine is a delayed functional marker, not a direct injury measurement

  • Dialysis decisions depend on refractory complications and clinical trajectory, not one eGFR cutoff

Definition

Acute kidney injury is an abrupt reduction in kidney function recognized by a rise in serum creatinine, a fall in urine output, or both. It is a syndrome with prerenal, intrinsic, and postrenal causes, not a single disease, and it can be dangerous even when the patient continues to make urine.

Labs

  • Creatinine rises after filtration falls and may not be at steady state

  • Potassium and phosphorus may rise while bicarbonate and calcium may fall

  • BUN is influenced by perfusion, catabolism, protein, steroids, and GI bleeding

  • Urine sediment, protein, blood, sodium indices, CK, hemolysis markers, and serologies are selected according to the suspected cause

Nursing Interventions

  • Strict intake/output

  • Daily weights

  • Monitor potassium and ECG changes

  • Review medications for renal dosing

  • Assess volume status

Red Flags

  • Anuria or rapidly falling urine output despite correction of reversible causes

  • Hyperkalemia with electrocardiographic change or refractory severe metabolic acidosis

  • Pulmonary edema, severe hypoxemia, or fluid overload unresponsive to medical therapy

  • Uremic pericarditis, encephalopathy, seizure, severe bleeding, or dialyzable toxin exposure

Nclex Pearls

  • Check catheter obstruction before assuming kidneys stopped making urine.

  • Low urine output is an early bedside clue.

Pathophysiology

KDIGO recognizes AKI by a creatinine increase of at least 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, at least 1.5 times a known or presumed baseline within 7 days, or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6 hours. Creatinine lags behind the actual filtration change and is diluted by fluid accumulation, while oliguria may appear earlier but can reflect catheter obstruction or hemodynamics rather than parenchymal death. Falling filtration retains potassium, acids, sodium, water, uremic solutes, and renally cleared drugs; severe or prolonged injury can also create maladaptive repair, fibrosis, CKD, and higher long-term cardiovascular risk.

Risk Factors

  • Older age, CKD, diabetes, heart failure, cirrhosis, or limited renal reserve

  • Sepsis, shock, major surgery, burns, hemorrhage, or severe volume loss

  • Nephrotoxins, contrast in susceptible settings, NSAIDs, RAAS-active drugs during low perfusion, or multiple interacting agents

  • Urinary retention, stones, pelvic or retroperitoneal disease, prostate disease, or a solitary functioning kidney

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose or exclude AKI from urine output alone

  • Do not automatically give fluid until obstruction, congestion, cardiac function, and effective perfusion are assessed

  • Do not delay emergency treatment while waiting for the creatinine to peak

Causal Links

  • Abrupt filtration loss -> retained potassium and acids -> dysrhythmia and hemodynamic risk

  • Reduced sodium and water clearance -> congestion -> pulmonary edema and further renal venous pressure

  • Tubular or glomerular injury -> maladaptive repair -> fibrosis -> higher CKD risk

  • Falling drug clearance -> rising exposure -> neurologic, cardiac, bleeding, or other toxicity
