Definition

Burkitt lymphoma is an exceptionally fast-growing mature germinal-center B-cell lymphoma, usually driven by an immunoglobulin-MYC rearrangement. Extensive marrow and blood involvement has historically been called Burkitt leukemia, but it is the same biologic disease and not a separate leukemia card.

Pathology

Tumor tissue contains a nearly uniform population of medium-sized B cells with an extremely high proliferation fraction. Tingible-body macrophages between dying tumor cells can create a starry-sky pattern, but that appearance alone is not diagnostic; immunophenotype and MYC-directed genetic testing are required.

Pathophysiology

  • MYC dysregulation accelerates cell growth, metabolism, and division, so tumor volume can increase over days and cellular turnover drives very high LDH and uric-acid burden.

  • When large numbers of cells lyse spontaneously or after therapy, potassium and phosphate rise, calcium falls, and uric acid or calcium-phosphate can injure the kidneys. This makes tumor-lysis prevention an immediate pre-treatment priority.

Etiology

Endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated epidemiologic patterns are recognized. Epstein-Barr virus is strongly associated with endemic disease and a subset of other cases, HIV and other immune deficiency increase risk, and chronic malaria exposure contributes to endemic risk. None of these associations replaces tissue diagnosis.

Risk Factors

  • Residence in or exposure to regions where endemic Burkitt lymphoma and malaria are common

  • HIV or another significant immunodeficiency

  • Epstein-Barr virus association, particularly in endemic disease

  • Childhood or young adulthood, although adults and older adults can be affected

Signs Symptoms

  • Mass that enlarges over days to weeks

  • Abdominal or ileocecal mass with pain, distention, obstruction, intussusception, bleeding, ascites, or organ enlargement in many sporadic cases

  • Jaw or facial-bone mass in many endemic presentations

  • Fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, or very high tumor burden

  • Marrow cytopenias, CNS symptoms, testicular swelling, bone pain, pleural or peritoneal disease, or other extranodal involvement

Diagnostics

  • Obtain urgent adequate tissue for morphology, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and MYC FISH or other cytogenetic testing; treatment should not be based on starry-sky morphology alone.

  • Typical findings include mature B-cell markers, germinal-center markers such as CD10 and BCL6, a proliferation fraction approaching the whole tumor, and a MYC rearrangement, interpreted in the current lymphoma classification.

  • Stage quickly with protocol-directed PET-CT or other imaging, marrow assessment, and cerebrospinal-fluid evaluation because CNS-directed treatment is a routine part of Burkitt protocols.

  • Coordinate pathology and treatment planning immediately; the diagnostic process must be accurate but should not become an avoidable delay in a tumor with an extremely short doubling time.

Labs

  • Obtain CBC, LDH, uric acid, potassium, phosphate, calcium, creatinine, glucose, liver tests, and urine output before therapy and repeat at the protocol-defined high-risk frequency.

  • Screen for HIV, hepatitis B and C, pregnancy, and other treatment-relevant conditions.

  • Cerebrospinal fluid, marrow, and imaging results determine stage and CNS strategy; a normal peripheral blood count does not exclude extensive extranodal disease.

  • Metabolic abnormalities can precede chemotherapy because spontaneous tumor lysis occurs.

Assessment

Treat suspected Burkitt lymphoma as a time-sensitive oncology problem. Assess airway and mass effect, abdominal pain and obstruction, neurologic function, renal perfusion and urine output, cardiac rhythm, hydration, weight, CBC, LDH, and tumor-lysis markers. Establish reliable access and document baseline function before intensive multiagent therapy.

Differential

Differentiate Burkitt lymphoma from DLBCL, MYC and BCL2-rearranged lymphoma, high-grade B-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration, B-lymphoblastic lymphoma, blastoid mantle-cell lymphoma, and plasmablastic lymphoma. These are not interchangeable labels or protocols.

Treatments

  • Begin specialist-directed, curative-intent intensive multidrug immunochemotherapy promptly after adequate classification and stabilization. Regimens vary by age, risk, organ function, and institution and commonly include rituximab and CNS-directed therapy.

  • Use a formal tumor-lysis prevention plan before cytotoxic treatment because spontaneous lysis and the first treatment can release a dangerous metabolic load; the plan includes prescribed hydration, urate-lowering therapy, frequent laboratory testing, and level-appropriate cardiac and renal monitoring.

  • Treat established metabolic emergencies and organ failure immediately; selected high-risk patients may need intensive-care or dialysis support.

  • Surgery is mainly for diagnosis or an acute complication such as perforation or obstruction, not primary disease control.

Contraindications

  • Do not delay tumor-lysis prevention while waiting for every staging detail when the diagnosis and burden make lysis imminent.

  • Rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because severe hemolysis and methemoglobinemia can occur; follow the current product label and protocol.

  • High-dose methotrexate and cytarabine require protocol-specific renal, hepatic, hydration, interaction, and neurologic safeguards and cannot be administered as generic routine chemotherapy.

  • Do not transfer an ordinary DLBCL regimen to Burkitt lymphoma without disease-specific oncology direction.

  • Lumbar puncture or intrathecal therapy requires platelet, coagulation, infection, pressure, and neurologic safety assessment.

Safety Considerations

  • Tumor lysis may be spontaneous, so a patient can arrive with kidney injury, arrhythmia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, or seizure before treatment.

  • Intensive therapy produces profound cytopenia, mucositis, infection risk, and medication-specific organ toxicity that require protocolized supportive care.

  • Abdominal disease can obstruct, bleed, or perforate; escalating pain or guarding is not assumed to be routine chemotherapy discomfort.

  • CNS symptoms require urgent evaluation because CNS involvement changes therapy.

  • Fertility, pregnancy, HIV treatment, and drug interactions should be addressed without compromising urgent lymphoma care.

Nursing Priorities

  • Before therapy, verify pathology and regimen, secure reliable access, obtain baseline weight, ECG or monitoring level, CBC, metabolic and organ tests, urine output, G6PD result when rasburicase is considered, and the exact tumor-lysis plan.

  • Maintain prescribed hydration and strict intake and output; trend potassium, phosphate, calcium, uric acid, creatinine, LDH, rhythm, neurologic status, and fluid balance at the ordered high-risk interval.

  • Assess abdominal distention, bowel sounds, stool or emesis blood, pain, and guarding to detect obstruction, bleeding, or perforation before shock develops.

  • Use neutropenic-fever, mucositis, nutrition, transfusion, and infection-prevention protocols throughout intensive treatment.

  • Teach the patient and family why therapy begins quickly, why CNS treatment may be given without neurologic symptoms, and which metabolic or infection symptoms require immediate reporting.

Red Flags

  • Oliguria, rising creatinine, arrhythmia, weakness, tetany, seizure, confusion, or rapid tumor-lysis laboratory change

  • Severe abdominal pain, guarding, distention, vomiting, absent stool or gas, or GI bleeding

  • Rapidly expanding head, neck, or mediastinal mass with airway or venous compromise

  • New focal deficit, headache with vomiting, seizure, or altered consciousness

  • Fever, hypotension, or sepsis signs during intensive therapy

Complications

  • Spontaneous or treatment-triggered tumor lysis syndrome, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, and seizure

  • Bowel obstruction, intussusception, hemorrhage, ischemia, or perforation

  • CNS and marrow involvement

  • Severe cytopenia, mucositis, infection, infertility, and treatment-specific organ toxicity

  • Relapsed or refractory disease

Prognosis

Many children and fit younger adults can be cured with intensive risk-adapted treatment. Prognosis is less favorable with advanced age, very high tumor burden or LDH, CNS disease, organ dysfunction, inability to deliver planned therapy, or relapse. The speed of disease makes treatment urgency and supportive care inseparable.

Prevention

There is no general screening test or vaccine. HIV prevention and treatment reduce one risk context, and malaria-control and EBV epidemiology matter in endemic regions, but they do not eliminate disease. Prevention within treatment focuses on tumor lysis, infection, infertility, and organ toxicity.

Patient Education

  • Burkitt lymphoma grows very quickly but is often highly treatment responsive, so urgent treatment can still be curative.

  • Laboratory checks and hydration begin before chemotherapy because tumor cells can break down on their own.

  • Report reduced urine, muscle cramps or weakness, palpitations, confusion, fever, severe mouth pain, or worsening abdominal symptoms immediately.

  • Do not add medicines or supplements without the oncology team because high-dose therapy has important renal and interaction requirements.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents usually follow pediatric mature B-cell protocols with age-specific CNS and supportive care.

  • Older or frail adults need expert regimen selection; simply reducing treatment without a disease-specific plan can sacrifice cure while retaining toxicity.

  • HIV-positive patients need coordinated antiretroviral, interaction, infection, and lymphoma management.

  • Pregnancy and significant renal, hepatic, or cardiac impairment require immediate multidisciplinary protocol adaptation.

Nclex Traps

  • A starry-sky pattern suggests but does not prove Burkitt lymphoma.

  • Tumor-lysis prevention starts before chemotherapy because lysis may already be occurring.

  • Burkitt leukemia describes blood or marrow extent of the same disease, not a separate leukemia treatment pathway.

  • CNS-directed therapy is routine in Burkitt protocols even when the initial neurologic examination is normal.
