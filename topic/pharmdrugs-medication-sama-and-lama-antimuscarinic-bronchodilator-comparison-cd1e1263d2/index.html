Used To Treat

Vagal bronchomotor tone in COPD and selected asthma regimens. Ipratropium is a short-acting maintenance bronchodilator in COPD and an adjunct to a SABA in moderate or severe acute asthma. Tiotropium, umeclidinium, and aclidinium are long-acting maintenance bronchodilators; they do not relieve an acute attack.

Description

Parasympathetic acetylcholine continuously narrows airways and stimulates secretion through muscarinic receptors, especially M3. Inhaled antimuscarinics block that signal without directly stimulating the heart. Ipratropium wears off sooner and is useful beside albuterol during a significant asthma exacerbation because the two drugs block different constrictor pathways. LAMAs remain bound at airway M3 receptors longer, providing maintenance bronchodilation and reducing COPD symptoms or exacerbations. Their quaternary ammonium structure limits blood-brain penetration, but local eye and urinary exposure can still be clinically dangerous.

Mechanism

Acetylcholine released from vagal nerves binds M3 receptors on airway smooth muscle. M3 couples to Gq, activates phospholipase C, generates IP3, releases intracellular calcium, and activates myosin light-chain kinase, producing bronchoconstriction. Competitive M3 antagonism interrupts this cascade, so calcium-dependent contraction and glandular secretion fall. Ipratropium dissociates relatively quickly. Tiotropium dissociates slowly from M1 and M3 but faster from M2, while umeclidinium and aclidinium also provide prolonged M3 blockade. Aclidinium is rapidly hydrolyzed in plasma, which helps limit systemic exposure. None suppresses eosinophilic inflammation, and none should be mistaken for an inhaled corticosteroid.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: all can cause paradoxical bronchospasm or immediate hypersensitivity. Aerosol or powder in the eye can precipitate acute narrow-angle glaucoma; antimuscarinic effects can worsen urinary retention in bladder-neck obstruction or prostatic disease. LAMA products are maintenance medicines, so using them during acute respiratory distress delays effective rescue treatment.

Adverse Effects

  • Dry mouth, cough, bitter taste, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention, tachycardia, and paradoxical bronchospasm

  • Acute eye pain, halos, red eye, blurred vision, nausea, or headache when ocular exposure precipitates angle closure

Contraindications

  • Product or atropine-derivative hypersensitivity; severe milk-protein allergy for lactose-containing dry powders

  • Use caution with narrow-angle glaucoma, prostatic hyperplasia, bladder-neck obstruction, and additive anticholinergic burden

Nursing Essentials

  • Keep mist out of the eyes, verify device loading and inspiratory technique, and never swallow an inhalation capsule.

  • Assess urinary output, eye symptoms, dry mouth, secretion thickness, breath sounds, symptoms, and rescue-inhaler availability.

Interactions

  • Other anticholinergics add dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, confusion, and glaucoma risk.

  • Combination with beta agonists is often intentional because mechanisms differ; duplicate products within combination inhalers must still be reconciled.

Key Labs

  • Symptoms, exacerbations, spirometry, inhaler technique, eye symptoms, and bladder emptying

  • Renal function when using renally cleared LAMAs in a patient with substantial impairment and systemic anticholinergic symptoms

Nclex Traps

  • Ipratropium supports albuterol in a significant acute asthma exacerbation but is slower and is not the sole rescue drug.

  • A HandiHaler capsule is pierced and inhaled; swallowing it does not deliver tiotropium to the lungs.
