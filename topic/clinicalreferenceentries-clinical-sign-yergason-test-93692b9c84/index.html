Definition

Yergason test is a bedside resisted-shoulder maneuver. It is a clue to long-head biceps tendon instability or pain. A positive finding means bicipital-groove pain or tendon snapping occurs.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Yergason test means bicipital-groove pain or tendon snapping occurs.

Why It Matters

A positive Yergason test response can signal long-head biceps tendon instability or pain. Contraction tensions the biceps tendon within the groove and stresses its stabilizing pulley. Yergason test cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • long-head biceps tendon instability or pain

Assessment Method

With elbow flexed, resist forearm supination and external rotation.

Positive Finding

Bicipital-groove pain or tendon snapping occurs.

Mechanism

Contraction tensions the biceps tendon within the groove and stresses its stabilizing pulley.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Yergason test response raises concern for long-head biceps tendon instability or pain because contraction tensions the biceps tendon within the groove and stresses its stabilizing pulley. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Yergason test does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Yergason test to focus evaluation of long-head biceps tendon instability or pain and the reviewed alternatives: subscapularis injury, elbow pain, radial tunnel syndrome. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: With elbow flexed, resist forearm supination and external rotation. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Yergason test as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Low sensitivity and multiple pain generators limit use. Therefore, Yergason test cannot confirm or exclude long-head biceps tendon instability or pain by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • subscapularis injury

  • elbow pain

  • radial tunnel syndrome

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from subscapularis injury, elbow pain, radial tunnel syndrome, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Yergason test may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Limited adjunct. When performed as described, Yergason test can adjust concern for long-head biceps tendon instability or pain, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis. Apply the reviewed limitation for Yergason test: Low sensitivity and multiple pain generators limit use.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Yergason test, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: With elbow flexed, resist forearm supination and external rotation. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Palpate gently and do not repeatedly provoke tendon subluxation.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact Yergason test observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: Bicipital-groove pain or tendon snapping occurs. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: With elbow flexed, resist forearm supination and external rotation. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, long-head biceps tendon instability or pain, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

Base escalation for Yergason test on the patient's current condition, associated findings, and the cause under evaluation; this card does not assign a universal urgency level.

Common Pitfalls
