Used To Treat

Acute uncomplicated malaria caused by susceptible Plasmodium species in patients at least 5 kg, and the preferred oral follow-on regimen after IV artesunate when clinically appropriate. It is not malaria prophylaxis and is not adequate initial monotherapy for severe malaria.

Mechanism

Artemether is demethylated to dihydroartemisinin. Ferrous heme generated during hemoglobin digestion cleaves the endoperoxide bridge and creates reactive carbon radicals that alkylate parasite proteins and membranes. Lumefantrine is lipophilic and concentrates in the acidic digestive vacuole, where it binds heme and prevents its polymerization into hemozoin. Free ferriprotoporphyrin IX catalyzes oxidative membrane injury, so residual parasites die after the rapid artemisinin phase. The six-dose schedule maintains partner exposure through several parasite cycles. Dietary fat increases bile-mediated solubilization and absorption of lumefantrine; fasting, malabsorption, or repeated vomiting lowers exposure and can permit treatment failure or select resistant parasites.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity and concurrent strong CYP3A4 inducers; avoid in high-risk QT conditions or with certain recent antimalarial exposures.

  • Do not use as initial therapy for severe malaria, where IV artesunate is required.

Nursing Essentials

  • Teach the exact six-dose schedule: initial dose, second dose eight hours later, then twice daily on days two and three. A simple 'three-day course' can hide a dangerous missed second dose.

  • Give every dose with food or a milky drink. If the patient cannot eat or repeatedly vomits, reassess absorption and consider another regimen rather than assuming tablets were absorbed.

Interactions

  • Strong CYP3A4 inducers such as rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, or St. John's wort can reduce exposure; strong inhibitors can increase lumefantrine and QT risk.

  • Other QT-prolonging drugs and recent halofantrine or quinine exposure require careful review.

Key Labs

  • Parasite density and follow-up smear when indicated, glucose, CBC, liver and renal function, potassium and magnesium, and ECG in cardiac/QT risk

  • Dose timing, food intake, vomiting, symptom improvement, recrudescence, pregnancy status, and whether radical cure is still needed for P. vivax or P. ovale

Nclex Traps

  • The six-dose schedule is not simply one dose twice daily for three days; day one includes a second dose eight hours after the first.

  • Food is part of effective dosing because fat markedly improves lumefantrine absorption.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: QT prolongation can occur, and exposure increases with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or hepatic impairment. Avoid in congenital long-QT syndrome, symptomatic arrhythmia, clinically important bradycardia, severe cardiac disease, uncorrected hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia, or with high-risk QT-prolonging antimalarials. Strong CYP3A4 inducers can reduce concentrations and cause failure. Re-dose according to label if vomiting occurs soon after administration.

Description

Artemether-lumefantrine is a fixed-dose artemisinin-combination antimalarial for uncomplicated blood-stage malaria: artemether becomes dihydroartemisinin, whose iron-activated endoperoxide rapidly damages parasite proteins and membranes, while lumefantrine blocks conversion of toxic free heme into inert hemozoin and clears residual organisms. This fast-plus-persistent pairing reduces recrudescence and slows resistance selection compared with either component alone. Lumefantrine absorption is strongly enhanced by fat, which is why each dose should be taken with food or a milky drink and vomiting or inability to eat can make an apparently completed course ineffective.

Adverse Effects

  • Headache, dizziness, anorexia, weakness, fever, cough, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, arthralgia, and myalgia

  • QT prolongation, hypersensitivity, liver-test elevation, neutropenia, anemia, and recrudescence from poor absorption or incomplete dosing
