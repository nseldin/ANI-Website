Plain Language

Factitious disorder imposed on another means a person intentionally falsifies or causes illness or injury in someone else and presents that person as sick. The psychiatric diagnosis belongs to the person causing the deception; the victim may also have genuine illness and needs protection from induced harm and unnecessary care.

Why It Matters

The victim can be poisoned, infected, injured, starved, suffocated, subjected to unnecessary tests or surgery, or denied appropriate care, and the behavior can be fatal. Nurses must stabilize the patient, document facts and information sources, recognize that real disease can coexist, and activate the required multidisciplinary safeguarding process without waiting for a psychiatric diagnosis of the suspected perpetrator.

Definition

Factitious disorder imposed on another is a mental disorder diagnosed in a person who intentionally falsifies physical or psychological signs or symptoms, induces illness or injury in someone else, and presents that individual to others as ill, impaired, or injured, without the behavior being explained solely by obvious external rewards. The person receiving the harmful care is the victim, not the holder of the perpetrator's psychiatric diagnosis. Caregiver-fabricated illness, fabricated or induced illness, and medical child abuse are victim-centered safeguarding frameworks that may be used without first proving the perpetrator meets a psychiatric diagnosis.

Pathology

The perpetrator's deception can distort the history, produce or simulate findings, interfere with treatment, or expose the victim to unnecessary medicines, tests, hospitalization, devices, and procedures. Genuine disease can coexist and may be worsened, concealed, or exploited. The pattern is established from multiple objective facts, source-attributed documentation, and longitudinal record review—not from one discrepancy, caregiver behavior, or unexplained illness. Immediate protection and mandated reporting follow the victim's risk and applicable law; they do not wait for a confession, proven motive, or completed psychiatric evaluation of the suspected perpetrator.

Pathophysiology

  • There is no single established biologic mechanism. The psychiatric formulation concerns intentional deceptive behavior by the perpetrator; the victim's pathology consists of actual induced injury, missed care, and iatrogenic exposure.

  • Illness behavior, relationship dynamics, prior adversity, identity, emotional needs, healthcare experience, and coexisting mental disorders may contribute, but clinicians cannot infer a complete motive from the medical pattern alone.

  • Repeated healthcare contact can amplify harm when falsified information leads to invasive care, while separation from the suspected source may change observed symptoms; neither pattern is diagnostic by itself.

Etiology

Etiology of the perpetrator's disorder is uncertain and multifactorial; no caregiver demographic, profession, relationship, trauma history, or psychiatric diagnosis proves FDIA. Victims may be children, dependent adults, older adults, disabled people, partners, or others, and the suspected perpetrator is not necessarily a mother. Safeguarding assessment focuses first on evidence of falsification or induction, actual or likely harm, and the victim's protection rather than speculative motive.

Risk Factors

  • A dependent child or adult with limited ability to describe events, communicate privately, access care independently, or resist a caregiver

  • Repeated unexplained illness, invasive procedures, multiple facilities, fragmented records, or discrepancies between caregiver reports and independent observation; these are review triggers, not proof

  • Symptoms reported only by one person, unexpected recurrence near a caregiver, unexplained specimen or device findings, or improvement in a protected setting; each requires careful multidisciplinary interpretation

  • Genuine chronic illness, disability, rare disease, prematurity, or complex technology dependence, which can coexist and increase both vulnerability and risk of misclassification

Signs Symptoms

  • The victim may have actual injury or illness from poisoning, suffocation, contamination, underfeeding, medication interference, wound or device manipulation, or another induced mechanism

  • Reports, symptoms, observations, laboratory findings, and prior records may conflict in a repeated pattern that cannot be explained by ordinary variation or fragmented care

  • The victim may undergo frequent admissions, procedures, medicines, devices, or specialty evaluations with escalating iatrogenic harm

  • Symptoms may appear, worsen, or be reported mainly in the suspected perpetrator's presence and change under independently observed care; this pattern alone is not conclusive

  • A genuine illness may be present at the same time, and the victim may show fear, developmental regression, feeding problems, trauma symptoms, attachment disruption, or reluctance to speak privately

Diagnostics

  • Stabilize and evaluate the victim's current illness or injury. Establish what was directly observed, what each person reported, what original records show, and whether there is objective evidence of falsification, induction, treatment interference, or unnecessary exposure.

  • Create an integrated chronology across facilities with dates, reported symptoms, witnessed findings, tests, medicines, procedures, caregivers present, clinical responses, missed appointments, and outcomes. Avoid copy-forward statements that convert suspicion into fact.

  • Use a multidisciplinary safeguarding team that may include the treating service, nursing, child-abuse pediatrics or adult safeguarding expertise, social work, psychiatry, pharmacy, laboratory, ethics, risk management, and legal or protective agencies.

  • Keep victim-centered and perpetrator-centered conclusions separate. Evidence may support suspected fabricated or induced illness and protective action even when the suspected perpetrator has not been interviewed, confessed, or diagnosed with FDIA.

Labs

  • No laboratory test, scan, record discrepancy, surveillance finding, or psychiatric scale independently diagnoses FDIA or proves who caused an abnormality.

  • Use targeted testing to identify and treat the victim's injury, infection, poisoning, malnutrition, bleeding, medication exposure, device complication, or genuine disease; avoid unnecessary repeat procedures.

  • Coordinate specimen collection, medication administration, device handling, and interpretation with the laboratory, pharmacy, and safeguarding team when clinically justified, following law and policy and preserving objective chain-of-information documentation.

Assessment

First assess the victim's airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, glucose when indicated, nutrition, hydration, pain, injuries, infection, bleeding, medication or toxin exposure, device integrity, development or cognition, and immediate danger. Interview the patient separately when developmentally and clinically appropriate and safe; compare each report with direct observation and the longitudinal record. Determine whether siblings or other dependents may be at risk, activate the local safeguarding pathway, and avoid confronting the suspected perpetrator alone before a coordinated protection plan is in place.

Differential

  • Genuine, rare, evolving, intermittent, or multiple medical conditions; treatment complications; medication errors; fragmented care; communication barriers; or caregiver misunderstanding

  • Caregiver anxiety, illness anxiety, somatic symptom concerns, delusional belief, obsessive behavior, poor health literacy, or overinterpretation without intentional falsification

  • Medical neglect, physical abuse, poisoning, coercive control, exploitation, or other maltreatment that may require protection even when FDIA criteria are not established

  • Malingering by proxy for a clear external incentive, while recognizing that mixed motives may exist and external benefit alone does not settle intent

  • Factitious disorder imposed on self in the patient, functional neurological symptom disorder, somatic symptom disorder, or another genuine mental disorder in the victim

Treatments

  • Protect and treat the victim first: stop active exposure when possible, treat actual injury or illness, reduce unnecessary tests and medicines, restore nutrition and function, and provide trauma-informed care.

  • Activate child-protection or adult-safeguarding procedures and make required reports based on reasonable concern and local law; do not wait for proof beyond the reporting threshold or a perpetrator psychiatric diagnosis.

  • Use one multidisciplinary plan for observation, communication, records, medicines, specimens, devices, visitation or separation decisions, and safe discharge, with legal and ethical oversight.

  • Provide the victim with medical, developmental, psychological, rehabilitation, educational, and family support according to identified needs and reassess genuine disease throughout recovery.

  • No medication treats the core deceptive behavior of the perpetrator. Any perpetrator psychotherapy or treatment of a separate condition occurs apart from the victim's immediate protection and has limited evidence; reunification is never based on treatment attendance alone.

Contraindications

  • Do not assign the perpetrator's FDIA diagnosis to the victim, and do not require that diagnosis before recognizing suspected fabricated or induced illness or reporting maltreatment.

  • Do not confront the suspected perpetrator alone, reveal the protection plan prematurely, or create an unsafe discharge before the safeguarding team evaluates immediate risk.

  • Do not dismiss all victim symptoms as fabricated; genuine illness and induced injury can coexist and still require evidence-based treatment.

  • Do not diagnose from one discrepancy, one caregiver characteristic, frequent healthcare use, rare disease, complex disability, normal tests, or improvement during separation alone.

  • Do not use hidden observation, record access, testing, separation, visitation restriction, or disclosure outside applicable law, consent, policy, ethics, and authorized safeguarding processes.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess for poisoning, suffocation, infection, bleeding, starvation, dehydration, medication interference, device manipulation, inflicted injury, and complications of unnecessary procedures.

  • Consider risk to siblings, other children, dependent adults, older adults, disabled people, pets, or anyone whose health narrative and care the suspected perpetrator controls.

  • Use respectful, trauma-informed, source-attributed, objective, time-stamped documentation and secure cross-setting communication; inaccurate labels or leaked suspicions can also harm the victim and investigation.

  • Reporting, separation, visitation, discharge, law-enforcement contact, and information sharing should follow current danger, jurisdictional law, authorized multidisciplinary planning, and the least harmful protective option.

Nursing Priorities

  • Stabilize the victim and treat genuine disease or induced injury; preserve necessary specimens, medication records, device observations, and other objective evidence according to policy.

  • Document exactly who reported each symptom, what staff observed, timing, caregiver presence, interventions, and response; avoid motive language, copied assumptions, or confrontation in the chart.

  • Give the patient a private, developmentally appropriate opportunity to communicate when safe, and assess fear, pain, nutrition, development, attachment, trauma, and ability to seek help.

  • Notify the designated safeguarding team when there is reasonable suspicion or reasonable concern and follow mandatory-reporting law and institutional policy; include concern for siblings or other dependents. Do not wait for a perpetrator diagnosis before protecting the victim.

  • Maintain a unified multidisciplinary plan for care, visitors, medicines, specimens, devices, communication, discharge, follow-up, and staff safety.

Red Flags

  • Airway compromise, unexplained apnea, poisoning, severe infection, bleeding, hypoglycemia, malnutrition, dehydration, device disruption, serious injury, or rapid deterioration

  • Evidence or strong concern that illness is being actively induced, treatment is being obstructed, or the victim may return immediately to an unsafe environment

  • Another child or dependent may be exposed, a patient cannot communicate privately, records show escalating invasive harm, or a caregiver is attempting unplanned removal from care

  • A proposed confrontation, disclosure, discharge, visitation change, or separation is proceeding without the responsible safeguarding and legal plan

Complications

  • Poisoning, infection, bleeding, hypoglycemia, suffocation, malnutrition, disability, organ damage, developmental harm, psychological trauma, and death

  • Iatrogenic harm from repeated medicines, radiation, procedures, surgery, devices, hospitalization, restrictions, and delayed recognition of genuine disease

  • Attachment disruption, fear, PTSD symptoms, anxiety, depression, school loss, social isolation, and long-term mistrust of healthcare

  • Harm to siblings or other dependents, unsafe reunification, fragmented investigations, lost evidence, and further abuse after premature confrontation or discharge

  • Mislabeling an innocent caregiver or victim when suspicion is recorded as fact without adequate objective and multidisciplinary review

Prognosis

Victim outcome depends on severity and duration of exposure, medical injury, developmental stage, protective intervention, treatment of genuine illness, and access to stable trauma-informed support. Symptoms may improve after protection, but physical and psychological effects can persist. Evidence for treatment of the perpetrator's core deceptive behavior is limited, and recurrence risk cannot be judged from confession, apparent cooperation, or therapy attendance alone; protection decisions require multidisciplinary evidence and ongoing review.

Prevention

  • Use coordinated records, source-attributed documentation, medication and procedure reconciliation, and early multidisciplinary review when repeated discrepancies create reasonable safeguarding concern.

  • Give patients developmentally appropriate private opportunities to communicate and maintain clear pathways for staff to report concerns without conducting their own uncoordinated investigation.

  • Reduce unnecessary invasive care, verify objective indications, support genuine complex illness, and plan cross-setting follow-up so both induced harm and harmful mislabeling are less likely.

Patient Education

  • The patient's immediate safety and medical needs come first. Real illness can exist at the same time as falsified or induced symptoms and should continue to receive appropriate care.

  • The psychiatric diagnosis belongs to the person causing the deception, not the victim. Safeguarding action may be needed before that person has a completed psychiatric evaluation.

  • Care teams document who reported each detail and what was directly observed so decisions are based on a complete record rather than one disagreement.

  • If you or someone dependent on a caregiver may be in immediate danger, contact emergency services or the responsible child-protection or adult-safeguarding service according to local procedures.

Special Populations

  • Infants and young children cannot reliably describe exposure and can deteriorate quickly; assess growth, feeding, development, injuries, apnea, medicines, devices, siblings, and caregiver-independent observation.

  • Disabled people and dependent adults may rely on the suspected person for communication, transport, medicine, finances, or consent; provide accessible private communication and adult-safeguarding support.

  • Older adults may have genuine multimorbidity, delirium, neurocognitive disorder, and caregiver dependence; separate neglect, exploitation, medication interference, and induced illness through objective review.

  • Pregnant patients or infants may face exposure through medication, feeding, or care interference; coordinate obstetric, neonatal, pediatric, toxicology, and safeguarding teams as indicated.

  • Cultural, language, health-literacy, rare-disease, and healthcare-access differences can create apparent discrepancies and require interpreters and specialist review rather than stereotyping.

Nclex Traps

  • The victim does not receive the perpetrator's psychiatric diagnosis. Victim-centered terms can guide safeguarding before FDIA is established in the suspected perpetrator. FDIA is not limited to mothers; any caregiver or other person must be assessed from evidence rather than stereotype.

  • Genuine illness can coexist. Stabilize and treat the patient while the multidisciplinary team evaluates falsification, induction, and unnecessary care.

  • Do not confront the suspected perpetrator alone. Do not wait for a perpetrator diagnosis or confession before following mandatory-reporting and protection procedures.

  • One discrepancy, difficult caregiver interaction, rare disease, frequent care, or improvement during separation does not independently prove FDIA.

  • No medication treats the core deceptive behavior; perpetrator treatment is separate from immediate victim protection and does not by itself establish safe reunification.

AAP, NICE CG89, NICE NG76, and APSAC support victim-centered terminology, genuine-illness coexistence, objective chronology, multidisciplinary safeguarding, reporting, and protection planning.
