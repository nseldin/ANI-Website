Definition

Nephritic syndrome develops when glomerular inflammation disrupts capillary walls and lowers filtration, allowing red cells and modest protein into urine while retaining salt and water. The characteristic pattern is hematuria with dysmorphic red cells or red-cell casts, hypertension, edema, rising creatinine, and usually subnephrotic proteinuria. Confirm or stage it with urinalysis with microscopy and quantified protein; distinguish it from nephrotic syndrome, urinary tract bleeding, interstitial nephritis, and thrombotic microangiopathy by sediment, protein burden, complement, and systemic findings. The priority is to control blood pressure, sodium retention, and pulmonary congestion because rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and kidney failure can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Urine output change, edema, thirst, dry mucosa, dizziness, flank/suprapubic pain, dysuria, confusion, weakness, weight change, or abnormal heart rhythm can reveal renal, fluid, or electrolyte stress.

  • Escalate anuria, severe potassium abnormality, pulmonary edema, seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, or worsening acidosis.

Diagnostics

  • urinalysis with microscopy and quantified protein

  • creatinine, complement, streptococcal, autoimmune, and infection studies guided by presentation

  • kidney biopsy when rapid progression, systemic features, or diagnostic uncertainty changes treatment

  • Distinguish Nephritic syndrome from nephrotic syndrome, urinary tract bleeding, interstitial nephritis, and thrombotic microangiopathy by sediment, protein burden, complement, and systemic findings.

Labs

  • creatinine and BUN rise when filtration worsens

  • potassium may rise with kidney failure

  • urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is concerning

  • WBC/nitrites/leukocyte esterase support UTI

Treatments

  • control blood pressure, sodium retention, and pulmonary congestion

  • treat the immune or infectious cause without empiric immunosuppression when infection remains possible

  • obtain urgent nephrology input for rapidly progressive disease or dialysis indications

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for severe hypertension, breathlessness with crackles, seizure, rapidly falling urine output, hyperkalemia, or rising creatinine because encephalopathy, pulmonary edema, or dialysis need may follow.

  • Measure blood pressure accurately and assess edema, lung sounds, urine color, and urine output because glomerular inflammation retains salt and water and can cause hypertensive pulmonary edema.

  • Trend creatinine, potassium, sodium, urinalysis, urine protein, hemoglobin, complement, and daily weight to detect falling filtration and disease pattern.

  • Follow prescribed sodium and fluid limits and administer ordered antihypertensive or diuretic therapy because controlling volume and pressure protects the injured glomeruli.

  • Avoid nephrotoxic medicines and unnecessary contrast when alternatives exist because reduced filtration increases the risk of additional kidney injury.

Complications

  • rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and kidney failure

  • hypertensive emergency can complicate Nephritic syndrome.

  • hyperkalemia can complicate Nephritic syndrome.

  • or pulmonary edema can complicate Nephritic syndrome.

  • cerebral or cardiac complications of severe hypertension

Red Flags

  • Severe hypertension with headache, vision change, or seizure

  • Pulmonary edema or rapidly increasing oxygen need

  • Oliguria or rapidly rising creatinine

  • Hyperkalemia with electrocardiographic change

Patient Education

  • Teach daily weight, blood-pressure and urine observation, individualized salt and fluid guidance, and medication avoidance review.

  • Tell the patient to report cola-colored urine, rapidly increasing swelling, severe headache, breathlessness, or markedly reduced urine promptly.

Nclex Traps

  • Red-cell casts localize bleeding to the nephron rather than the bladder

  • Do not equate all edema and proteinuria with nephrotic syndrome
