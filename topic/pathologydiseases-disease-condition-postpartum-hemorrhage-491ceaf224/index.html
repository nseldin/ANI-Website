Pathology

Excessive bleeding after birth causes hypovolemia and shock; uterine atony is most common.

Etiology

Four Ts: tone/uterine atony, trauma/laceration, tissue/retained placenta, thrombin/coagulopathy.

Signs Symptoms

  • Heavy bleeding

  • boggy uterus if atony

  • tachycardia

  • hypotension late

  • pallor

  • decreased urine output

  • clots

Diagnostics

  • Quantified blood loss

  • fundal assessment

  • CBC/coags

  • type and cross

Treatments

  • Fundal massage for atony

  • uterotonics

  • IV fluids/blood

  • repair lacerations/remove retained tissue

  • massive transfusion/surgery if severe

Nursing Priorities

  • Quantify cumulative blood loss and perform frequent fundal and lochia assessment because visual estimation commonly underrecognizes rapid obstetric hemorrhage.

  • Massage a boggy uterus and administer ordered oxytocin or other uterotonic therapy because uterine atony is common and prompt contraction can reduce bleeding.

  • Activate the obstetric hemorrhage protocol, establish rapid access, and prepare warmed blood products because ongoing loss can cause coagulopathy, hypothermia, and shock.

  • Escalate immediately for persistent heavy bleeding, a firm uterus with continued hemorrhage, falling blood pressure, rising pulse, confusion, or oliguria because retained tissue, trauma, inversion, or coagulopathy may require urgent procedure.

Complications

  • Hypovolemic shock

  • DIC

  • Sheehan syndrome

  • death

Patient Education

  • Teach that soaking pads rapidly, passing large clots, fainting, racing heart, breathlessness, or increasing weakness after birth requires emergency evaluation.

  • Explain postpartum anemia follow-up, medication instructions, and emotional support after a hemorrhage because recovery includes both physical and psychological effects.

Nclex Traps

  • If the uterus is boggy, massage it first; if firm with bleeding, think laceration.

Definition

Postpartum hemorrhage: Excessive bleeding after birth causes hypovolemia and shock; uterine atony is most common.

Red Flags

  • Persistent heavy bleeding or rapidly increasing quantified blood loss

  • Firm uterus with ongoing hemorrhage

  • Hypotension, tachycardia, confusion, or oliguria

  • Expanding hematoma, severe pelvic pain, or signs of coagulopathy

    Cue

    Expansion
    • Cue

      T1

      Meaning

      Tone. Uterine atony prevents myometrial contraction from compressing placental-bed vessels.

    • Cue

      T2

      Meaning

      Trauma. Laceration, hematoma, rupture, or inversion can bleed despite a firm uterus.

    • Cue

      T3

      Meaning

      Tissue. Retained placental tissue or clot can prevent effective uterine contraction and sustain bleeding.

    • Cue

      T4

      Meaning

      Thrombin. Coagulopathy impairs stable clot formation and can amplify obstetric bleeding.

    Limitations

    • The framework does not rank causes for an individual patient.

    • Multiple Ts may occur together.

    • Visible blood loss can underestimate severity, and vital signs may change late.

    Activate the hemorrhage protocol and quantify blood loss. Assess fundal tone, bleeding source, placenta completeness, vital signs, perfusion, and coagulation context. Prepare medications, blood products, procedures, and escalation according to orders and protocol.
