Plain Language

Major depressive disorder is an illness in which depressed mood or loss of interest stays present with other changes in sleep, appetite, energy, movement, thinking, self-worth, or thoughts of death. It is more than an ordinary bad day or expected sadness, and effective treatment is available.

Why It Matters

Major depression can impair eating, hydration, sleep, self-care, work, and relationships and can progress to suicide, psychotic depression, catatonia, or severe physical decline. Nurses must assess safety and function directly rather than relying on a depression score alone.

Definition

In DSM-5-TR, major depressive disorder requires at least one major depressive episode: five or more qualifying symptoms during the same 2-week period, including depressed or irritable mood or loss of interest or pleasure, with clinically meaningful distress or impairment and no history of a manic or hypomanic episode. Substance, medication, medical, psychotic-spectrum, bipolar, grief, and cultural context must be assessed. ICD-11 uses depressive-episode and single- or recurrent-depressive-disorder terminology with its own requirements; a PHQ-9 result does not independently diagnose either system.

Pathology

Major depressive disorder is not defined by one structural lesion, laboratory result, or measured neurotransmitter deficit. Clinically important symptoms arise through interacting reward, mood-regulation, sleep and circadian, stress-response, cognitive, genetic, medical, and social processes. Reduced activity and withdrawal can decrease rewarding experience, while rumination and sleep disruption can reinforce hopeless expectations, but none of these mechanisms is independently diagnostic.

Pathophysiology

  • Reduced reward and motivation can produce anhedonia, low initiation, and withdrawal; reduced rewarding activity can then maintain depressive predictions and functional loss.

  • Sleep, appetite, concentration, energy, and psychomotor regulation may change together, while severe illness can add psychotic symptoms, catatonia, dehydration, or nutritional compromise.

Etiology

No single cause explains major depressive disorder. Prior episodes, family vulnerability, trauma, loss, chronic illness or pain, perinatal change, sleep disruption, substance exposure, medicines, isolation, discrimination, and socioeconomic adversity may interact; none alone proves the diagnosis or assigns blame.

Risk Factors

  • A prior depressive episode, prior self-harm or suicide attempt, or a family history of depressive or bipolar illness

  • Trauma, abuse, bereavement, major loss, chronic stress, loneliness, discrimination, or material hardship

  • Chronic medical illness, pain, disability, sleep disorder, pregnancy or postpartum transition, alcohol or drug use, and selected medication effects

Signs Symptoms

  • Depressed or irritable mood and loss of interest or pleasure that persist with meaningful distress or impaired daily function

  • Sleep, appetite or weight, energy, concentration, decision-making, self-worth, guilt, and observable movement or speech changes

  • Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, self-harm, psychotic symptoms, catatonia, or inability to maintain nutrition, hydration, hygiene, or other basic needs in severe illness

Diagnostics

  • Use a clinical interview and longitudinal history to establish symptom number, duration, course, distress, impairment, prior episodes, and classification-specific requirements.

  • Assess lifetime mania or hypomania, psychosis, catatonia, suicide risk, substances, medicines, medical contributors, trauma, grief, developmental and cultural context, and obtain collateral when appropriate.

  • PHQ-9 or another validated measure may support screening, baseline severity, and response tracking but requires clinical confirmation and does not independently establish or exclude the diagnosis.

Labs

  • There is no blood test, scan, or biomarker that confirms major depressive disorder.

  • Use targeted CBC, thyroid, metabolic or electrolyte, nutritional, pregnancy, toxicology, sleep, neurologic, or other testing only when history, examination, population, or planned treatment supports it.

  • A normal targeted evaluation does not diagnose depression; the clinical syndrome, course, impairment, and exclusions remain decisive.

Assessment

Ask privately and directly about suicidal thoughts, self-harm, plan, intent, means, preparations, prior attempts, psychosis, severe agitation, self-neglect, nutrition, hydration, sleep, substance use, trauma and safeguarding, medicines, function, supports, and lifetime periods of unusually increased energy or reduced need for sleep. Describe change from baseline and do not substitute a questionnaire score for the interview.

Differential

  • Bipolar depression, persistent depressive disorder, adjustment disorder, grief, demoralization, PTSD, anxiety, eating, or psychotic disorders

  • Substance- or medication-induced depressive disorder, intoxication, withdrawal, or treatment adverse effects

  • Hypothyroidism, anemia, nutritional deficiency, sleep disorder, neurologic disease, chronic pain, delirium, neurocognitive disorder, and other medical explanations

Treatments

  • Offer depression-specific psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation, or interpersonal therapy according to severity, access, preference, prior response, and comorbidity.

  • Antidepressant pharmacotherapy uses shared decision-making; representative options include sertraline, venlafaxine, or bupropion, but the actual medicine, contraindications, interactions, and monitoring are individualized.

  • Electroconvulsive therapy is a specialist urgent option for catatonia, psychotic depression, severe suicidality, life-threatening nutritional decline, or another need for rapid definitive response.

Contraindications

  • Do not reflexively treat suspected bipolar depression as unipolar major depressive disorder without evaluating lifetime mania, hypomania, mixed symptoms, and antidepressant activation.

  • Check the selected medicine's current contraindications, interactions, pregnancy considerations, seizure risk, organ function, and discontinuation requirements.

  • Do not abruptly stop an antidepressant; tapering and monitoring depend on the medicine, duration, dose, symptoms, and clinical plan.

Safety Considerations

  • Track symptoms, function, suicidal thinking, activation, emerging mania or mixed symptoms, adverse effects, adherence, substance use, and access to potentially lethal medicines.

  • Review antidepressant treatment within 2 weeks and after 1 week for people aged 18 to 25 or those with heightened suicide risk, then continue risk-based follow-up.

  • Older adults and other susceptible patients need attention to falls, bleeding, hyponatremia, polypharmacy, delirium, and medical presentations of depression.

Nursing Priorities

  • Establish immediate safety and assess suicidal ideation, intent, plan, means, prior behavior, psychosis, catatonia, agitation, and ability to maintain basic needs.

  • Assess nutrition, hydration, sleep, hygiene, mobility, substance exposure, medication access, supports, and concrete functional loss; document objective change from baseline.

  • Use simple achievable activity goals, administer and monitor prescribed treatment, support follow-up, and escalate psychosis, catatonia, medical compromise, or inability to maintain safety.

Red Flags

  • Current suicide plan, intent, accessible means, preparations, or recent attempt

  • Command hallucinations, psychotic guilt, catatonia, severe agitation, or emerging mixed or manic activation

  • Refusal or inability to eat or drink, severe self-neglect, delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, or acute neurologic change

Complications

  • Suicide attempt or death, self-harm, dehydration, malnutrition, substance-related injury, or preventable medical decline

  • Loss of housing, employment, education, relationships, or ability to complete essential self-care

  • Psychotic depression, catatonia, relapse, recurrence, and chronic functional impairment

Prognosis

Many people improve substantially with appropriate care, but recurrence risk increases with prior episodes, severe illness, and residual symptoms. Continuation treatment commonly extends at least 6 months after remission and is individualized for recurrence, adverse effects, preference, comorbidity, and safety risk.

Prevention

  • Recognize recurrence early and maintain an individualized relapse-warning, crisis, and follow-up plan.

  • Support treatment adherence and safe tapering, regular sleep and activity, social connection, and treatment of medical, pain, and substance contributors.

  • Reassess suicide risk and emerging bipolar features after major clinical, medication, or life changes.

Patient Education

  • Major depression is an illness, not weakness, and a screening score supports assessment but does not define the diagnosis.

  • Antidepressant benefit may take several weeks; report worsening suicidal thoughts, marked agitation, unusually high energy, or little need for sleep promptly.

  • Do not stop medication abruptly, and use the written safety or crisis plan when warning signs return.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need developmental assessment and age-specific specialist treatment pathways rather than automatic use of an adult medication plan.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum illness require individualized treatment review and urgent response to suicide risk, psychosis, or inability to care for self or infant.

  • Older adults may present with cognitive or physical complaints and have greater falls, sodium, interaction, and polypharmacy risk; adapt care for disability, language, culture, and medical illness.

Nclex Traps

  • A PHQ-9 result does not independently diagnose or exclude major depressive disorder.

  • Improvement in energy can occur before mood and hopelessness improve, so suicide monitoring remains necessary early in treatment.

  • Always assess lifetime mania and hypomania; ordinary bereavement neither automatically excludes nor establishes major depressive disorder.
