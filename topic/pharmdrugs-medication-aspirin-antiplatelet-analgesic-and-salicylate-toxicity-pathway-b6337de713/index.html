Used To Treat

Understanding why low-dose aspirin is antiplatelet, why larger doses provide analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects, why bleeding persists after plasma drug clears, and why overdose causes a mixed acid-base emergency.

Description

Aspirin is an irreversible cyclooxygenase inhibitor whose dominant effect changes with dose: antiplatelet at low exposure, then analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory as systemic exposure rises. It permanently acetylates platelet COX-1, blocking thromboxane A2 synthesis and reducing aggregation for the platelet's remaining life because platelets lack nuclei and cannot make replacement enzyme. Endothelial cells can make new enzyme, helping low systemic exposure favor platelet inhibition. Higher exposures inhibit prostaglandins more broadly, reducing pain, fever, and inflammation but increasing gastric injury, renal perfusion problems, and bleeding. Toxic concentrations uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, stimulate the respiratory center, generate heat and organic acids, and create a mixed respiratory alkalosis plus metabolic acidosis rather than a simple overdose pattern.

Mechanism

Aspirin transfers an acetyl group to COX-1 and COX-2, preventing conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin H2. In platelets, loss of COX-1 lowers thromboxane A2, so activation, vasoconstriction, and aggregation decline until new platelets enter circulation. In gastric mucosa and kidney, lower protective prostaglandins reduce mucus, bicarbonate, and afferent arteriolar support, explaining ulcers and kidney injury in vulnerable patients. In toxicity, direct medullary stimulation produces tachypnea and respiratory alkalosis first; mitochondrial uncoupling then raises oxygen consumption, heat, lactate, ketoacids, and metabolic acidosis. Falling serum pH drives more nonionized salicylate into the brain, which is why abrupt respiratory suppression or intubation without preserving minute ventilation can worsen CNS toxicity.

Boxed Warning

Aspirin itself does not carry the non-aspirin-NSAID cardiovascular-thrombosis boxed warning. Its major warnings are serious GI bleeding, hypersensitivity or aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, hemorrhage, renal injury, pregnancy-related fetal risk later in gestation, and Reye syndrome in children or teenagers with viral illness. Tinnitus, tachypnea, vomiting, diaphoresis, fever, confusion, or acid-base disturbance suggests salicylate toxicity and requires urgent poison-center or emergency management.

Adverse Effects

  • Dyspepsia, bruising, prolonged bleeding, nausea, tinnitus, bronchospasm in susceptible patients, and occult or overt GI blood loss.

  • Major GI or intracranial hemorrhage, anaphylaxis, acute kidney injury, severe salicylate poisoning, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, hyperthermia, seizures, cerebral edema, and death.

Contraindications

  • Avoid through active major bleeding, serious aspirin hypersensitivity, or aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease unless a specialist-directed desensitization plan exists.

  • Do not use for fever in children or teenagers with influenza, varicella, or another viral syndrome unless specifically prescribed because salicylate exposure is associated with Reye syndrome.

Nursing Essentials

  • Clarify the purpose and dose: cardiovascular platelet inhibition, analgesia, inflammation, or another indication. An enteric coating delays dissolution but does not erase systemic bleeding or GI risk.

  • In toxicity, trend respiratory rate, temperature, mental status, glucose, electrolytes, pH, bicarbonate, renal function, and serial salicylate levels; clinical worsening outweighs reassurance from one falling concentration.

Interactions

  • Anticoagulants, other antiplatelets, SSRIs/SNRIs, corticosteroids, alcohol, and other NSAIDs can increase bleeding or gastric injury.

  • Ibuprofen taken around low-dose aspirin can competitively occupy platelet COX-1 before aspirin and may reduce irreversible antiplatelet effect; exact timing and indication require clinician or pharmacist review.

Key Labs

  • CBC, hemoglobin/hematocrit, platelets, renal function, stool or bleeding evaluation, and indication-specific monitoring.

  • For toxicity: serial salicylate concentrations, blood gas, electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, temperature, ECG, urine pH during alkalinization, and frequent neurologic reassessment.

Nclex Traps

  • Platelet inhibition outlasts aspirin's plasma half-life because the target platelet cannot synthesize replacement COX-1.

  • A near-normal pH can hide two opposing disorders. Salicylate poisoning commonly produces respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis at the same time.
