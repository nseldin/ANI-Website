Definition

Accessory muscle use is an observed respiratory-effort sign in which extra neck, shoulder-girdle, chest-wall, or abdominal muscles visibly help with breathing. These muscles should not be prominent during quiet resting breathing.

Plain Language

The patient is recruiting extra muscles to pull air in or push it out because ordinary breathing effort is not enough.

Why It Matters

Accessory muscle use at rest is a warning of increased work of breathing and possible respiratory distress. If effort fades while alertness, airflow, or breath depth worsens, that can mean exhaustion and impending respiratory failure rather than improvement.

Associated Conditions

  • increased work of breathing from airway obstruction, reduced lung or chest-wall compliance, high ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle weakness, or impending respiratory failure, with normal recruitment possible during vigorous exercise

Assessment Method

Observe the patient at rest from the front and side for inspiratory tensing of the sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles, clavicle or shoulder elevation, supraclavicular or intercostal retraction, and active abdominal expiration; document rate, depth, speech, posture, breath sounds, oxygenation, mental status, and whether effort is improving or fading.

Positive Finding

Neck, shoulder-girdle, chest-wall, or abdominal muscles not normally prominent during quiet breathing visibly contract in time with inspiration or forced expiration.

Mechanism

When the diaphragm and usual respiratory muscles cannot meet ventilatory demand, additional muscles elevate the sternum and upper ribs, stabilize the shoulder girdle, or actively push air out, increasing total work and energy use.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Accessory muscle use finding raises concern for increased work of breathing from airway obstruction, reduced lung or chest-wall compliance, high ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle weakness, or impending respiratory failure, with normal recruitment possible during vigorous exercise because when the diaphragm and usual respiratory muscles cannot meet ventilatory demand, additional muscles elevate the sternum and upper ribs, stabilize the shoulder girdle, or actively push air out, increasing total work and energy use. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Accessory muscle use does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Accessory muscle use to focus evaluation of increased work of breathing from airway obstruction, reduced lung or chest-wall compliance, high ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle weakness, or impending respiratory failure, with normal recruitment possible during vigorous exercise and the reviewed alternatives: voluntary deep breathing, shivering, neck muscle tension, seizure activity. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Observe the patient at rest from the front and side for inspiratory tensing of the sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles, clavicle or shoulder elevation, supraclavicular or intercostal retraction, and active abdominal expiration; document rate, depth, speech, posture, breath sounds, oxygenation, mental status, and whether effort is improving or fading. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Accessory muscle use as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Exercise, singing, anxiety, chronic adaptation, body habitus, neck movement, and voluntary deep breathing can alter visibility; disappearing accessory effort can mean improvement or dangerous fatigue, so airflow, rate, depth, gas exchange, and mental status must be trended together. Therefore, Accessory muscle use cannot confirm or exclude increased work of breathing from airway obstruction, reduced lung or chest-wall compliance, high ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle weakness, or impending respiratory failure, with normal recruitment possible during vigorous exercise by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • voluntary deep breathing

  • shivering

  • neck muscle tension

  • seizure activity

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as voluntary deep breathing, shivering, neck muscle tension, seizure activity.

False Negative Situations

Accessory muscle use may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased work of breathing from airway obstruction, reduced lung or chest-wall compliance, high ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle weakness, or impending respiratory failure, with normal recruitment possible during vigorous exercise.

Modern Status

Current sign of increased respiratory effort at rest and a warning of respiratory distress; it is not a diagnosis or a reliable severity grade by itself. Interpret Accessory muscle use in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Exercise, singing, anxiety, chronic adaptation, body habitus, neck movement, and voluntary deep breathing can alter visibility; disappearing accessory effort can mean improvement or dangerous fatigue, so airflow, rate, depth, gas exchange, and mental status must be trended together.

Safety Precautions

Observe Accessory muscle use with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Observe the patient at rest from the front and side for inspiratory tensing of the sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles, clavicle or shoulder elevation, supraclavicular or intercostal retraction, and active abdominal expiration; document rate, depth, speech, posture, breath sounds, oxygenation, mental status, and whether effort is improving or fading. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Escalate immediately when accessory muscle use accompanies inability to speak, stridor, cyanosis, low oxygenation, paradoxical breathing, confusion, falling respiratory rate or depth, or exhaustion; do not force the patient supine, and do not mistake fading effort with worsening alertness for recovery.
