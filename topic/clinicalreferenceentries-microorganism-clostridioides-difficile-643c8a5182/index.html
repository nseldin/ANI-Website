Definition

Clostridioides difficile is a Gram-positive, obligately anaerobic, spore-forming bacillus that can colonize the colon and can cause antibiotic-associated diarrhea and colitis when toxigenic strains proliferate and produce disease. Detecting the organism or one of its genes is not automatically the same as diagnosing infection; symptoms and the testing strategy must fit the clinical picture.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    The accepted species name is Clostridioides difficile; Clostridium difficile is a former scientific name and remains searchable. C. difficile and C. diff abbreviate the organism, whereas CDI abbreviates the disease C. difficile infection. Keeping those identities separate prevents an asymptomatic carrier from being mislabeled as having active colitis.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    C. difficile is a bacillus that grows under anaerobic conditions and forms environmentally persistent spores. Vegetative cells produce disease-associated toxins, while spores help the organism survive outside the body and tolerate conditions that kill many non-spore-forming bacteria. Acid-fast staining is not its defining method, and routine morphology alone cannot distinguish toxigenic infection from colonization.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    The colon and healthcare environment are important reservoirs. Spores shed in feces contaminate hands, equipment, toilets, and surfaces; ingestion after hand-to-mouth transfer completes the fecal-oral route. Antibiotics increase risk because they disrupt colonization resistance supplied by the normal intestinal microbiota, creating ecological space for C. difficile to expand. Exposure and colonization can occur without immediate symptoms, so one fixed species-level incubation period is not dependable.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Antibiotic-mediated disruption of the gut microbiota lowers competition and allows toxigenic C. difficile to proliferate. Toxin A and toxin B disrupt intestinal epithelial cell function, damage the mucosal barrier, and provoke inflammation. The resulting permeability, fluid secretion, and inflammatory injury explain watery diarrhea and colitis; severe injury can progress to ileus, toxic megacolon, perforation, shock, or death.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Active infection commonly presents with new clinically significant diarrhea and abdominal symptoms after antibiotic or healthcare exposure, although community-associated disease also occurs. Severe disease may produce leukocytosis, kidney injury, ileus, marked abdominal distention, hypotension, or systemic toxicity. Absence of diarrhea does not exclude fulminant disease when ileus is present, but routine testing of formed stool or asymptomatic patients risks diagnosing colonization instead of disease.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Diagnosis combines compatible symptoms with a validated stool testing algorithm for toxin and/or toxigenic organism detection. Different assays answer different questions: a highly sensitive nucleic-acid test can detect a toxigenic strain even when toxin-mediated disease is not established, while a toxin assay is less sensitive but more directly demonstrates free toxin. Testing should therefore follow institutional diagnostic stewardship rules, and a resolved patient should not receive a test of cure merely to prove eradication.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Place a patient with suspected or confirmed CDI on Contact Precautions promptly according to facility guidance, use gloves and gowns, dedicate equipment when possible, and clean with an appropriate sporicidal process. Hand hygiene remains essential; soap-and-water washing is emphasized when hands are visibly soiled and during C. difficile outbreaks because alcohol does not reliably remove spores. Antibiotic stewardship reduces the ecological pressure that enables disease.

    Prevention and safety

  • Content

    Reviewed against NCBI Taxonomy, CDC clinical, diagnostic, and acute-care prevention guidance, and a peer-reviewed toxin study. The treatment link is restricted to symptomatic CDI and does not recommend therapy for asymptomatic carriage.
