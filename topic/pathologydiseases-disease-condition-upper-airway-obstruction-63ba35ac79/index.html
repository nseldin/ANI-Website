Definition

Upper airway obstruction develops when edema, infection, foreign material, trauma, tumor, or laryngospasm narrows the airway above the thorax where small radius changes sharply increase resistance. The characteristic pattern is stridor, hoarseness, drooling, tripod posture, retractions, inability to handle secretions, or rapidly worsening voice and breathing. Confirm or stage it with immediate airway-focused examination while minimizing agitation; distinguish it from lower-airway wheeze, vocal-cord dysfunction, croup, epiglottic disease, anaphylaxis, and panic by sound location, voice, secretion handling, and visualization. The priority is to call for expert airway help early and provide oxygen in the least upsetting manner because complete obstruction can complicate Upper airway obstruction. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • immediate airway-focused examination while minimizing agitation

  • flexible visualization or imaging only when the airway is stable enough

  • cause-directed testing for allergy, infection, trauma, mass, or foreign body after oxygenation is secured

  • Distinguish Upper airway obstruction from lower-airway wheeze, vocal-cord dysfunction, croup, epiglottic disease, anaphylaxis, and panic by sound location, voice, secretion handling, and visualization.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • call for expert airway help early and provide oxygen in the least upsetting manner

  • treat anaphylaxis, edema, infection, or foreign body according to cause

  • prepare for awake, surgical, or other advanced airway rescue when deterioration is likely

Nursing Priorities

  • Prepare for controlled intubation or emergency front-of-neck access while minimizing sedation and repeated examination in a tenuous airway because loss of muscle tone or edema progression can convert partial obstruction into cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate failure.

  • Assess ability to speak, cough, swallow, and handle secretions; listen for stridor or silence; inspect for swelling, trauma, burns, or foreign body without agitating the patient because a partially open airway can close abruptly.

  • Keep the patient upright when tolerated, summon airway expertise, provide high-concentration oxygen, attach continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, and bring age- and cause-appropriate difficult-airway equipment because oxygenation may be lost during transfer or repeated attempts.

  • Use cause-specific first aid: encourage effective cough for mild foreign-body obstruction, perform guideline-directed thrusts for severe choking, give intramuscular epinephrine for anaphylaxis, and avoid blind finger sweeps because the wrong maneuver can worsen impaction or delay definitive care.

  • Activate resuscitation for inability to speak or cough, silent airflow, cyanosis, falling consciousness, severe stridor with fatigue, rapidly expanding neck or tongue swelling, or poor air movement after intervention because complete obstruction causes hypoxic arrest within minutes.

Complications

  • complete obstruction can complicate Upper airway obstruction.

  • hypoxic arrest can complicate Upper airway obstruction.

  • aspiration can complicate Upper airway obstruction.

  • post-obstructive pulmonary edema can complicate Upper airway obstruction.

  • failed or traumatic intubation

  • rapid decompensation after sedatives or supine positioning

Red Flags

  • Inability to speak, cough effectively, or move air

  • Cyanosis, falling consciousness, bradycardia, or apnea

  • Severe stridor with retractions, fatigue, drooling, or tripod positioning

  • Rapidly expanding tongue, floor-of-mouth, facial, or neck swelling

Patient Education

  • Teach choking prevention that fits the cause, including food size, supervision, denture fit, and safe swallowing, because recurrent obstruction is often preventable.

  • Patients with prior anaphylaxis should carry accessible epinephrine and use it promptly for airway symptoms while someone calls emergency services.

Nclex Traps

  • Stridor is an upper-airway emergency sign, not simply loud wheezing

  • Do not force a distressed patient with suspected critical obstruction to lie flat
