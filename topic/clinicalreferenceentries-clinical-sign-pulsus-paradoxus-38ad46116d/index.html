Definition

Pulsus paradoxus is a blood-pressure and pulse sign. It is a clue to exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume. A positive finding means systolic pressure falls excessively during inspiration and peripheral pulse strength may fade.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Pulsus paradoxus means systolic pressure falls excessively during inspiration and peripheral pulse strength may fade.

Why It Matters

A positive Pulsus paradoxus response can signal exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume. Pericardial constraint or extreme respiratory pressure swings increase ventricular interdependence and reduce left-sided filling during inspiration. Pulsus paradoxus cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume

Assessment Method

Measure systolic pressure slowly while noting Korotkoff sounds during expiration-only and throughout respiration.

Positive Finding

Systolic pressure falls excessively during inspiration and peripheral pulse strength may fade.

Mechanism

Pericardial constraint or extreme respiratory pressure swings increase ventricular interdependence and reduce left-sided filling during inspiration.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Pulsus paradoxus response raises concern for exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume because pericardial constraint or extreme respiratory pressure swings increase ventricular interdependence and reduce left-sided filling during inspiration. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Pulsus paradoxus does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Pulsus paradoxus to focus evaluation of exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume and the reviewed alternatives: hypovolemia, massive pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Measure systolic pressure slowly while noting Korotkoff sounds during expiration-only and throughout respiration. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Pulsus paradoxus as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Arrhythmia, tachypnea, obesity, and positive-pressure ventilation complicate measurement. Therefore, Pulsus paradoxus cannot confirm or exclude exaggerated inspiratory reduction in left-ventricular stroke volume by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • hypovolemia

  • massive pulmonary embolism

  • tension pneumothorax

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from hypovolemia, massive pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Pulsus paradoxus may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Pulsus paradoxus, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Measure systolic pressure slowly while noting Korotkoff sounds during expiration-only and throughout respiration. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not delay echocardiography or stabilization to perfect a cuff measurement in an unstable patient.
