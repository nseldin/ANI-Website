Risk Factors

  • Critical illness commonly produces several simultaneous processes through shock, ventilation, kidney injury, gastric losses, fluids, and medications.

  • Chronic lung disease creates baseline hypercapnia and bicarbonate retention that can be mistaken for a new acute disorder.

  • Low albumin can conceal a clinically important accumulation of unmeasured anions.

Signs Symptoms

  • Tachypnea, bradypnea, respiratory fatigue, altered consciousness, hypotension, vomiting, diarrhea, edema, and toxin clues help assign cause and urgency.

  • SpO2 can remain acceptable while ventilation fails and PaCO2 rises, especially with supplemental oxygen.

  • A patient can look critically ill with a near-normal pH because two primary processes offset each other.

Diagnostics

  • Confirm labeled values and units; common ABG shorthand pH/PaCO2/PaO2/HCO3 varies by institution, so never infer an unlabeled sequence when ambiguity remains.

  • Apply expected compensation and explicitly name every primary process supported by the mismatch.

  • Calculate anion gap from the contemporaneous chemistry panel, use the laboratory's reference interval, and consider albumin and delta analysis rather than assuming a universal normal gap.

Labs

  • Blood-gas HCO3 is usually calculated from pH and PCO2, whereas chemistry total CO2 is measured and consists mostly of bicarbonate; small differences are expected.

  • Anion gap, albumin, sodium, chloride, chemistry bicarbonate, lactate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, glucose, creatinine, potassium, salicylate, and osmolal gap are selected by pattern.

  • PaO2 and arterial oxygen saturation describe arterial oxygenation; a VBG does not provide an arterial PaO2 substitute.

Treatments

  • Stabilize airway, ventilation, oxygenation, perfusion, and dangerous electrolyte abnormalities while cause-directed testing proceeds.

  • Treat each primary disorder, such as insulin and fluids for ketoacidosis, perfusion and source control for lactic acidosis, ventilation for CO2 retention, chloride and potassium for appropriate metabolic alkalosis, or antidote and dialysis for selected toxins.

  • Repeat gases when physiology or therapy changes because one snapshot cannot confirm trajectory.

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify patient identity, arterial or venous source, oxygen device, FiO2, ventilator settings, collection time, and specimen quality because interpretation begins with knowing what was sampled under which conditions.

  • Assess pH first, identify the ventilatory PaCO2 and bicarbonate process moving pH, then test expected compensation because this sequence prevents a near-normal pH from hiding a mixed disorder.

  • Assess PaO2 and saturation separately from acid-base status and calculate the anion gap with albumin context when indicated because oxygenation and unmeasured acids answer different clinical questions.

  • Review electrolytes, lactate, ketones, renal function, respiratory examination, medications, and prior gases because blood-gas numbers describe physiology but do not identify the cause by themselves.

  • Escalate immediately for critical pH, severe hypoxemia, rapidly rising PaCO2, dangerous potassium change, or a result inconsistent with the patient's condition because urgent treatment or immediate repeat sampling may be required.

Complications

  • A missed mixed disorder can delay ventilation, toxin treatment, dialysis, perfusion restoration, or electrolyte correction.

  • Misusing venous oxygen values can conceal hypoxemia, while relying on SpO2 alone can conceal hypercapnia.

  • Treating an artifact can create iatrogenic alkalosis, sodium load, volume overload, or inappropriate ventilator change.

Contraindications

  • Do not label compensation before calculating whether it is appropriate.

  • Do not use PaO2 from a VBG or call PvCO2 PaCO2.

  • Do not infer clinical improvement from pH normalization if lactate, anion gap, ketones, ventilation, or perfusion is worsening.

Red Flags

  • pH below 7.20 or above 7.60 with clinical deterioration

  • PaO2 or saturation critically below the ordered target

  • Rapid PaCO2 rise with somnolence, fatigue, or weak respirations

  • Unexpected result suggesting sampling error while the patient is unstable

Patient Education

  • Explain that a blood gas answers oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base questions separately, so one normal component does not make the entire result normal.

  • Teach patients that repeated gases may be needed after oxygen or ventilator changes because the sample reflects one moment under specific support settings.

Nclex Traps

  • Read pH first, but do not stop after finding one value that points in the same direction.

  • Normal pH can hide two primary disorders.

  • Oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base balance are related but separate questions.

Systematic blood gas interpretation

Diagnostic reasoning and blood gases

Definition

Systematic blood-gas interpretation is a repeatable method that identifies the pH state, primary process or processes, adequacy of compensation, oxygenation context, and likely cause without letting one abnormal value dominate. It separates acidemia from acidosis and alkalemia from alkalosis, so offsetting disorders remain visible. A blood gas is a time-stamped physiologic snapshot; it must be read with the patient, specimen type, oxygen and ventilator settings, electrolytes, and treatment timeline.

Etiology

Blood gases become abnormal through altered ventilation, bicarbonate balance, acid production, kidney response, or mixed mechanisms. Sampling from an artery or vein, tourniquet delay, air contamination, delayed analysis, rapidly changing ventilation, and treatment between draws can also change apparent results. The method is therefore designed to detect both biology and preanalytic mismatch.

Pathology

Pattern recognition alone fails when both PaCO2 and HCO3 move in acidifying or alkalinizing directions or when opposing disorders normalize pH. A simple arrow method might label pH 7.25, PaCO2 40, HCO3 17 as metabolic acidosis and overlook that PaCO2 should be much lower; calculation reveals added respiratory acidosis. It can likewise miss high-gap acidosis plus metabolic alkalosis when bicarbonate appears only mildly low.

Pathophysiology

Step 1 is clinical stability and specimen identification. Step 2 classifies pH as acidemia, alkalemia, or near normal. Step 3 asks which PaCO2 and HCO3 changes drive that direction; if both drive it, two primary processes exist. Step 4 applies the appropriate expected-compensation estimate. Step 5 calculates the locally defined anion gap, considers albumin adjustment, and uses delta relationships when a high gap is present. Step 6 checks oxygenation with PaO2 only on an arterial specimen and relates it to FiO2, SpO2, work of breathing, and perfusion. Step 7 integrates lactate, ketones, chloride, potassium, renal function, losses, drugs, toxins, and serial trends. This sequence works because it follows the bicarbonate-to-CO2 ratio before assigning a disease name.
