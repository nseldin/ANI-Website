Summary

The systemic circulation is a branching pressure-and-exchange network: elastic arteries buffer pulsatile ejection, muscular arteries distribute flow, arterioles regulate resistance, capillaries exchange gases and solutes, and venules and veins return blood while storing much of the circulating volume. Vessel structure matches function. Thick elastic or muscular walls tolerate and regulate pressure, one-cell-thick capillaries shorten diffusion distance, and compliant veins accommodate volume at low pressure.

Quick Answer

Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return it, and capillaries connect the microcirculation between them. Most arteries and veins have intima, media, and adventitia, but their proportions differ; capillaries are mainly endothelium and basement membrane. Arterioles are the major adjustable resistance vessels, so small radius changes strongly alter flow and arterial pressure. Veins are high-compliance reservoirs aided by valves, skeletal-muscle contraction, and breathing. Local metabolic signals redirect blood toward active tissues while neural and hormonal systems defend whole-body pressure.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The aorta and large elastic arteries contain abundant elastin that stretches during systole and recoils during diastole. Muscular arteries contain more smooth muscle and distribute flow to organs. Arterioles have small lumens and proportionally strong smooth-muscle control. Capillaries form vast networks with walls thin enough for exchange; continuous, fenestrated, and sinusoidal patterns match different organs. Venules collect capillary blood and participate in inflammation. Veins have thinner walls, larger lumens, and high compliance; limb veins often contain one-way valves. The tunica intima includes endothelium, the media contains smooth muscle and elastic tissue, and the adventitia provides connective support and external vessels or nerves.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: pressure, resistance, and exchange

    Text

    The heart supplies the pressure gradient, while vascular resistance determines how readily flow occurs. Because resistance is highly sensitive to radius, arteriolar constriction can markedly reduce organ flow and raise upstream arterial pressure; dilation does the opposite. Elastic recoil smooths intermittent ventricular ejection into more continuous flow. At capillaries, slow velocity across a huge combined cross-sectional area permits diffusion and regulated movement of water and solutes. Venous return depends on the pressure gradient toward the right atrium, blood volume, venous tone, valves, skeletal-muscle pumping, and respiratory pressure changes. A vessel's role therefore comes from both its wall and its position in the circuit.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Sympathetic nerves and circulating catecholamines redistribute tone, while the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, vasopressin, and natriuretic systems influence pressure and volume. Endothelium releases mediators such as nitric oxide that affect smooth muscle, platelet behavior, inflammation, and permeability. Local carbon dioxide, acidity, oxygen need, adenosine, temperature, and metabolic activity help match tissue perfusion to demand. Cerebral, coronary, renal, skin, and skeletal-muscle beds do not regulate identically. For detailed renal autoregulation and acid-base effects, follow the connected renal and acid-base foundations rather than assuming that one systemic rule explains every organ.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Atherosclerotic plaque narrows arteries and can rupture, promoting thrombosis and downstream ischemia. Aneurysm reflects wall weakness and risks rupture or dissection. Excess arteriolar constriction or inadequate circulating volume reduces tissue perfusion; pathologic dilation can cause distributive shock. Venous valve failure and obstruction raise hydrostatic pressure, causing edema and stasis. Capillary leak moves fluid and proteins into interstitium, reducing effective intravascular volume. Endothelial injury promotes inflammation and coagulation. Peripheral arterial disease causes exertional ischemic pain and poor healing, whereas deep-vein thrombosis threatens pulmonary embolism. The same swollen limb can therefore represent different vascular failures requiring different responses.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Vasodilators reduce vascular tone through different mechanisms; vasopressors increase tone and may support pressure but can compromise peripheral perfusion if excessive. Antihypertensives alter volume, cardiac function, neural drive, or vascular resistance. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs reduce selected thrombotic pathways but increase bleeding risk; they do not dissolve every existing clot. Statins reduce atherosclerotic risk beyond a simple cholesterol number. Diabetes, smoking, hypertension, inflammation, and dyslipidemia injure vessels over time. Compression and mobility may support venous return in appropriate patients, but suspected acute arterial compromise or an unassessed painful swollen limb requires evaluation before routine assumptions.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    Blood pressure is a systemic clue, not a direct measurement of every organ's flow. Compare both arms when indicated and use correct cuff size and technique. Pulse examination, capillary refill, skin temperature, color, and neurologic function assess consequences of flow. An ankle-brachial index evaluates lower-extremity arterial disease; duplex ultrasound evaluates flow and venous thrombosis; CT or MR angiography maps vessels; conventional angiography can diagnose and treat. Lactate may rise with impaired oxygen delivery but is not specific to one vascular cause. D-dimer can help exclude venous thromboembolism only in the right pretest context; it does not diagnose a clot by itself.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess central stability and compare distal pulses, temperature, color, sensation, movement, pain, capillary refill, edema, and symmetry. Serial comparison detects change better than one undocumented finding. Position and mobilize according to the cause: elevating a venous-edematous limb may help, whereas severe arterial ischemia may worsen when the limb is raised. Protect insensate or poorly perfused skin, avoid constrictive devices, administer vasoactive or antithrombotic therapy with ordered monitoring, and watch IV sites for infiltration. In shock, track mentation, urine output, skin perfusion, pressure trends, and lactate alongside the underlying cause rather than pursuing a pressure number alone.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Sudden severe limb pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, or poikilothermia suggests acute arterial occlusion and demands immediate escalation. A tearing chest or back pain with pulse or pressure difference may signal aortic dissection. Unilateral swelling and pain with sudden dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, or hypoxemia raises concern for venous thromboembolism. Uncontrolled bleeding, rapidly expanding hematoma, threatened airway from neck swelling, or shock after vascular procedure is urgent. New neurologic deficit can be vascular even without pain. A normal capillary refill does not safely exclude a proximal occlusion or evolving compartment syndrome.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Artery and vein describe direction, not oxygen content. Veins are not passive empty tubes; their compliance and tone strongly affect venous return. Capillary exchange is not governed by a single memorized pressure in every organ, and edema is not always fluid overload. A palpable pulse does not prove adequate microvascular perfusion, while an absent distal pulse may be chronic or acute and must be compared with symptoms and prior findings. Never massage a suspected DVT. Do not apply the same positioning rule to arterial and venous disease. Treat systemic pressure as one part of perfusion, not as a synonym for blood flow.
