Definition

Primary sclerosing cholangitis is a chronic fibroinflammatory disease that creates multifocal strictures in intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic bile ducts. Obstructed bile flow causes cholestatic injury, recurrent bacterial cholangitis, progressive biliary fibrosis, and major lifetime risks of cirrhosis and hepatobiliary cancer.

Pathology

Repeated duct-wall inflammation and concentric periductal fibrosis narrow and sometimes obliterate bile ducts. Alternating strictured and relatively preserved or dilated segments create the characteristic cholangiographic pattern, while chronic bile stasis injures hepatocytes and promotes infection and biliary cirrhosis.

Pathophysiology

  • Narrowed ducts raise resistance to bile flow. Retained bile acids and pressure injure duct epithelium and liver tissue, while stagnant bile permits ascending bacteria to produce acute cholangitis.

  • PSC has a strong immune and gut association with inflammatory bowel disease, especially a colitis phenotype, but the initiating cause remains uncertain. The same chronic duct injury also creates a field at risk for cholangiocarcinoma.

Etiology

The cause is incompletely understood and likely combines genetic, immune, microbiome, and environmental factors. Inflammatory bowel disease is the major association, but PSC can occur without known IBD and can be diagnosed in any sex or age group. It must be separated from secondary and IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis.

Risk Factors

  • Inflammatory bowel disease, especially ulcerative-colitis-pattern disease

  • Family history of PSC or inflammatory bowel disease

  • Another autoimmune or immune-mediated condition

  • A prior PSC diagnosis with a new relevant stricture, increasing the need to assess obstruction and malignancy rather than simply attributing symptoms to chronic disease

Signs Symptoms

  • No symptoms initially, with cholestatic liver tests discovered incidentally

  • Fatigue, pruritus, right-upper-quadrant discomfort, jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, or hepatosplenomegaly

  • Fever, chills, jaundice, and abdominal pain suggesting acute bacterial cholangitis

  • Weight loss, worsening pruritus, or progressive jaundice with a relevant stricture or possible malignancy

  • Ascites, edema, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy, or other advanced cirrhosis findings

Diagnostics

  • Evaluate a cholestatic liver profile with high-quality MRI and MRCP and exclude secondary causes and IgG4-related disease.

  • Do not use ERCP solely to diagnose uncomplicated PSC. Reserve it for a relevant stricture, tissue sampling, therapeutic intervention, or inadequate response to antibiotics when obstruction remains concerning.

  • Use liver biopsy when high-quality MRCP is normal but small-duct PSC is suspected or when autoimmune-hepatitis overlap is being assessed.

  • Perform ileocolonoscopy with biopsies at diagnosis because inflammatory bowel disease can be clinically silent, and establish current hepatobiliary and colorectal surveillance based on phenotype and age.

Labs

  • Alkaline phosphatase and GGT are commonly elevated but can fluctuate; a normal or improving value does not by itself exclude progression.

  • Bilirubin can rise with a relevant stricture, cholangitis, or advanced disease, while AST and ALT are often only modestly elevated.

  • Albumin, INR, and platelet count reflect synthetic function and portal-hypertension progression.

  • Serum IgG4 helps identify IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis, while CA 19-9 is an imperfect adjunct that never independently confirms cholangiocarcinoma.

Assessment

Trend fever, pain, jaundice, pruritus, stool and urine color, weight, nutrition, mental status, bleeding, ascites, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, and platelets. Distinguish chronic cholestasis from acute infected obstruction, decompensated cirrhosis, or malignancy, because each has a different urgency and treatment.

Differential

Differentiate PSC from secondary sclerosing cholangitis, IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis, choledocholithiasis or other obstruction, cholangiocarcinoma, and autoimmune-hepatitis overlap. The bare term sclerosing cholangitis is therefore not a safe exact synonym for PSC.

Treatments

  • No medication has been proved to reliably reverse PSC or improve transplant-free survival; clinical-trial participation is encouraged when appropriate.

  • Selected patients may continue standard or intermediate-dose ursodeoxycholic acid only when there is objective biochemical or symptomatic improvement under hepatology guidance.

  • Treat bacterial cholangitis promptly with antibiotics and evaluate for obstruction. Use ERCP with sampling and treatment for a clinically relevant stricture or inadequate response when indicated.

  • Manage pruritus, fat-soluble-vitamin and bone risk, inflammatory bowel disease, portal-hypertension complications, and nutrition through coordinated care.

  • Liver transplantation is definitive treatment for advanced liver failure, recurrent uncontrolled cholangitis, or selected other severe complications.

Contraindications

  • High-dose ursodeoxycholic acid should not be used because it has been associated with harm.

  • Routine oral vancomycin is not established therapy for adult PSC outside an appropriate specialist or research context.

  • General immunosuppression does not treat uncomplicated PSC; it is reserved for a confirmed overlap or another indication.

  • Avoid diagnostic ERCP when MRCP can establish large-duct disease because ERCP adds pancreatitis, cholangitis, bleeding, and perforation risk.

  • Do not use CA 19-9 alone to diagnose or exclude cholangiocarcinoma.

Safety Considerations

  • Fever, chills, jaundice, and right-upper-quadrant pain can signal infected obstruction and progress to sepsis.

  • An indicated ERCP requires antimicrobial and procedural planning and postprocedure monitoring for pancreatitis, bleeding, infection, and perforation.

  • Worsening pruritus, weight loss, bilirubin, duct dilation, or CA 19-9 trend may require urgent malignancy and stricture evaluation.

  • PSC-associated IBD carries a higher colorectal-cancer risk than IBD alone and requires intensified surveillance.

  • Cirrhosis changes medication, procedure, bleeding, nutrition, and infection safety even if the patient feels well.

Nursing Priorities

  • Trend temperature, pain, jaundice, pruritus, stool and urine changes, weight, intake, mental status, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, and platelets.

  • Activate urgent evaluation for cholangitis or sepsis and obtain cultures without delaying antibiotic and biliary-obstruction management.

  • Provide skin and sleep support for pruritus and assess fat-soluble vitamins, nutrition, bone health, and fall risk.

  • Coordinate IBD treatment and colonoscopic, gallbladder, hepatobiliary, and cirrhosis surveillance and reinforce why surveillance continues without symptoms.

  • Before and after ERCP, verify indication and prophylaxis plan and monitor pain, fever, bleeding, vomiting, peritoneal findings, and hydration.

Red Flags

  • Fever or chills with jaundice or right-upper-quadrant pain

  • Hypotension, confusion, oliguria, or other sepsis signs

  • Rapidly rising bilirubin, progressive duct dilation, unexplained weight loss, or worsening pruritus

  • Severe abdominal pain, fever, bleeding, or persistent vomiting after ERCP

  • GI bleeding, tense ascites, encephalopathy, or another decompensated-cirrhosis finding

Complications

  • Recurrent bacterial cholangitis and clinically relevant biliary strictures

  • Biliary cirrhosis, portal hypertension, liver failure, and transplantation

  • Cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder malignancy

  • PSC-IBD-associated colorectal cancer

  • Severe pruritus, fat-soluble-vitamin deficiency, osteoporosis, malnutrition, and recurrent postprocedure complications

Prognosis

PSC has a variable but often progressive course. Small-duct disease may remain limited or evolve into large-duct PSC, and symptom absence does not guarantee stability. Transplantation is effective for advanced disease, although PSC can recur in the graft.

Prevention

No strategy prevents PSC. Prevent delayed complications through regular hepatology follow-up, current cancer and IBD surveillance, prompt evaluation of cholangitis or obstruction symptoms, vaccination and cirrhosis care when indicated, bone and nutrition protection, and avoidance of unproven high-dose therapy.

Patient Education

  • PSC scars bile ducts; antibiotics treat a superimposed infection but do not reverse the underlying fibrosis.

  • Call urgently for fever, chills, jaundice, increasing right-upper-quadrant pain, confusion, very dark urine, or reduced urine.

  • Keep colonoscopy, imaging, laboratory, and hepatology appointments even when symptoms are mild because cancer and stricture risk can be silent.

  • Do not start high-dose ursodiol, vancomycin, supplements, or other unproven treatments without hepatology review.

Special Populations

  • PSC with inflammatory bowel disease requires intensified colonoscopic surveillance and coordination between hepatology and gastroenterology.

  • Children more often have autoimmune-hepatitis overlap and follow age-specific cancer-surveillance thresholds.

  • Pregnancy requires coordinated liver, IBD, medication, and obstetric planning.

  • Patients with advanced cirrhosis or recurrent cholangitis need early transplant-center involvement rather than waiting for terminal decompensation.

Nclex Traps

  • MRCP, not routine diagnostic ERCP, is the first-line cholangiographic study.

  • Antibiotics treat acute bacterial cholangitis, not the chronic PSC mechanism.

  • PSC is strongly associated with IBD and cholangiocarcinoma and is distinct from primary biliary cholangitis.

  • A falling alkaline phosphatase alone does not prove the duct disease is cured.
