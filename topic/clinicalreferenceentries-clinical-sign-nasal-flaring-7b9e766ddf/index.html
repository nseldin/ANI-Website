Definition

Nasal flaring is an observed respiratory sign in which the nares, meaning the nostril openings, widen repeatedly during inspiration. It is especially important in infants and young children.

Plain Language

The nostrils spread wider with each breath because the patient is working harder to pull air in.

Why It Matters

Persistent nasal flaring can be an early sign of respiratory distress, particularly in an infant. Interpret it with feeding, grunting, retractions, respiratory rate, oxygenation, color, alertness, and fatigue; brief flaring while crying is not equivalent to persistent distress.

Assessment Method

Observe nostril movement during inspiration.

Positive Finding

The nares widen repeatedly with breathing.

Mechanism

Dilating the nasal valves reduces upper-airway resistance during high ventilatory demand.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Nasal flaring finding raises concern for increased work of breathing, especially in infants because dilating the nasal valves reduces upper-airway resistance during high ventilatory demand. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Nasal flaring does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Nasal flaring to focus evaluation of increased work of breathing, especially in infants and the reviewed alternatives: nasal obstruction, normal crying, fever tachypnea. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Observe nostril movement during inspiration. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Nasal flaring as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Crying and agitation can cause transient flaring. Therefore, Nasal flaring cannot confirm or exclude increased work of breathing, especially in infants by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • nasal obstruction

  • normal crying

  • fever tachypnea

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as nasal obstruction, normal crying, fever tachypnea.

False Negative Situations

Nasal flaring may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased work of breathing, especially in infants.
