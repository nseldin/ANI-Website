Definition

Unterberger stepping test is a bedside vestibular maneuver. It is a clue to possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone. A positive finding means the body rotates substantially to one side.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Unterberger stepping test means the body rotates substantially to one side.

Why It Matters

A positive Unterberger stepping test response can signal possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone. Asymmetric vestibular tone may bias repeated stepping toward the weaker side. Unterberger stepping test cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone

Assessment Method

With close guarding, ask the patient to march in place with eyes closed for a defined number of steps.

Positive Finding

The body rotates substantially to one side.

Mechanism

Asymmetric vestibular tone may bias repeated stepping toward the weaker side.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Unterberger stepping test response raises concern for possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone because asymmetric vestibular tone may bias repeated stepping toward the weaker side. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Unterberger stepping test does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Unterberger stepping test to focus evaluation of possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone and the reviewed alternatives: normal variation, leg-length difference, cerebellar disease. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: With close guarding, ask the patient to march in place with eyes closed for a defined number of steps. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Unterberger stepping test as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Test-retest reliability and diagnostic specificity are poor. Therefore, Unterberger stepping test cannot confirm or exclude possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • normal variation

  • leg-length difference

  • cerebellar disease

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from normal variation, leg-length difference, cerebellar disease, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Unterberger stepping test may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Limited and unreliable adjunct; should not localize vestibular disease alone. Unterberger stepping test remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone. Apply the reviewed limitation for Unterberger stepping test: Test-retest reliability and diagnostic specificity are poor.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Unterberger stepping test, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: With close guarding, ask the patient to march in place with eyes closed for a defined number of steps. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Prevent falls and do not infer lesion side from modest rotation.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact Unterberger stepping test observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: The body rotates substantially to one side. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: With close guarding, ask the patient to march in place with eyes closed for a defined number of steps. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, possible asymmetric vestibulospinal tone, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."
