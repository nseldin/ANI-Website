Plain Language

Tobacco use disorder is a treatable illness in which nicotine-containing tobacco use becomes hard to control and continues despite harm or strong risk. It can involve cigarettes, cigars, pipes, smokeless tobacco, or other nicotine products; it is not a lack of willpower.

Why It Matters

Tobacco use is a major preventable cause of cancer, heart and blood-vessel disease, lung disease, pregnancy complications, and early death. Counseling plus medication can more than double the chance of quitting, and withdrawal can be managed without confusing it with a dangerous seizure or delirium syndrome.

Definition

Tobacco use disorder is a problematic pattern of tobacco or nicotine use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR diagnosis requires at least 2 qualifying features within 12 months, with severity based on the number present. Withdrawal, nicotine poisoning, and current tobacco exposure are separate assessments; one cigarette, one laboratory result, or patient interest in quitting does not establish the diagnosis.

Pathology

There is no single laboratory test that diagnoses tobacco use disorder. Nicotine rapidly reinforces use and repeated exposure can produce tolerance, craving, and withdrawal, while smoke and product toxicants drive cancer, vascular injury, chronic lung disease, poor wound healing, and pregnancy and newborn harms. Risk varies by product, dose, duration, combustion, co-use, and patient disease.

Pathophysiology

  • Nicotine activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and reward pathways. Repeated exposure produces neuroadaptation, so falling nicotine levels trigger craving and withdrawal that can reinforce continued use.

  • Withdrawal commonly causes irritability or anger, anxiety, restlessness, trouble concentrating, increased appetite, insomnia, depressed mood, and strong craving. It is uncomfortable and relapse-prone but is not usually a seizure-delirium syndrome.

  • Acute nicotine toxicity is poisoning rather than a DSM tobacco-intoxication diagnosis. Nausea, vomiting, salivation, dizziness, sweating, abdominal pain, blood-pressure and heart-rate changes, confusion, weakness, seizure, or respiratory failure can occur after concentrated exposure, especially liquid nicotine ingestion.

Etiology

Dependence reflects nicotine pharmacology, product design and rapid delivery, early exposure, genetics, stress and trauma, mood and attention symptoms, social environment, targeted marketing, housing and occupational context, and access to treatment. Continued use despite knowing the risk is a feature of dependence, not proof that the patient does not care about health.

Risk Factors

  • Early initiation, frequent use, high-delivery products, dual or multiple nicotine products, prior unsuccessful quit attempts, and living or working with tobacco exposure

  • Depression, anxiety, ADHD, psychosis, trauma, other substance use, chronic stress, and limited behavioral or medication access

  • Pregnancy, cardiovascular or lung disease, diabetes, cancer, planned surgery, poor wound healing, and secondhand-smoke exposure in the home

  • Adolescent product appeal, flavored products, social use, and underestimation of nicotine in e-cigarettes or pouches

Signs Symptoms

  • Using more or longer than intended, unsuccessful quit attempts, craving, substantial time using, role or relationship harm, risky use, continued use despite disease, tolerance, and withdrawal

  • Smoking soon after waking, waking to use, difficulty avoiding prohibited settings, using while acutely ill, and repeated return to use despite a strong intention to stop

  • Withdrawal with craving, irritability, anxiety, restlessness, poor concentration, sleep disturbance, appetite increase, or low mood after reduction or cessation

  • Product-related cough, wheeze, dyspnea, chest symptoms, oral lesions, vascular symptoms, delayed healing, or pregnancy and newborn concerns require medical assessment but do not determine diagnostic severity alone

Diagnostics

  • Ask about every combustible, smokeless, heated, vaping, pouch, and medicinal nicotine product; frequency, time to first use, amount, triggers, prior quit attempts, withdrawal, current motivation, household exposure, pregnancy, comorbidity, and the DSM-5-TR features over the same 12 months.

  • Confirm at least 2 qualifying features with impairment or distress and record severity from the criterion count. Product quantity and nicotine concentration can support planning but do not replace criteria.

  • Assess withdrawal separately and screen for depression, suicide risk, alcohol or other drug use, cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms, oral disease, and medication interactions.

  • Carbon monoxide or cotinine testing may document recent exposure for a defined purpose but cannot diagnose the disorder by itself; values differ by product, timing, secondhand exposure, and assay.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses tobacco use disorder. Use carbon monoxide, cotinine, lung function, imaging, cardiovascular testing, cancer screening, pregnancy testing, or other studies only for a supported exposure or disease question.

  • Before bupropion, review seizure and eating-disorder history, abrupt alcohol or sedative discontinuation, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, blood pressure, pregnancy, and interacting medicines.

  • Before varenicline, review renal function, pregnancy or lactation, prior hypersensitivity or serious skin reaction, seizure history, and current neuropsychiatric symptoms; adjust and monitor according to the current label.

Assessment

Ask permission to discuss tobacco, determine current product and nicotine exposure, withdrawal, prior treatment response, readiness and confidence, and the patient’s preferred goal. Identify urgent chest pain, severe dyspnea, stroke symptoms, poisoning, suicidality, or pregnancy concerns first; then choose counseling and medication based on dependence pattern, contraindications, comorbidity, cost, access, and patient preference.

Differential

Differentiate tobacco withdrawal from anxiety, depression, sleep disorder, stimulant or caffeine change, medication effects, and withdrawal from another substance. Differentiate acute nicotine poisoning from gastroenteritis, cholinergic poisoning, hypoglycemia, cardiac disease, and co-ingestion. Nicotine exposure and a tobacco-related disease can exist without meeting use-disorder criteria, while the disorder can exist before obvious organ disease.

Treatments

  • Combine behavioral counseling with an FDA-approved cessation medication when acceptable and safe. Representative options are varenicline, bupropion sustained release, and nicotine replacement therapy such as a patch plus gum or lozenge.

  • Use brief clinician advice, a shared quit or reduction plan, coping and trigger skills, proactive follow-up, and repeated treatment after lapses. Quitlines, text or digital supports, groups, and individual counseling can extend care.

  • Combination nicotine replacement—a long-acting patch plus a short-acting form—can improve success for many adults. Match product strength and timing to current nicotine exposure, withdrawal, preferences, and label instructions rather than using one universal regimen.

  • Treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, ADHD, psychosis, alcohol or drug use, and tobacco-related disease. A lapse should prompt reassessment of adherence, withdrawal, dose, triggers, and support rather than discharge from treatment.

Contraindications

  • Bupropion is contraindicated in seizure disorder, current or prior anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa, and abrupt discontinuation of alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, or antiepileptic drugs; review monoamine oxidase inhibitor timing and label-specific risks.

  • Varenicline is contraindicated after a serious hypersensitivity or skin reaction to varenicline; severe renal impairment requires dose adjustment and neuropsychiatric or seizure symptoms require prompt review.

  • Do not recommend e-cigarettes as an FDA-approved cessation medication; evidence and product content vary, and dual use can maintain nicotine dependence and toxic exposure.

  • Do not withhold counseling because a patient is not ready for an immediate quit date, and do not assume cold-turkey cessation is the only valid approach.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess mood and suicide risk before and during a quit attempt, especially in patients with depression or other psychiatric illness; withdrawal symptoms and medication effects need clinical context.

  • Keep nicotine liquids, gum, lozenges, patches, cigarettes, and pouches locked away from children and pets. For ingestion or substantial skin exposure, contact Poison Help or emergency services and do not induce vomiting.

  • Review all nicotine sources to prevent unintended excess during nicotine replacement, and teach correct patch, gum, and lozenge use and safe disposal.

  • Stopping smoking can change metabolism of some medicines because smoke—not nicotine alone—induces enzymes; review drugs with narrow therapeutic ranges and monitor clinically.

Nursing Priorities

  • Record all products, frequency, time to first use, last use, withdrawal, prior medication response, readiness, pregnancy, comorbidity, current medicines, and household secondhand exposure.

  • Offer brief clear advice, help choose counseling and medication, teach correct use and adverse effects, and arrange follow-up close enough to adjust the plan during early withdrawal.

  • Monitor craving, withdrawal, blood pressure when relevant, mood and suicide risk, sleep, medication adherence, renal considerations, adverse effects, and tobacco-related symptoms.

  • For suspected nicotine poisoning, remove ongoing exposure safely, assess airway, breathing, circulation, neurologic and cardiac status, contact Poison Help or emergency services, and preserve product information.

Red Flags

  • Chest pain, acute dyspnea, hemoptysis, stroke symptoms, syncope, dangerous arrhythmia, or another possible tobacco-related emergency

  • Nicotine-liquid ingestion, seizure, severe weakness, confusion, persistent vomiting, breathing difficulty, or major pediatric exposure

  • Suicidal intent, severe mood change, psychosis, or inability to remain safe during treatment or withdrawal

  • Pregnancy with acute poisoning, severe respiratory or cardiac symptoms, or inability to access prenatal and cessation support

Complications

  • Lung, oral, and other cancers; coronary, cerebrovascular, and peripheral vascular disease; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; and early death

  • Pregnancy and newborn complications, impaired fertility, poor wound and bone healing, infection risk, dental disease, and secondhand-smoke harm

  • Withdrawal distress, repeated relapse, financial burden, stigma, and reduced adherence when treatment is not offered

  • Nicotine poisoning from concentrated products, accidental child exposure, and burns or lung injury associated with some devices or unregulated products

Prognosis

Stopping tobacco improves health at any age and benefits patients who already have tobacco-related disease. Many people need repeated quit attempts and medication adjustments; a lapse does not erase progress. Continued follow-up improves the chance of sustained remission and helps detect changing medication levels or disease symptoms after smoking stops.

Prevention

  • Prevent youth initiation, reduce secondhand exposure, keep homes and vehicles smoke-free, and store all nicotine products securely.

  • Screen routinely with respectful product-specific questions and offer treatment at every clinical contact rather than waiting for advanced disease.

  • Use evidence-based cessation support before surgery, during pregnancy, and in cardiovascular, cancer, pulmonary, behavioral-health, and substance-use care.

Patient Education

  • Nicotine dependence is treatable. Counseling plus medication works better than either alone for many adults.

  • A nicotine patch can provide steady control while gum or a lozenge treats sudden cravings; use each exactly as taught and keep it away from children and pets.

  • Call for help if you develop a seizure, severe rash, major mood change, suicidal thoughts, chest pain, or breathing trouble.

  • A lapse is not failure. Contact the care team so the dose, trigger plan, and support can be adjusted.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy: provide behavioral counseling first and individualize medication decisions after discussing known harms of tobacco and the evidence and uncertainty for each cessation medicine; coordinate prenatal care.

  • Adolescents need confidential, developmentally appropriate assessment of cigarettes, vaping, pouches, peers, mental health, school, and family exposure; medication decisions require clinician oversight.

  • Patients with serious mental illness or another substance use disorder benefit from the same active cessation treatment, with closer mood, interaction, and relapse monitoring rather than therapeutic pessimism.

  • Renal impairment, seizure risk, eating-disorder history, cardiovascular disease, and polypharmacy change medication selection and monitoring.

Nclex Traps

  • Tobacco withdrawal causes craving and mood, sleep, appetite, and concentration symptoms but is not usually a seizure-delirium withdrawal.

  • Counseling plus medication is more effective than simply advising willpower.

  • Bupropion is contraindicated with seizure disorder and current or prior anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa.

  • E-cigarettes are not FDA-approved cessation medicines, and legal availability does not establish safety.
