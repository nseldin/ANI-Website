Definition

Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis develops when immune complexes after nephritogenic group A streptococcal infection activate complement and inflame glomeruli. The characteristic pattern is cola-colored urine, edema, hypertension, oliguria, red-cell casts, low complement, and kidney injury one to several weeks after throat or skin infection. Confirm or stage it with urinalysis with microscopy, urine protein, creatinine, electrolytes, and complement; distinguish it from IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, C3 glomerulopathy, hemolytic-uremic syndrome, and active infection-related nephritis by timing, complement, serology, and systemic findings. The priority is to control sodium, fluid, blood pressure, potassium, and pulmonary edema because hypertensive encephalopathy can complicate Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. can follow if the process advances.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • urinalysis with microscopy, urine protein, creatinine, electrolytes, and complement

  • streptococcal antibody evidence chosen for prior throat or skin infection

  • kidney biopsy only for atypical complement course, rapid progression, or diagnostic uncertainty

  • Distinguish Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis from IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, C3 glomerulopathy, hemolytic-uremic syndrome, and active infection-related nephritis by timing, complement, serology, and systemic findings.

Labs

  • creatinine and BUN rise when filtration worsens

  • potassium may rise with kidney failure

  • urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is concerning

  • WBC/nitrites/leukocyte esterase support UTI

Treatments

  • control sodium, fluid, blood pressure, potassium, and pulmonary edema

  • treat any still-active streptococcal infection to limit transmission, not to instantly reverse immune injury

  • use dialysis for refractory overload, potassium, acid, or uremic complications

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for seizure, severe hypertension, pulmonary edema, hyperkalemia, oliguria, or rapidly rising creatinine because critical blood-pressure control or dialysis may be required.

  • Ask about recent throat or skin infection and assess edema, cola-colored urine, blood pressure, and urine output because immune glomerular injury often appears after the infection improves.

  • Obtain ordered urinalysis, creatinine, potassium, complement, streptococcal testing, and daily weight because severity reflects kidney effects rather than throat symptoms.

  • Administer prescribed antibiotic therapy for remaining streptococcal infection while explaining that supportive kidney care treats the nephritis because antibiotics do not instantly remove deposited immune injury.

  • Follow sodium and fluid limits and give ordered diuretic or antihypertensive therapy because volume retention can cause pulmonary edema and hypertensive encephalopathy.

Complications

  • hypertensive encephalopathy can complicate Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

  • pulmonary edema can complicate Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

  • hyperkalemia can complicate Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

  • acute kidney injury can complicate Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

  • rarely rapidly progressive disease

Red Flags

  • Seizure or severe hypertensive neurologic symptoms

  • Pulmonary edema or worsening respiratory distress

  • Oliguria or rapidly rising creatinine

  • Hyperkalemia with rhythm change

Patient Education

  • Teach families that dark urine and swelling can begin after the original sore throat or skin infection seems resolved.

  • Review medication completion, daily weight and blood-pressure follow-up, and urgent signs such as breathlessness, severe headache, seizure, or reduced urine.

Nclex Traps

  • The renal syndrome follows infection after a latent interval; simultaneous hematuria suggests another pattern

  • Antibiotics do not remove already formed immune complexes
