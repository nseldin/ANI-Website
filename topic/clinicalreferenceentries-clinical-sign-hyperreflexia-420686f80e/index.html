Definition

Hyperreflexia means muscle-stretch reflexes are unusually brisk, spread beyond the expected muscle, or repeat after a tendon is tapped. It is a localization clue, not a diagnosis by itself.

Plain Language

A tendon tap causes a stronger, wider, or repeating jerk than expected.

Why It Matters

Hyperreflexia can indicate an upper motor neuron or spinal cord problem, especially when asymmetric or accompanied by weakness, increased tone, Babinski response, clonus, or bowel or bladder change. Symmetric brisk reflexes can be normal, so the whole pattern matters.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

With the patient relaxed and positioned symmetrically, use consistent tendon taps, compare the same reflex on both sides and above and below a suspected level, grade the response, and assess spread, repetition or clonus, tone, strength, gait, and plantar responses.

Positive Finding

A tendon tap produces an abnormally brisk, spreading, or repeating response relative to the patient's other reflexes and clinical context; sustained clonus or clear asymmetry is especially concerning.

Mechanism

Corticospinal and other descending pathways normally modulate spinal reflex arcs; reduced inhibition allows a larger or repetitive muscle response to the same stretch.

Evidence Limitations

Reflex grading is subjective and affected by relaxation, strike force, reinforcement, anxiety, age, medication, and examiner technique; a 3-plus response is not automatically pathologic. Therefore, Hyperreflexia cannot confirm or exclude increased reflex activity from loss of descending inhibitory control in an upper motor neuron or spinal cord lesion, with physiologic briskness, anxiety, endocrine, medication, toxic, and metabolic contributors by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • constitutional brisk reflexes

  • anxiety

  • thyrotoxicosis

  • serotonin toxicity

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from constitutional brisk reflexes, anxiety, thyrotoxicosis, serotonin toxicity, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Hyperreflexia may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current localization sign; an isolated symmetric brisk response can be normal, while pattern, asymmetry, clonus, weakness, tone, and other upper motor neuron findings determine significance. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Hyperreflexia, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: With the patient relaxed and positioned symmetrically, use consistent tendon taps, compare the same reflex on both sides and above and below a suspected level, grade the response, and assess spread, repetition or clonus, tone, strength, gait, and plantar responses. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not diagnose cord disease from one brisk reflex; rapidly new hyperreflexia with weakness, sensory level, bowel or bladder change, severe back or neck pain, or toxic autonomic findings requires urgent evaluation.
