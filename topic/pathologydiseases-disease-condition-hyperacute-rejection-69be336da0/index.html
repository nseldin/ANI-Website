Definition

Hyperacute rejection develops when preformed recipient antibodies bind graft endothelium within minutes to hours, activating complement, thrombosis, and immediate ischemic graft failure. The characteristic pattern is abrupt graft dysfunction almost immediately after reperfusion with poor perfusion, thrombosis, swelling, anuria, or organ-specific failure. Confirm or stage it with urgent graft-flow imaging and function tests; distinguish it from technical thrombosis, kinking, ischemia-reperfusion injury, severe acute rejection, and shock by timing, imaging, antibody, and pathology. The priority is to activate the transplant and surgical team immediately because graft necrosis and loss can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Fever or hypothermia, chills, source pain/redness/drainage, cough, dysuria, diarrhea, rash, mucosal lesions, lymph nodes, mental-status change, hypotension, tachypnea, or immunosuppression cues reveal infection risk.

  • Escalate sepsis signs, neutropenic fever, rapidly spreading infection, airway swelling, meningitis signs, or shock.

Diagnostics

  • urgent graft-flow imaging and function tests

  • crossmatch, donor-specific antibody, complement, and tissue evaluation

  • rapid exclusion of surgical vascular obstruction while transplant teams mobilize

  • Distinguish Hyperacute rejection from technical thrombosis, kinking, ischemia-reperfusion injury, severe acute rejection, and shock by timing, imaging, antibody, and pathology.

Labs

  • WBC may rise or fall

  • lactate may rise with sepsis

  • CRP/ESR may rise with inflammation

  • organ labs can worsen in severe infection

Treatments

  • activate the transplant and surgical team immediately

  • support organ function and circulation

  • remove a nonviable graft or use specialized antibody-directed rescue only when appropriate

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for absent graft perfusion, anuria, severe acidosis, rising lactate, refractory hypotension, or new coagulopathy because hyperacute rejection can become a surgical emergency.

  • Notify the transplant team immediately when a new graft loses function within minutes to hours because antibody-mediated vascular injury can rapidly destroy the organ.

  • Monitor graft-specific perfusion and output, including urine for a kidney graft or hemodynamics for a heart graft, to detect abrupt thrombosis and ischemia.

  • Trend blood pressure, lactate, creatinine, liver tests, oxygenation, coagulation, and urine output according to the transplanted organ because systemic instability may accompany graft failure.

  • Maintain vascular access and prepare imaging, biopsy, plasmapheresis, blood products, or urgent graft removal as directed because definitive rescue is transplant-team dependent.

Complications

  • graft necrosis and loss

  • thrombosis can complicate Hyperacute rejection.

  • systemic inflammation can complicate Hyperacute rejection.

  • bleeding can complicate Hyperacute rejection.

  • shock can complicate Hyperacute rejection.

  • urgent return to organ support

Red Flags

  • Abrupt absence of graft perfusion or function

  • Anuria or rapidly worsening organ-specific laboratory findings

  • Severe acidosis, rising lactate, or refractory hypotension

  • New thrombosis or disseminated coagulation findings

Patient Education

  • Explain that hyperacute rejection is an immediate immune attack on graft blood vessels and is not caused by something the patient did after surgery.

  • Review the transplant team plan, possible procedures, and emotional support needs using clear, direct language.

Nclex Traps

  • Hyperacute rejection is usually immediate, not a gradual late creatinine rise

  • Do not assume absent urine is rejection until surgical vascular obstruction is checked
