Used To Treat

Prevention of Plasmodium falciparum malaria and treatment of acute uncomplicated malaria according to weight and geographic resistance. It is not adequate for severe malaria, relapsing hypnozoites, or patients who cannot reliably absorb oral drug.

Mechanism

Atovaquone binds the ubiquinol oxidation site of Plasmodium cytochrome bc1 complex III, blocking electron transfer, dissipating mitochondrial membrane potential, and impairing regeneration of ubiquinone needed by dihydroorotate dehydrogenase for pyrimidine synthesis. Proguanil is metabolized by CYP2C19 to cycloguanil, a selective parasite dihydrofolate-reductase inhibitor that reduces tetrahydrofolate and nucleotide synthesis. The parent proguanil appears to lower the threshold for mitochondrial depolarization, creating synergy beyond cycloguanil alone. Single cytochrome-b mutations can rapidly produce atovaquone resistance, which is why atovaquone should not be used alone for malaria. Fat and bile improve dissolution and absorption; low exposure permits parasite survival and resistance.

Contraindications

  • Hypersensitivity and severe renal impairment for prophylaxis; follow exact label restrictions for treatment.

  • Do not use alone for P. vivax/P. ovale radical cure, severe malaria, or when oral absorption is unreliable.

Nursing Essentials

  • Give with food or a milky drink at the same time each day. Ask directly about vomiting and diarrhea because a swallowed dose is not necessarily an absorbed dose.

  • For prophylaxis, start before exposure, take daily during travel, and continue after leaving for the exact interval; mosquito avoidance is still required.

Interactions

  • Rifampin or rifabutin markedly lowers atovaquone exposure; avoid the combination. Tetracycline and metoclopramide can also reduce concentrations.

  • Warfarin effect may change with proguanil; CYP2C19 phenotype or inhibitors alter cycloguanil formation, although parent-proguanil synergy remains.

Key Labs

  • Pregnancy test when relevant, renal and liver function, CBC if prolonged or high-risk, parasite smear and density for treatment

  • Food intake, vomiting/diarrhea, travel dates, adherence, fever after travel, treatment response, and species-directed radical-cure need

Nclex Traps

  • Atovaquone-proguanil prevents and treats susceptible malaria but does not eradicate vivax/ovale hypnozoites.

  • Food is pharmacologic: fasting or vomiting can lower atovaquone exposure enough to cause failure.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not use for severe or complicated malaria because oral absorption and evidence are inadequate. Avoid prophylaxis in severe renal impairment and use treatment cautiously according to labeling because both exposure and outcomes are affected. Rare severe hepatitis, hepatic failure, anaphylaxis, and severe skin reactions occur. If vomiting happens within the label interval, repeat the dose; persistent vomiting requires an alternative because absorption cannot be trusted.

Description

Atovaquone-proguanil is a fixed antimalarial combination that blocks two linked parasite pathways: atovaquone inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome bc1 and collapses membrane potential, while cycloguanil formed from proguanil inhibits parasite dihydrofolate reductase and the parent proguanil amplifies mitochondrial collapse. The pairing limits the rapid resistance and incomplete activity seen when either pathway is attacked alone. The combination acts on blood stages and early liver stages, allowing short pre-travel and post-travel prophylaxis, but it does not eradicate dormant Plasmodium vivax or Plasmodium ovale hypnozoites. Atovaquone is extremely lipophilic, so food or a milky drink greatly improves absorption; vomiting, diarrhea, malabsorption, or fasting can turn correct tablets into inadequate exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, anorexia, cough, oral ulcers, and elevated transaminases

  • Severe hepatitis, pancytopenia in renal impairment, hypersensitivity, severe skin reaction, and treatment failure from poor absorption or resistance
