Used To Treat

Severe poisoning by a long-acting barbiturate, especially phenobarbital, with prolonged coma, respiratory failure, shock despite support, or persistent toxicity despite multiple-dose activated charcoal. It is not a routine treatment for therapeutic phenobarbital use or every short-acting sedative overdose.

Description

Long-acting barbiturates enhance inhibitory GABA-A signaling and at toxic concentrations directly depress brainstem respiratory drive, vascular tone, and myocardial performance. Severe phenobarbital poisoning can therefore produce prolonged coma, hypothermia, hypotension, aspiration, skin pressure injury, and days of mechanical ventilation. Phenobarbital has enough water solubility, circulating fraction, and limited distribution to be removed by modern hemodialysis despite protein binding. EXTRIP recommends ECTR for severe long-acting barbiturate poisoning when coma, ventilation, shock, or persistent toxicity makes endogenous clearance too slow. MDAC can further increase elimination if airway and bowel function are safe. The goal is to shorten dangerous organ-support exposure, not to awaken every sedated patient immediately.

Mechanism

Barbiturates prolong GABA-A chloride-channel opening and, at high concentrations, suppress excitatory signaling, producing dose-dependent CNS, respiratory, and cardiovascular depression. Intermittent HD removes unbound phenobarbital from plasma; protein-bound and tissue drug then re-equilibrate into the circulating compartment for further clearance. Phenobarbital's relatively small distribution volume and longer half-life make this clinically useful. MDAC adsorbs drug in the gut and captures drug that cycles from blood into the intestinal lumen, increasing nonrenal clearance. ECTR does not reverse receptor effects already present in the brain instantaneously, so ventilation, temperature management, pressure protection, and vasopressors continue until clinical recovery follows the falling body burden.

Administration Timing

  • Stabilize airway, ventilation, circulation, temperature, glucose, aspiration risk, and pressure areas; obtain serial long-acting-barbiturate concentrations and use MDAC when the airway and bowel are protected.

  • Consult ECTR early for prolonged ventilated coma, shock despite supportive measures, or persistent severe toxicity despite MDAC. Base the decision on the confirmed long-acting agent and clinical course rather than a generic positive barbiturate screen.

  • Use intermittent hemodialysis as the preferred modality when feasible and continue MDAC during treatment if safe. Repeat clinical and concentration assessment after treatment because delayed absorption and redistribution can produce rebound.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm the specific barbiturate and distinguish phenobarbital from short-acting agents and nonbarbiturate sedatives because urine immunoassay alone neither quantifies severity nor proves ECTR benefit.

  • Use continuous respiratory/capnography, ECG, blood pressure, temperature, neurologic, urine-output, skin, eye, and pressure-injury monitoring; deep coma can hide corneal injury, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis, and compartment pressure.

  • Protect the airway before charcoal and stop MDAC for vomiting, distention, ileus, or obstruction because depressed reflexes and slowed bowel motility can cause aspiration or charcoal impaction. During dialysis, monitor access, hemodynamics, temperature, electrolytes and removal of concurrently needed anticonvulsants because ECTR can shift physiology and clear therapeutic drugs along with phenobarbital.

  • Avoid repeated empiric stimulant or flumazenil use because flumazenil does not antagonize barbiturate effects and can provoke seizures in susceptible mixed overdoses, while stimulants add physiologic stress without removing the drug.

Key Labs

  • Serial phenobarbital or specific long-acting-barbiturate concentration, blood gas, glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, liver tests, CK, lactate, CBC, ECG and chest imaging when aspiration is suspected.

  • During and after ECTR, pair concentration decline with ventilator requirement, hemodynamics, temperature, pupils/reflexes, MDAC delivery, bowel function and rebound surveillance to detect persistent clinical toxicity or renewed absorption despite a falling initial concentration.

Adverse Effects

  • ECTR risks include hypotension, access bleeding/infection, hypothermia, electrolyte shifts and unintended medication clearance. MDAC adds aspiration and bowel-obstruction risk.

  • Prolonged coma itself causes aspiration pneumonia, pressure injury, rhabdomyolysis, corneal abrasion, venous thrombosis, and ventilator complications; ECTR may reduce exposure time but does not erase existing injury.

Contraindications

  • Do not use ECTR for ordinary phenobarbital therapy, a clinically improving mild overdose, or a nonspecific positive urine screen because procedural risk is not justified without severe poisoning and a urine screen cannot identify the agent or quantify toxicity. Confirm a severe long-acting exposure.

  • MDAC is contraindicated with an unprotected airway, ileus, obstruction, or perforation risk because charcoal can be aspirated or trapped in a nonfunctioning or injured bowel. Hemodynamic instability requires experienced modality selection and support because the ECTR approach may need modification rather than automatic abandonment.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Refractory shock, worsening acidosis, prolonged ventilator-dependent coma, rising concentration, or persistent toxicity despite MDAC requires urgent ECTR initiation or transfer.

  • Rebound sedation or concentration after ECTR requires renewed airway assessment, serial levels, evaluation for ongoing absorption, and possible additional MDAC or ECTR.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP recommendations apply to severe long-acting barbiturate poisoning and rely on low-certainty clinical evidence plus strong pharmacokinetic rationale. They should not be generalized to all sedative-hypnotics.

  • Urinary alkalinization can alter phenobarbital elimination but does not replace preferred ECTR in severe poisoning and adds fluid/electrolyte complexity.

Nclex Traps

  • There is no flumazenil-like antidote for barbiturates; airway and circulation come first.

  • ECTR is for severe long-acting poisoning such as phenobarbital rather than every sedative or positive urine screen because a nonspecific screen cannot establish a dialyzable agent or justify procedural risk.

  • Continue MDAC during ECTR when safe because the two methods clear different compartments.

Regulatory Status

EXTRIP consensus rescue, not an FDA indication. Agent confirmation, severity selection, MDAC, modality and stopping criteria require nephrology and toxicology guidance.

Boxed Warning
