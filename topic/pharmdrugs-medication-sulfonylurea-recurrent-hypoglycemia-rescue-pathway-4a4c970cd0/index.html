Used To Treat

Recurrent or anticipated hypoglycemia after sulfonylurea exposure. Dextrose immediately restores glucose; octreotide is an off-label rescue adjunct that suppresses ongoing insulin release and helps prevent the dextrose-insulin-rebound cycle.

Description

Sulfonylureas close ATP-sensitive potassium channels in pancreatic beta cells, causing depolarization, calcium entry, and insulin release even when blood glucose is already low. IV dextrose treats the immediate neuroglycopenia but also raises pancreatic glucose exposure and can provoke another insulin surge while the secretagogue remains active. Octreotide acts upstream by inhibiting beta-cell insulin secretion, so it is often used after documented or recurrent sulfonylurea hypoglycemia rather than escalating dextrose alone. One normal glucose is not clearance: long-acting agents, kidney disease, large exposure, and extended-release products can cause delayed or recurrent hypoglycemia after apparent recovery.

Mechanism

Glucose supplies immediate substrate for brain ATP production and reverses the acute deficit, but it does not remove the sulfonylurea. Octreotide is a somatostatin analog that activates inhibitory receptors, reduces intracellular cyclic signaling and calcium-dependent hormone release, and thereby suppresses inappropriate insulin secretion. Complex carbohydrates provide slower ongoing substrate once the patient can eat safely. This layered strategy explains why rescue uses dextrose for the low value, octreotide for the driver, food for sustained substrate, and observation for recurrence.

Administration Timing

  • For seizure, unconsciousness, or inability to swallow, call 911 and give protocol-directed IV dextrose; use labeled glucagon only when vascular access is unavailable and recognize that it is temporary and can also stimulate insulin release. Never force oral sugar into an unprotected airway.

  • After confirmed or recurrent sulfonylurea hypoglycemia, consult Poison Help and use octreotide under the local toxicology protocol. Exact dose, route, repeat interval, and duration vary by age, product, recurrence, and protocol; its poisoning use is not the FDA-labeled octreotide indication.

  • Feed complex carbohydrates when awake and safe to swallow, monitor glucose frequently through treatment and for a protocol-defined interval after the last dextrose and octreotide, and extend observation for long-acting products, children, kidney disease, or recurrent lows.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact diabetes drug because metformin, insulin, meglitinides, sulfonylureas, and combination tablets create different hazards. Record formulation, amount, time, co-ingestants, meals, kidney and liver function, and measured weight.

  • At the bedside, trend glucose frequently during initial rescue and after every therapy change. Use a reliable line and independently verify concentrated dextrose to prevent extravasation, hyperglycemia, and medication error; trend potassium, magnesium, phosphate, and fluid balance during prolonged high-carbohydrate support.

  • Do not discharge solely because the patient ate and one glucose is normal. Document a recurrence-free observation period after the final glucose-raising and insulin-suppressing intervention.

Key Labs

  • Confirm serial point-of-care glucose with laboratory testing when readings conflict with the clinical picture because poor perfusion or meter limitations can misstate the true glucose; assess electrolytes, creatinine, hepatic function, and ECG as indicated to detect clearance problems, metabolic shifts, or dysrhythmias that change monitoring and rescue. Insulin, C-peptide, and sulfonylurea screens can clarify an unexplained case but should not delay treatment because neuroglycopenia can progress while confirmatory testing is pending.

  • A high insulin with preserved C-peptide supports endogenous or secretagogue-driven insulin release, whereas exogenous insulin patterns differ; assay timing and analog detection limitations require endocrine or toxicology interpretation.

Adverse Effects

  • Untreated hypoglycemia causes adrenergic symptoms, confusion, focal deficits, seizure, coma, brain injury, dysrhythmia, and death.

  • Dextrose can cause hyperglycemia, osmotic diuresis, electrolyte shifts, phlebitis, and tissue injury after extravasation. Octreotide can cause nausea, abdominal pain, bradycardia, conduction changes, glucose disturbance, gallbladder effects, and injection reactions.

Contraindications

  • Do not give oral carbohydrate to a patient who cannot protect the airway. Do not treat recurrent sulfonylurea toxicity with repeated dextrose alone when ongoing insulin secretion is evident and octreotide is available under expert care.

  • Octreotide hypersensitivity is a labeled contraindication; significant bradycardia or conduction disease, pregnancy, and young age require individualized specialist risk-benefit assessment rather than an unsupervised template.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Any recurrent low glucose, seizure, coma, dysrhythmia, large pediatric ingestion, need for continuous dextrose, or inability to maintain nutrition requires monitored admission and toxicology. Persistent hypoglycemia after octreotide should trigger diagnosis review for insulin, mixed agents, sepsis, liver failure, adrenal disease, or another cause.

Interactions

  • Beta blockers can blunt tremor and tachycardia, leaving neuroglycopenia as the first obvious sign. Alcohol, insulin, meglitinides, poor intake, and kidney or liver dysfunction deepen or prolong hypoglycemia.

  • Glucagon mobilizes hepatic glycogen but may fail after prolonged fasting or liver disease and may stimulate further insulin release; it is a bridge, not definitive sulfonylurea control.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A single adult tablet can cause prolonged hypoglycemia; every exploratory ingestion needs poison-center assessment and weight-based rescue.

  • Population

    Older adults or kidney disease

    Note

    Reduced clearance, poor intake, polypharmacy, and blunted warning symptoms increase recurrence and lengthen observation.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal hypoglycemia threatens both patients; rescue should not be delayed, while octreotide use remains specialist-directed because poisoning-specific pregnancy evidence is limited.

Evidence Limitations

  • Octreotide is not FDA-approved specifically for sulfonylurea poisoning. Evidence supports reduced recurrent hypoglycemia but comes largely from limited trials, observational studies, case series, and a 2025 systematic review; exact regimens and observation windows remain protocol dependent.

Nclex Traps

  • Dextrose fixes the number but can intensify sulfonylurea-driven insulin release.

  • Octreotide suppresses insulin secretion; it does not replace the first rescue glucose in symptomatic hypoglycemia.

  • One normal glucose after treatment does not rule out recurrence.

Regulatory Status

Dextrose and glucagon products have labeled hypoglycemia uses. Octreotide use to prevent recurrent sulfonylurea hypoglycemia is off-label and poison-center or toxicologist directed; no single regimen is appropriate for all products and populations.

Boxed Warning

RECURRENT-HYPOGLYCEMIA WARNING: seizure, unconsciousness, or inability to swallow requires 911 and parenteral rescue; never force food or liquid into the mouth. After sulfonylurea exposure, glucose can fall again after initial correction, so call Poison Help, suppress ongoing insulin release when indicated, and observe after the last treatment.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
