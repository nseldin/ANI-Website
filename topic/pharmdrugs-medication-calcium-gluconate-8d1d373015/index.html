Used To Treat

Acute symptomatic hypocalcemia and selected emergency states, including OFF-LABEL treatment of magnesium toxicity, hyperkalemic cardiac membrane instability, and calcium-channel-blocker poisoning. It is less concentrated and generally less caustic than calcium chloride but can still cause severe extravasation injury.

Mechanism

After dissociation, ionized calcium enters the same extracellular calcium pool regardless of whether it came from gluconate or chloride. It supports depolarization, cardiac excitation-contraction coupling, vascular smooth-muscle tone, and calcium-dependent acetylcholine release. A larger extracellular gradient can drive more calcium through L-type channels that are not completely blocked, partially overcoming CCB toxicity. At the neuromuscular junction, calcium counterbalances magnesium-mediated reduction in transmitter release. During hyperkalemia it raises the threshold potential relative to the resting membrane potential and reduces electrical instability. None of these mechanisms removes the offending toxin or potassium, which explains the need for simultaneous definitive treatment.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): Do not infuse through an infiltrated line or combine directly with bicarbonate, phosphate, or incompatible ceftriaxone solutions because precipitation can occur. Neonatal ceftriaxone restrictions are especially strict. Persistent cardiogenic shock after protocol calcium requires high-dose insulin, vasopressors, and early advanced mechanical-support consultation. Worsening magnesium toxicity with respiratory failure requires ventilation and possible dialysis when renal clearance is inadequate.

Contraindications

  • Do not infuse through an infiltrated line or combine directly with bicarbonate, phosphate, or incompatible ceftriaxone solutions because precipitation can occur. Neonatal ceftriaxone restrictions are especially strict.

  • Hypercalcemia is a contraindication to routine replacement. Digoxin toxicity requires expert monitoring and risk-benefit judgment rather than an automatic calcium bolus.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm that the request is for systemic calcium gluconate rather than hydrofluoric-acid skin/eye treatment. HF exposure has route-specific decontamination and calcium protocols and should open the dedicated HF card.

  • Use a patent large peripheral or central vein, monitor the site continuously, and stop for burning, swelling, erythema, blanching, or resistance because calcium gluconate extravasation can still cause delayed necrosis.

  • Maintain ECG, blood pressure, respiratory status, reflexes, urine output, and serial ionized calcium. In magnesium toxicity, return of reflexes and respiratory effort reflects physiologic antagonism, but magnesium clearance may still require renal support.

  • For neonatal patients, verify ceftriaxone exposure and line compatibility because the active label prohibits concurrent ceftriaxone-calcium administration in neonates and includes strict separation rules for older patients.

Interactions

  • Calcium precipitates with bicarbonate and phosphate and can form dangerous ceftriaxone-calcium precipitates under label-defined conditions. Separate lines and flush as directed.

Key Labs

  • Ionized calcium, total calcium and albumin, magnesium, potassium, phosphorus, creatinine, glucose, blood gas, lactate, and toxin-specific measurements as appropriate. A total calcium alone can misrepresent the active ion in critical illness.

  • Continuous ECG and frequent hemodynamics, urine output, deep-tendon reflexes in magnesium toxicity, and serial IV-site/neurovascular checks.

Nclex Traps

  • Calcium gluconate contains less elemental calcium per milliliter than calcium chloride; compare elemental dose, not ampules.

  • Peripheral calcium gluconate is less caustic, not non-caustic. Inspect the site throughout administration.

  • For magnesium toxicity, stop magnesium and support ventilation while calcium antagonizes its effects; calcium does not remove magnesium.

Description

Calcium gluconate is an injectable calcium salt that raises extracellular ionized calcium to stabilize excitable myocardium and support neuromuscular and cardiac function in hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia, magnesium toxicity, and selected poisonings. A typical 10 percent solution supplies approximately 9.3 mg of elemental calcium per milliliter, about one-third the elemental-calcium concentration in the same volume of 10 percent calcium chloride. That lower concentration often makes it preferable through a peripheral vein, although 'safer' does not mean harmless: tissue necrosis and calcinosis remain possible. In magnesium toxicity, calcium restores neuromuscular and cardiac function by opposing magnesium's physiologic effects. In hyperkalemia it stabilizes excitable myocardium without changing the potassium concentration. In calcium-channel-blocker poisoning it may temporarily improve inotropy and vascular tone while high-dose insulin and vasopressors treat the broader shock state.

Administration Timing

  • Use a protocol that converts the ordered dose to elemental calcium and accounts for the product concentration. Do not substitute an equal volume of calcium gluconate for calcium chloride or vice versa.

  • For symptomatic magnesium toxicity or hyperkalemic ECG change, administer while ventilation, magnesium-source control, potassium-shifting/removal therapy, and other resuscitation proceed. For CCB poisoning, pair calcium with high-dose insulin and vasopressor assessment rather than waiting for calcium failure.

  • Recheck ECG, perfusion, symptoms, and ionized calcium after treatment. Repeated doses or an infusion require a reliable line and serial calcium because response may be transient while hypercalcemia accumulates.

Adverse Effects

  • Extravasation can cause calcinosis cutis, necrosis, ulceration, and secondary infection. Rapid administration can cause vasodilation, hypotension, bradycardia, dysrhythmia, syncope, or cardiac arrest.

  • Hypercalcemia may cause nausea, weakness, confusion, constipation, polyuria, kidney injury, and dysrhythmia. Repeated empirical doses without ionized-calcium monitoring increase this risk.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent cardiogenic shock after protocol calcium requires high-dose insulin, vasopressors, and early advanced mechanical-support consultation. Worsening magnesium toxicity with respiratory failure requires ventilation and possible dialysis when renal clearance is inadequate.

  • Suspected extravasation requires immediate stop, aspiration through existing access when the local protocol directs, limb elevation, pharmacy/provider notification, and specialty follow-up because injury can declare itself over days.

Evidence Limitations

  • Off-label toxicology evidence is strongest as physiologic and consensus support rather than randomized outcome trials. AHA 2025 considers calcium reasonable for severe CCB poisoning but not a replacement for high-dose insulin and vasopressors.

  • Topical, nebulized, intra-arterial, or infiltrated calcium for hydrofluoric acid is exposure-specific and should not be inferred from this systemic-injection card.

Regulatory Status

FDA-labeled for acute symptomatic hypocalcemia in indicated patients. Toxicologic rescue uses are frequently OFF-LABEL and should follow AHA, poison-center, and institutional guidance.
