Definition

Autoimmune hepatitis is an immune-mediated hepatocellular inflammatory disease that can be silent, chronic, cirrhotic, acutely severe, or present as acute liver failure. It is diagnosed from a coherent combination of liver chemistry, immunoglobulins, antibodies, liver histology, and exclusion of viral, drug-induced, metabolic, and cholestatic causes.

Pathology

Liver biopsy commonly shows portal and periportal lymphocyte and plasma-cell inflammation extending across the limiting plate into hepatocytes, called interface hepatitis. Uncontrolled necroinflammation activates fibrogenic pathways, producing bridging fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and loss of hepatic synthetic and detoxifying function.

Pathophysiology

  • Loss of immune tolerance allows autoreactive lymphocytes and antibody-associated immune activity to injure hepatocytes. Immunosuppression reduces that attack, which is why normalization of AST, ALT, and IgG is both a treatment target and evidence that ongoing scar formation is being controlled.

  • Serologic patterns support classification but do not create the disease by themselves. Some patients are antibody negative, and positive ANA is common in other conditions, so biopsy and exclusion logic prevent both missed and false diagnoses.

Etiology

The exact initiating cause is unknown. Genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers likely interact, and autoimmune disease can cluster in the patient or family. Drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis must be distinguished from idiopathic AIH because the need and duration of immunosuppression may differ.

Risk Factors

  • Female sex, while recognizing that every sex and age can be affected

  • Personal or family history of autoimmune disease

  • A compatible genetic susceptibility and immune phenotype

  • Exposure to a medicine associated with autoimmune-like liver injury, requiring careful causal assessment rather than automatic idiopathic diagnosis

Signs Symptoms

  • Asymptomatic elevation of aminotransferases

  • Fatigue, malaise, anorexia, nausea, arthralgia, right-upper-quadrant discomfort, pruritus, or jaundice

  • Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, edema, ascites, bruising, or other cirrhosis findings

  • Rapid jaundice, coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, confusion, or encephalopathy in acute severe disease or liver failure

  • Coexisting thyroid, joint, skin, intestinal, or other autoimmune manifestations

Diagnostics

  • Combine AST and ALT pattern, IgG, ANA and smooth-muscle or other relevant antibodies, medication and supplement history, viral and metabolic exclusion, imaging, and compatible liver histology; no single result is diagnostic.

  • Liver biopsy is central when safe because it supports interface hepatitis, stages fibrosis, and evaluates competing causes.

  • Use current simplified or revised diagnostic scoring only as support; classification criteria and clinical diagnosis are not identical.

  • Evaluate for cirrhosis, acute severity, overlap with primary biliary cholangitis or primary sclerosing cholangitis, and transplantation need at presentation rather than waiting for treatment failure.

Labs

  • AST and ALT are commonly elevated in a hepatocellular pattern; bilirubin and INR identify severity and synthetic failure.

  • Serum IgG is often elevated, but a normal value does not exclude acute or atypical disease.

  • ANA, smooth-muscle, anti-LKM1, anti-LC1, or soluble-liver-antigen antibodies support specific contexts, while approximately a minority of patients remain seronegative.

  • Albumin and platelet count help identify chronic advanced disease; biochemical remission requires normalization of AST, ALT, and IgG rather than partial improvement alone.

Assessment

Assess the pace of jaundice, mental status, bleeding, glucose, ascites, edema, infection, medication and supplement timeline, alcohol exposure, pregnancy plans, autoimmune symptoms, adherence, and prior test trends. Immediately distinguish stable chronic inflammation from acute severe AIH or liver failure because the latter needs transplant-center timing, not only routine clinic immunosuppression.

Differential

Differentiate AIH from viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, Wilson disease, alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and overlap syndromes. Positive ANA alone is not enough, and negative antibodies do not exclude AIH.

Treatments

  • Prednisone or prednisolone with azathioprine is a standard first-line approach, individualized to severity, cirrhosis, pregnancy, blood counts, thiopurine risk, and organ function.

  • Budesonide with azathioprine may be used in selected noncirrhotic patients without acute severe disease to reduce some systemic steroid exposure.

  • Mycophenolate or tacrolimus may be used for intolerance or inadequate response under hepatology supervision.

  • Acute liver failure, worsening synthetic function, or inadequate early response in acute severe disease requires timely transplant-center evaluation.

  • Treat cirrhosis, portal-hypertension, bone, infection, nutritional, and other complications to reduce secondary harm while immunosuppression controls hepatic inflammation.

Contraindications

  • Do not use budesonide in cirrhosis or acute severe AIH because altered hepatic handling and severity make it inappropriate.

  • Mycophenolate is contraindicated in pregnancy and requires strict reproductive-risk management.

  • Azathioprine requires pretreatment and ongoing CBC and liver monitoring because severe marrow suppression, infection, pancreatitis, and hepatotoxicity can occur.

  • Do not stop glucocorticoids abruptly; adrenal suppression and AIH flare can follow.

  • Do not delay transplant referral while repeatedly changing immunosuppression in worsening acute liver failure.

Safety Considerations

  • Immunosuppression can mute fever and worsen bacterial, viral, or fungal infection; screen and reassess promptly.

  • Glucocorticoids affect glucose, blood pressure, mood, sleep, bone, eyes, infection risk, and adrenal function.

  • Increasing INR, hypoglycemia, or encephalopathy signals failing synthesis and detoxification and is more urgent than aminotransferase height alone.

  • Treatment withdrawal is considered only after sustained biochemical remission and careful reassessment; relapse is common.

  • Cirrhosis changes cancer, variceal, medication, and procedure safety even when hepatitis chemistry improves.

Nursing Priorities

  • Trend mental status, bleeding, jaundice, ascites, edema, weight, intake, glucose, AST, ALT, bilirubin, INR, albumin, IgG, CBC, and treatment-specific renal or drug tests.

  • Screen for infection before and during immunosuppression and escalate fever, hypotension, respiratory symptoms, urinary symptoms, or subtle decline.

  • Monitor steroid effects and azathioprine marrow and hepatic toxicity and verify that tapers and laboratory appointments are understood.

  • Review vaccines, bone health, alcohol, OTC acetaminophen and NSAIDs, herbal products, pregnancy plans, and interaction risks.

  • Use teach-back to distinguish biochemical remission from cure and to explain why medication adherence continues after symptoms resolve.

Red Flags

  • New confusion, asterixis, somnolence, or other hepatic encephalopathy

  • Increasing INR, active bleeding, hypoglycemia, rapidly worsening jaundice, or acute kidney injury

  • New ascites with fever or pain, GI bleeding, or hemodynamic instability

  • Severe infection or profound cytopenia during immunosuppression

  • Failure of acute severe disease to improve promptly under specialist treatment

Complications

  • Fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and liver failure requiring transplantation

  • Cirrhosis-associated hepatocellular carcinoma risk

  • Relapse during or after treatment reduction

  • Serious infection, osteoporosis, diabetes, hypertension, cytopenia, and other treatment toxicity

  • Pregnancy and postpartum flare or medication-related fetal risk

Prognosis

Most patients respond to immunosuppression, and achieving complete biochemical remission lowers the risk of progression. Delay in diagnosis, established cirrhosis, incomplete response, or acute liver failure worsens prognosis. Relapse is common, so long-term maintenance and surveillance are often necessary.

Prevention

There is no established primary prevention. Prevent secondary harm with hepatitis A and B vaccination when indicated, alcohol avoidance, medication and supplement review, bone protection, infection precautions, pregnancy planning, and regular biochemical and cirrhosis surveillance.

Patient Education

  • Autoimmune hepatitis is an immune attack on liver cells; it is not contagious and is not diagnosed by ANA alone.

  • Take immunosuppressive medicines and tapers exactly as prescribed even after energy and jaundice improve.

  • Avoid alcohol and check OTC medicines, supplements, and vaccines with the liver team.

  • Seek urgent help for confusion, bleeding, black stool, vomiting blood, rapidly increasing jaundice, fever, severe abdominal swelling, or very low urine output.

Special Populations

  • Aim for stable biochemical remission before conception and coordinate hepatology and maternal-fetal care throughout pregnancy and postpartum.

  • Prednisone or prednisolone and azathioprine are often continued when needed in pregnancy, while mycophenolate must not be used.

  • Children may have aggressive disease and overlap features and need pediatric hepatology protocols and growth monitoring.

  • Older adults and patients with cirrhosis need individualized infection, bone, frailty, and medication-risk management.

Nclex Traps

  • Positive ANA does not diagnose AIH, and negative autoantibodies do not exclude it.

  • Budesonide is not the safer steroid choice in cirrhosis or acute severe AIH.

  • Normalization of symptoms alone is not remission; AST, ALT, and IgG are followed.

  • Establish liver-failure and transplant urgency before focusing on a routine outpatient immunosuppression plan.
