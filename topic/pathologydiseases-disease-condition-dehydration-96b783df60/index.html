Definition

Dehydration develops when water loss exceeds replacement, raising tonicity when free-water loss predominates and reducing effective circulating volume when loss is substantial. The characteristic pattern is thirst, dry mucosa, orthostasis, tachycardia, low urine output, concentrated urine, weight loss, or confusion interpreted alongside sodium and the cause of loss. Confirm or stage it with serial weight, intake and output, orthostatic vital signs, sodium, glucose, creatinine, and osmolality; distinguish it from hemorrhage, sepsis, adrenal crisis, hyperglycemic osmotic diuresis, and isolated dry mouth by perfusion, laboratory, and loss history. The priority is to replace oral water and electrolytes when safe because hypovolemic shock and acute kidney injury can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Urine output change, edema, thirst, dry mucosa, dizziness, flank/suprapubic pain, dysuria, confusion, weakness, weight change, or abnormal heart rhythm can reveal renal, fluid, or electrolyte stress.

  • Escalate anuria, severe potassium abnormality, pulmonary edema, seizure, dysrhythmia, shock, or worsening acidosis.

Diagnostics

  • serial weight, intake and output, orthostatic vital signs, sodium, glucose, creatinine, and osmolality

  • urine concentration and sodium when renal handling helps identify the cause

  • focused evaluation for gastrointestinal, renal, skin, respiratory, or access-related losses

  • Distinguish Dehydration from hemorrhage, sepsis, adrenal crisis, hyperglycemic osmotic diuresis, and isolated dry mouth by perfusion, laboratory, and loss history.

Labs

  • creatinine and BUN rise when filtration worsens

  • potassium may rise with kidney failure

  • urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is concerning

  • WBC/nitrites/leukocyte esterase support UTI

Treatments

  • replace oral water and electrolytes when safe

  • use isotonic intravenous fluid first for hemodynamic compromise, then correct remaining tonicity deliberately

  • stop ongoing losses and review diuretics, glucose, fever, and access barriers

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for hypotension, syncope, confusion, severe tachycardia, oliguria, shock, seizure, severe hypernatremia or hyponatremia, persistent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, or failure to respond because organ hypoperfusion or a dangerous electrolyte disorder needs urgent monitored care.

  • Identify fluid losses, reduced intake, fever, diuretics, osmotic diuresis, bleeding, third spacing, age-related risk, weight change, orthostatic symptoms, thirst, mucosa, skin, cognition, and perfusion, and check glucose and ketones when diabetic osmotic loss is possible because dehydration ranges from free-water deficit to hemodynamic volume depletion.

  • Trend heart rate, lying and standing pressure when safe, capillary refill, mental status, daily weight, intake and output, urine concentration, sodium, glucose, urea, creatinine, bicarbonate, and lactate when ill because the pattern determines fluid type, rate, and urgency.

  • Replace fluid by the safest oral, enteral, or intravenous route in measured stages, use telemetry when severe electrolyte loss creates dysrhythmia risk, and reassess pulse, pressure, lungs, urine output, cognition, and electrolytes after each stage because rapid or excessive replacement can cause edema or dangerous sodium correction.

  • Treat the loss source with antiemetic, diarrhea, fever, glucose, medication, swallowing, or access interventions, obtain cultures when infection is suspected, and document ongoing losses because replacement cannot catch up when the mechanism continues unmeasured.

Complications

  • hypovolemic shock and acute kidney injury

  • hypernatremic neurologic injury or overly rapid correction

  • falls can complicate Dehydration.

  • delirium can complicate Dehydration.

  • medication accumulation can complicate Dehydration.

Red Flags

  • Hypotension, syncope, cool mottled skin, delayed capillary refill, or rising lactate

  • Confusion, seizure, severe weakness, or marked sodium abnormality

  • Oliguria or anuria with worsening creatinine

  • Persistent vomiting, high-output diarrhea, hematemesis, melena, or ongoing major loss

Patient Education

  • Teach small frequent oral rehydration with an appropriate electrolyte solution and how to match ongoing losses because plain water or very sugary drinks may not safely replace what was lost.

  • Provide an individualized early-call threshold for urine reduction, dizziness, weight loss, vomiting, diarrhea, or glucose elevation because timely treatment can prevent shock and kidney injury.

Nclex Traps

  • Dehydration is not synonymous with low blood volume; tonicity and circulation must both be assessed

  • Do not correct chronic hypernatremia rapidly
