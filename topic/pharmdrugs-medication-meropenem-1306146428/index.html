Used To Treat

Serious susceptible intra-abdominal, skin/soft-tissue, bacterial meningitis, bloodstream, pulmonary, urinary, and polymicrobial infections; commonly used for severe ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and susceptible Pseudomonas. It does not cover MRSA, atypicals, Stenotrophomonas, or every carbapenemase producer.

Mechanism

Meropenem acylates PBPs involved in elongation and septation; efficacy follows unbound time above MIC. Prolonged infusion can improve target attainment for severe infection or higher susceptible MICs, but cannot overcome true resistance. The 1-beta-methyl group confers stability to renal DHP-I. Renal clearance makes dose adjustment essential, while augmented clearance can cause underexposure in critical illness. Meropenem markedly lowers valproate by disrupting valproate-glucuronide recycling, so a patient may lose antiseizure protection even though meropenem is less proconvulsant than imipenem.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Carbapenem-valproate coadministration can precipitate breakthrough seizures. Serious allergy, CDI, cytopenias, hepatic injury, rhabdomyolysis, and concentration-related CNS toxicity require surveillance.

Contraindications

  • Serious hypersensitivity to meropenem or related beta-lactams requires avoidance or specialist-directed strategy.

  • Avoid with valproate when an active alternative exists because dose escalation often cannot reliably restore valproate concentration.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify organism/MIC, source, renal trajectory, CNS history, valproate, infusion duration, and source control before administration because spectrum alone does not guarantee safe effective exposure.

  • Trend muscle pain/weakness and CK when rhabdomyolysis is suspected, and escalate new neurologic changes for drug hold, renal reassessment, and EEG when indicated because ongoing muscle injury can cause kidney injury and concentration-related neurotoxicity can progress to nonconvulsive status or seizure.

Interactions

  • Meropenem profoundly lowers valproate/divalproex concentrations; probenecid increases meropenem exposure by reducing renal secretion.

  • Other nephrotoxic or neurotoxic drugs can reduce clearance or obscure meropenem-associated encephalopathy.

Key Labs

  • Culture/MIC and carbapenemase testing, creatinine/eGFR, CBC, liver tests, mental status, CK when muscle symptoms occur, and valproate concentration if exposed.

  • A susceptible result assumes an exposure regimen; missed prolonged infusions or augmented clearance can still fail.

Nclex Traps

  • Meropenem covers susceptible Pseudomonas and can be used for meningitis; ertapenem lacks Pseudomonas and is not a meningitis substitute.

  • Lower seizure risk than imipenem does not mean no neurotoxicity.

    Note

    Pediatric indication, meningitis role, formulation, dose, interval, renal maturation, and maximum dose differ by agent and age. Use the exact label or pediatric infectious-disease protocol because beta-lactams are not safely scaled from an adult regimen by intuition.

    Older adult caution

    Note

    Older adults can have sharply reduced renal clearance despite an unremarkable serum creatinine. Estimate kidney function, reassess it during acute illness, and connect new confusion, myoclonus, or seizure to possible beta-lactam accumulation rather than assuming delirium is unrelated.

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Many beta-lactams have substantial pregnancy and lactation experience, but infection severity, allergy history, maternal organ function, placental exposure, neonatal bilirubin handling, and the exact agent still matter. Treat serious infection promptly with an evidence-supported regimen rather than applying one class-wide pregnancy rule.

Description

Meropenem is an IV broad-spectrum carbapenem that crosses Gram-negative outer membranes, binds multiple PBPs, and rapidly stops cell-wall cross-linking. It resists hydrolysis by many ESBL and AmpC enzymes and retains activity against susceptible Pseudomonas, anaerobes, streptococci, and many Enterobacterales. Unlike imipenem, it is stable to human renal dehydropeptidase I and does not need cilastatin. It reaches inflamed CSF and is labeled for bacterial meningitis in selected pediatric patients, with lower epileptogenic potential than imipenem, though renal accumulation can still cause encephalopathy or seizures. KPC, OXA-48-like, NDM/VIM/IMP, porin loss, and efflux can all defeat it.

Adverse Effects

  • Headache, nausea, diarrhea, rash, phlebitis, liver-test elevation, thrombocytosis or cytopenias, candidiasis, and CDI.

  • Anaphylaxis, severe cutaneous reaction, encephalopathy, myoclonus, seizure, interstitial nephritis, and postmarketing rhabdomyolysis can occur.
