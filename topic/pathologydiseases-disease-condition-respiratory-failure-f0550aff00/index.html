Definition

Respiratory failure develops when the lungs cannot sustain adequate oxygen transfer, carbon-dioxide elimination, or both, and compensatory work is failing. The characteristic pattern is hypoxemia, rising or inadequately cleared carbon dioxide, severe work of breathing, altered mentation, fatigue, or inability to protect the airway. Confirm or stage it with immediate pulse oximetry and blood gas interpreted with the baseline; distinguish it from asthma or COPD exacerbation without failure, metabolic acidosis, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, opioid toxicity, and neuromuscular weakness by gas pattern and mechanism. The priority is to support oxygenation and ventilation before arrest because cardiac arrest can complicate Respiratory failure. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • immediate pulse oximetry and blood gas interpreted with the baseline

  • continuous respiratory effort, mental-status, and hemodynamic assessment

  • chest imaging, ECG, infection, pulmonary, neuromuscular, toxic, and metabolic testing directed at the cause

  • Distinguish Respiratory failure from asthma or COPD exacerbation without failure, metabolic acidosis, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, opioid toxicity, and neuromuscular weakness by gas pattern and mechanism.

Labs

  • trend abnormal values against baseline

  • watch electrolytes, CBC, renal function, liver function, and oxygenation when relevant

Treatments

  • support oxygenation and ventilation before arrest

  • treat the airway, lung, circulation, infection, toxin, or neuromuscular cause

  • use noninvasive or invasive ventilation according to airway protection, trajectory, and reversibility

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for apnea, a silent chest, inability to protect the airway, refractory hypoxemia, rising carbon dioxide with somnolence, or rapidly decreasing effort because these findings indicate impending ventilatory collapse.

  • Assess respiratory rate, work of breathing, chest movement, breath sounds, oxygen saturation, mental status, and ability to speak because exhaustion and rising carbon dioxide can precede respiratory arrest even before saturation collapses.

  • Position the airway, apply prescribed oxygen, and prepare bag-mask, noninvasive, or invasive ventilation because respiratory failure means oxygenation or carbon dioxide clearance is no longer meeting tissue needs.

  • Obtain and trend blood gases, carbon dioxide, pH, oxygenation, and response after each support change because a worsening pH or rising carbon dioxide shows inadequate ventilation rather than simple anxiety.

  • Identify and treat reversible drivers such as opioid effect, bronchospasm, mucus plugging, pneumothorax, pulmonary edema, infection, or neuromuscular weakness because ventilatory support alone does not remove the cause.

Complications

  • cardiac arrest can complicate Respiratory failure.

  • aspiration can complicate Respiratory failure.

  • ventilator injury can complicate Respiratory failure.

  • shock can complicate Respiratory failure.

  • delirium can complicate Respiratory failure.

  • hypoxic brain damage can complicate Respiratory failure.

Red Flags

  • Apnea, agonal breathing, or inability to protect the airway

  • Refractory hypoxemia despite increasing support

  • Rising carbon dioxide with acidosis, confusion, or somnolence

  • Exhaustion, paradoxical breathing, or a rapidly falling respiratory rate

Patient Education

  • Explain that oxygen can improve a low saturation but cannot replace ventilation when carbon dioxide is accumulating, so repeat assessment remains essential.

  • Teach the patient and family to seek emergency help for blue lips, new confusion, inability to speak normally, severe breathlessness, or failure of the prescribed rescue plan.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal pulse-oximeter value does not exclude dangerous hypercapnia

  • A tiring patient whose respiratory rate falls may be deteriorating, not recovering
