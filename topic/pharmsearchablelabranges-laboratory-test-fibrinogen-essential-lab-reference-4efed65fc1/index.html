Name

Fibrinogen

Range

Use the reporting laboratory's method and interval. Mayo Clinic Laboratories reports 200-393 mg/dL for one plasma assay; pregnancy, age, inflammation, assay method, anticoagulants, specimen handling, and the distinction between functional and antigenic fibrinogen affect interpretation. There is no universal critical fibrinogen cutoff for every bleeding, trauma, obstetric, or DIC protocol.

Why

Fibrinogen (factor I) is made by the liver and converted by thrombin into fibrin, the structural mesh of a clot. A low functional fibrinogen result means less usable clot substrate and raises concern for consumption in DIC, major hemorrhage or dilution, advanced liver disease, fibrinolytic therapy, or a congenital quantitative or functional disorder. A high result commonly reflects its acute-phase response during inflammation, infection, pregnancy or estrogen exposure, tissue injury, malignancy, obesity, diabetes, or nephrotic syndrome; it is nonspecific and does not diagnose thrombosis. Assess active bleeding or oozing, bruising, vital signs and perfusion, recent trauma, surgery or transfusion, obstetric bleeding, sepsis, liver disease, medications, and the platelet, PT/INR, aPTT, D-dimer, hemoglobin, and trend pattern. Cause-directed evaluation may include repeat functional fibrinogen, fibrinogen antigen or thrombin-time testing for suspected dysfunction, and broader DIC, liver, bleeding, or viscoelastic testing as ordered. Escalate immediately for heavy or rapidly progressive bleeding, shock, obstetric hemorrhage, bleeding from multiple sites, or suspected DIC with clinical deterioration. One fibrinogen value does not diagnose or exclude DIC; because fibrinogen rises with inflammation, an apparently normal or high value can still be falling relative to baseline during evolving consumption.

High Causes

  • Acute or chronic inflammation or infection

  • Pregnancy or estrogen exposure

  • Tissue injury, surgery, or malignancy

  • Obesity, diabetes, or nephrotic syndrome

  • Acute-phase response that can partly mask consumption

Low Causes

  • DIC or other acute intravascular consumption and fibrinolysis

  • Major hemorrhage, dilution, or large-volume transfusion

  • Advanced liver synthetic dysfunction

  • Fibrinolytic therapy

  • Congenital afibrinogenemia, hypofibrinogenemia, or dysfibrinogenemia

  • Assay interference or preanalytic error

Critical Concerns

  • Heavy bleeding, shock, obstetric hemorrhage, diffuse oozing, or rapid clinical deterioration with falling fibrinogen requires urgent escalation and the ordered hemorrhage or DIC pathway.

  • Thrombocytopenia plus prolonged PT/aPTT, decreased fibrinogen, and markedly elevated D-dimer supports concern for DIC, but diagnosis requires the clinical context and serial laboratory pattern.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess bleeding, perfusion, obstetric, trauma, surgical, sepsis, and liver context; trend fibrinogen with hemoglobin, platelets, PT/INR, aPTT, D-dimer, and clinical response.

  • Verify specimen quality and anticoagulant exposure when a result is unexpected, and anticipate repeat, antigenic, or broader coagulation testing as ordered.

  • Follow the ordered hemorrhage, transfusion, or DIC protocol; do not infer a universal replacement threshold from this reference card.

Nclex Traps

  • Low fibrinogen can contribute to bleeding because there is less substrate for fibrin clot formation.

  • High fibrinogen is often an acute-phase finding; it does not diagnose a clot, and one normal value does not exclude evolving DIC.
