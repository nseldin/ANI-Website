Definition

Staphylococcus epidermidis is a Gram-positive coccus that commonly colonizes human skin and mucous membranes. It is usually a commensal organism, but it can cause clinically important infection when a skin barrier is breached or when it adheres to an indwelling or prosthetic device. Colonization, blood-culture contamination, and true infection are different states and must be distinguished with the specimen context and the patient's clinical findings.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    S. epidermidis is a bacterial species in the genus Staphylococcus and is commonly described as a coagulase-negative staphylococcus. The abbreviation S. epidermidis is an identity alias; coagulase-negative staphylococci is a broader laboratory group, not a synonym for this species. It also must not be merged with Staphylococcus aureus, which differs in coagulase phenotype and clinical behavior.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    The cells are spherical, Gram positive, non-spore-forming, nonmotile, and facultatively anaerobic. They may appear in clusters because staphylococci divide in more than one plane. Gram reaction and arrangement can guide an early differential, while species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility require validated laboratory methods rather than morphology alone.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Human skin is the major reservoir, so the organism may enter a specimen during collection without causing disease. The same skin-associated ecology also creates an opportunity for endogenous infection when vascular access, surgery, or implanted material bypasses normal barriers. Transfer on hands or equipment can contribute to spread. Because colonization may precede infection for an indeterminate time, one species-wide incubation period would be misleading.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Attachment to foreign material and biofilm formation are central to clinically significant device-associated disease. A biofilm can help organisms persist on catheters, prosthetic valves, joints, or other implanted material and can make eradication difficult even when the organism appears less invasive than S. aureus. This draft does not equate that mechanism with S. aureus protein A or assert an unsupported toxin relationship.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    S. epidermidis is an important cause of infection involving intravascular catheters and other prosthetic material and can be associated with bloodstream infection and prosthetic-valve infection. It is also a frequent blood-culture contaminant. Nurses should not dismiss or diagnose infection from the organism name alone: the number and timing of positive culture sets, collection sites, device history, symptoms, hemodynamic status, and clinician assessment help distinguish contamination from true infection. Fever, hypotension, altered mental status, or other sepsis findings require prompt escalation.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Blood culture can recover S. epidermidis in suspected bloodstream infection, but interpretation is context dependent because skin flora can contaminate a specimen. Concordant growth from appropriately collected specimens, compatible clinical findings, and an implicated device increase concern for true infection; a single result is not independently definitive. Species identification and susceptibility testing should come from the clinical laboratory, and cultures should be collected according to current facility policy without delaying urgent care in an unstable patient.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Standard Precautions apply to every patient. Hand hygiene, aseptic device access, appropriate skin antisepsis, clean and intact dressings, environmental cleaning, and prompt removal of devices that are no longer needed reduce opportunities for entry and spread. S. epidermidis alone does not create a universal transmission-based isolation requirement; additional precautions depend on the syndrome, drainage, resistance findings, and current facility guidance.
