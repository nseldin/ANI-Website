Used To Treat

Extended-release products treat hypertension and chronic stable or vasospastic angina. Immediate-release oral nifedipine has protocol-defined obstetric uses, including selected acute severe pregnancy hypertension and off-label tocolysis, but formulation, dose, and monitoring must follow the exact obstetric protocol. It is not an AV-node rate-control drug.

Mechanism

Nifedipine binds vascular L-type Cav1.2 channels and reduces calcium entry into arterial smooth muscle. Lower calcium-calmodulin signaling reduces myosin light-chain kinase activity, relaxes resistance arterioles, lowers systemic vascular resistance and afterload, and dilates coronary arteries. In myometrium, less calcium entry reduces calcium-dependent actin-myosin contraction, explaining tocolytic use. Rapid vasodilation unloads arterial baroreceptors and increases sympathetic tone, which can cause tachycardia and worsen ischemia; extended-release delivery smooths the concentration curve and blunts that reflex. Nifedipine undergoes CYP3A first-pass metabolism, so grapefruit or strong CYP3A inhibitors can approximately double or otherwise raise exposure, while inducers can produce loss of effect.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Do not use short-acting immediate-release nifedipine as an improvised sublingual or bite-and-swallow treatment for hypertensive emergency because uncontrolled pressure reduction, reflex tachycardia, ischemia, stroke, and death have been reported with rapid use. Extended-release products must be swallowed according to product instructions and are not interchangeable by tablet manipulation. Excessive hypotension, worsening angina after starting or increasing therapy, advanced aortic stenosis, hepatic impairment, CYP3A interactions, and concurrent magnesium sulfate or other vasodilators require close clinical monitoring.

Contraindications

  • Serious hypersensitivity and product-specific restrictions; avoid uncontrolled rapid pressure reduction and assess severe aortic stenosis or shock.

  • Strong CYP3A inducers can make treatment ineffective; grapefruit and inhibitors can raise exposure. Do not crush, chew, or split a controlled-release product unless the exact label explicitly permits it.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify immediate-release versus extended-release product, indication, swallowing instructions, pressure target, pulse, edema and chest symptoms. A tablet shell from some osmotic extended-release products can appear in stool after the drug has been released and is not proof of failed absorption.

  • In obstetric use, follow the exact maternal-fetal protocol, reassess pressure and symptoms, and monitor additive hypotension/neuromuscular effects with magnesium or other therapy rather than assuming pregnancy makes every nifedipine regimen equivalent.

Interactions

  • Strong CYP3A inhibitors and grapefruit increase nifedipine exposure; CYP3A inducers such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, rifampin, or St. John's wort can reduce effect.

  • Other antihypertensives, nitrates, PDE5 inhibitors, alcohol, magnesium sulfate, and volume depletion can add hypotension. Tacrolimus concentrations may change and require protocol monitoring.

Key Labs

  • Blood pressure, pulse, orthostasis, edema and lung findings, angina pattern, renal perfusion after major pressure change, and liver function when impairment or injury is suspected.

  • In pregnancy, monitor maternal symptoms and fetal/uterine status according to indication; with interacting transplant therapy, follow tacrolimus troughs.

Nclex Traps

  • Nifedipine is a vascular DHP, not an AV-node blocker. It does not provide diltiazem-like atrial-fibrillation rate control.

  • The immediate-release and extended-release products are not interchangeable shortcuts. Rapid concentration rise is exactly what creates the ischemic danger.

Description

Nifedipine is a vascular-selective dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker that rapidly relaxes arterial smooth muscle and, in the uterus, can reduce myometrial contraction. Extended-release formulations provide maintenance blood-pressure and antianginal control. Short-acting immediate-release nifedipine can drop pressure abruptly, trigger baroreceptor-mediated tachycardia, increase myocardial oxygen demand, and compromise cerebral or coronary perfusion if improvised for routine hypertensive urgency. This is why the formulation is part of the drug's identity: a controlled-release tablet is not merely a slower version that can be crushed, and a bite-and-swallow or sublingual shortcut is unsafe outside a validated protocol. Peripheral edema reflects capillary pressure redistribution and is not automatically heart failure.

Adverse Effects

  • Peripheral edema, headache, flushing, dizziness, palpitations, reflex tachycardia, nausea, heartburn, constipation, and gingival overgrowth.

  • Severe hypotension, syncope, worsening angina or MI, pulmonary edema in vulnerable patients, hepatic injury, hypersensitivity, and prolonged shock after overdose or modified-release ingestion.
