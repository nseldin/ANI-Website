Plain Language

Shared and induced delusional phenomena occur when a person in a close relationship adopts or develops delusional beliefs alongside someone who already has prominent delusions. This is not a standalone diagnosis; each person needs an independent assessment because similar beliefs can have different causes.

Why It Matters

A shared belief can drive food or medication refusal, unsafe flight, stalking, financial loss, neglect of dependents, self-harm, or harm toward a perceived target. Nurses should assess each person separately when safe, identify medical and psychiatric causes, protect vulnerable people, and base urgency on current actions and risk rather than automatically separating a relationship or labeling one person the inducer.

Definition

Shared and induced delusional phenomena is ANI's clinical-concept entry for delusional symptoms that arise or are reinforced in the context of a close relationship with someone who has prominent delusions. It is not a standalone DSM-5-TR diagnosis. DSM-5 removed the former shared psychotic disorder category: each person is assessed for delusional disorder or another complete syndrome, and a person who has clinically important delusional symptoms without meeting a named disorder may fit other specified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder. ICD-11 describes the rare or uncommon shared or induced presentation within delusional disorder 6A24, not as a separate diagnosis. The relationship pattern itself does not diagnose any participant.

Pathology

The phenomenon may involve social isolation, dependence, repeated confirmation, unequal influence, shared threat interpretation, or coercion, but none is universal and there is no biomarker or proven one-way transmission mechanism. One person may have an established psychotic disorder while another develops related symptoms, both may have independent disorders, or the apparent agreement may reflect culture, misinformation, fear, accommodation, coercive control, delirium, dementia, substances, or another cause. Clinical meaning comes from each person's symptom pattern, reality testing, function, relationship context, and intended actions.

Pathophysiology

  • There is no disease pathophysiology for the relationship phenomenon itself; it describes an observed clinical pattern rather than a distinct brain disorder.

  • Isolation, dependence, trust, repeated exposure to one explanation, fear, and reduced contact with alternative perspectives may reinforce belief conviction, but clinicians must not assume a dominant and passive role without evidence.

  • Psychosis, mood disorder, neurocognitive disease, delirium, substance exposure, trauma, or coercion may affect one or more people independently and must be assessed separately.

Etiology

No single cause explains shared or induced delusional phenomena. Close contact, isolation, dependence, caregiver relationships, stress, trauma, cognitive impairment, and an individual's vulnerability may contribute, but the pattern does not prove manipulation or abuse. Historical terms such as folie à deux and induced delusional disorder can imply a simple primary-secondary direction that may not fit the evidence. Cultural or religious agreement, misinformation, conspiracy belief, groupthink, and coercive compliance are not automatically delusions.

Risk Factors

  • Close or dependent relationships with limited outside contact and repeated exposure to one person's prominent delusions, without treating closeness or isolation as proof

  • A chronic psychotic disorder with prominent delusions in one person and emotional, cognitive, developmental, medical, or social vulnerability in another

  • Dementia, delirium, sensory loss, substance use, sleep deprivation, trauma, depression, bipolar disorder, neurologic disease, or intellectual or communication needs in either person

  • Coercive control, abuse, exploitation, financial dependence, caregiver dependence, shared housing, or barriers to private assessment

  • Beliefs that prompt weapon access, stalking, retaliation, food or medicine refusal, unsafe travel, self-harm, or neglect of children or other dependents

Signs Symptoms

  • Two or more closely connected people express delusional beliefs with substantially matching content, timing, and behavioral consequences

  • One person may have a chronic psychotic disorder with prominent delusions while another has related symptoms that do not meet a complete named psychotic disorder

  • Belief intensity or behavior may change with independent contact, broader social input, treatment, or a safer environment, but change during separation is not a diagnostic test

  • Fear, suspiciousness, withdrawal, repeated checking, food or treatment refusal, relocation, spending, surveillance, stalking, or protective actions may follow the belief

  • Hallucinations, disorganization, mood episodes, cognitive change, delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, trauma symptoms, coercion, and genuine external threats may coexist or provide another explanation

Diagnostics

  • Do not diagnose the phenomenon as one shared disorder. Assess each person independently for delusional disorder, schizophrenia-spectrum illness, mood disorder with psychosis, delirium, dementia, substance or medication effects, and medical or neurologic causes.

  • When safe and lawful, interview people separately and compare each person's exact belief, onset, source, conviction, evidence considered, reality testing, distress, impairment, actions, culture, cognition, substances, medicines, and longitudinal course.

  • DSM-5-TR uses the example delusional symptoms in the context of relationship within other specified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder when characteristic symptoms cause distress or impairment but do not meet a full named disorder; this remains an individual diagnosis, not a relationship-wide label.

  • Obtain collateral history with consent or lawful authority and assess actual threats, misinformation, cultural or spiritual beliefs, coercive control, abuse, cognitive impairment, and accommodation before concluding that a belief is delusional or induced.

  • No relationship pattern, shared narrative, screening score, response to separation, laboratory result, or response to antipsychotic medication proves direction of influence or diagnosis.

Labs

  • No laboratory test, brain scan, EEG, genetic test, or psychological scale confirms shared or induced delusional phenomena.

  • Use targeted medical, neurologic, cognitive, toxicology, medication, infection, metabolic, sensory, and sleep evaluation for each person according to onset, age, examination, and differential.

  • Normal testing does not establish a psychotic cause, and one abnormal result does not explain every participant's belief; integrate findings separately for each person.

Assessment

First assess each person's airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, glucose when indicated, attention, cognition, neurologic status, intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis, mood episode, suicide, self-harm, homicide, target, weapons, food or treatment refusal, self-care, and dependent safety. Arrange private communication when safe, use interpreters or accommodations, and document who stated each fact. Explore relationship power, access to money and care, coercion, genuine threats, culture, and what action each person plans without arguing about or validating the belief.

Differential

  • Two independent psychotic disorders, delusional disorder, schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizoaffective disorder, or other specified schizophrenia-spectrum and psychotic disorder in one or more people

  • Bipolar or major depressive disorder with psychotic features, PTSD, obsessions or overvalued ideas, severe anxiety, dissociation, or personality pathology

  • Delirium, dementia or another neurocognitive disorder, brain injury, seizure, infection, metabolic or endocrine illness, medication effect, intoxication, or withdrawal

  • Culturally or religiously shared belief, misinformation, conspiracy belief, groupthink, rumor, accommodation, role play, or shared fear without delusional-level conviction and impairment

  • Coercive control, abuse, exploitation, forced agreement, factitious behavior, malingering, or a genuine external threat that requires safeguarding or legal assessment

Treatments

  • Treat immediate medical, psychiatric, and safeguarding risks for each person and create separate formulations, diagnoses, consent decisions, and care plans rather than treating a relationship as one patient.

  • Build therapeutic alliance with calm reality-based communication. Validate fear or distress without validating the belief, avoid argument, and focus first on sleep, food, safety, function, support, and the person's goals.

  • Treat any independently diagnosed psychotic, mood, substance-related, cognitive, neurologic, or medical condition according to its own evidence. No medication treats the sharedness itself. Antipsychotic treatment belongs only when a separately assessed psychotic syndrome warrants it; it is not automatic for every person who agrees with the belief.

  • Increase access to safe independent support, information, follow-up, housing, communication, and practical resources when acceptable. Separate interviews support assessment; physical separation or contact restriction is not a universal treatment and requires current safety, safeguarding, legal, and least-restrictive justification.

  • Coordinate psychiatry, primary care, neurology or geriatrics when indicated, social work, safeguarding, domestic-violence services, family support with consent, and a written crisis and lethal-means plan.

Contraindications

  • Do not document shared psychotic disorder as a current standalone DSM diagnosis, and do not assign one diagnosis jointly to a couple, family, or group.

  • Do not assume who is the inducer, who is passive, that the relationship is romantic, or that shared symptoms prove coercion, abuse, cult involvement, or deliberate manipulation.

  • Do not label a culturally shared belief, misinformation, groupthink, conspiracy theory, or agreement with another person as psychosis without individual clinical requirements.

  • Do not argue, ridicule, deceive, endorse, or reinforce the belief, and do not use forced separation, involuntary care, seclusion, restraint, or disclosure solely because beliefs are shared.

  • Do not miss delirium, dementia, substances, medicines, neurologic illness, mood disorder, genuine danger, or abuse by attributing every participant's behavior to the relationship.

Safety Considerations

  • Ask each person separately when possible about suicide, self-harm, homicide, targets, commands, weapon and lethal-means access, stalking, retaliation, fleeing, and ability to delay action.

  • Assess food, fluids, shelter, essential medicine or other essential care, healthcare refusal, poisoning, financial exploitation, domestic violence, coercive control, abuse, and safety of children or dependent adults.

  • Use current risk, capacity for the specific decision, law, policy, and the least restrictive effective approach for observation, separation, visitation, disclosure, involuntary care, seclusion, or restraint.

  • Preserve source-attributed documentation and confidentiality while coordinating information necessary for imminent-risk response and safeguarding.

Nursing Priorities

  • Triage acute medical, neurologic, delirium, substance, psychosis, mood, suicide, violence, and safeguarding risks for each person before applying the relationship formulation.

  • Interview separately when safe, use calm nonconfrontational language, validate emotion without validating the belief, and ask what action the person intends.

  • Document each person's exact words, belief conviction, onset, source, observed behavior, function, planned actions, culture, cognition, substances, and collateral source without copying one account into another's record.

  • Support independent access to food, medicines, communication, finances, housing, care, and trusted people while avoiding abrupt separation unsupported by safety or law.

  • Coordinate individualized medical and psychiatric treatment, safeguarding, social support, family work with consent, and crisis planning across the responsible teams.

Red Flags

  • Suicidal or homicidal intent, a named target, dangerous commands, weapon access, stalking, escalating retaliation, unsafe flight, or inability to delay action

  • Refusal of food, fluids, shelter, essential medicine, or urgent care because of the belief; severe self-neglect or a dependent person at risk

  • Fluctuating attention, fever, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, focal neurologic deficit, seizure, head injury, intoxication, withdrawal, or rapid cognitive decline in any participant

  • Coercive control, abuse, exploitation, confinement, blocked private communication, financial control, or a plan for unsafe discharge or forced separation without safeguarding review

Complications

  • Suicide, self-harm, assault, stalking, retaliation, weapon use, unsafe flight, treatment refusal, malnutrition, exposure, and neglect of dependents

  • Reinforced fear and isolation, relationship breakdown, financial or housing loss, school or work disruption, exploitation, and delayed independent care

  • Missed psychotic, mood, substance-related, neurologic, medical, delirium, or neurocognitive disorder in one or more people

  • Stigma, coercion, unnecessary medication, forced separation, confidentiality harm, and false allegations when a historical label is treated as proof

  • Continued abuse or coercive control when clinicians assume every apparent agreement is voluntary or psychiatric

Prognosis

Course depends on each person's underlying diagnosis, duration and conviction of symptoms, cognition, substance exposure, relationship safety, social isolation, and access to treatment and independent support. Symptoms may lessen when an underlying disorder is treated or the person gains safe alternative perspectives, but separation alone is not a reliable cure or diagnostic test. Persistence does not prove deliberate collusion, and improvement does not establish which person caused the belief.

Prevention

  • There is no proven universal prevention. Early treatment of psychosis, mood illness, delirium, dementia, substance use, and sensory or medical problems may reduce risk of escalating shared symptoms.

  • Support safe outside relationships, private access to healthcare and communication, reliable information, stable housing, sleep, medication continuity, and crisis planning.

  • Train teams to document individual evidence, cultural context, coercion and safeguarding concerns, and current risk rather than reusing historical primary-secondary labels.

Patient Education

  • This term describes a relationship-context pattern of similar delusional symptoms; it is not one diagnosis shared by two people and does not by itself prove who influenced whom.

  • Each person needs a separate assessment for psychosis, mood symptoms, substances, medicines, delirium, dementia, medical illness, culture, actual threats, and coercion.

  • Care focuses on safety, respectful communication, independent support, and treatment of any condition each person actually has. Acknowledge distress without affirming the belief. Medication and physical separation are not automatic.

  • Seek urgent help for a plan to harm self or someone else, weapon access, stalking, refusal of essential food or care, severe confusion, seizure, intoxication or withdrawal, abuse, or a dependent person in danger.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require developmental, family, trauma, psychosis, substance, school, and safeguarding assessment and private communication appropriate to age and safety.

  • Older adults require urgent review for delirium, dementia, sensory loss, medication effects, infection, isolation, caregiver dependence, exploitation, and decision-specific capacity.

  • People with intellectual, communication, language, hearing, vision, autism, brain injury, or cognitive disability need accommodations so reliance on another person's account is not mistaken for shared delusion.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum change requires obstetric, sleep, medication, mood, psychosis, suicide, infant-safety, and relationship assessment for each affected person.

  • Cultural, spiritual, migration, discrimination, political violence, and genuine community-threat contexts must be understood before shared beliefs are labeled delusional.

Nclex Traps

  • Shared psychotic disorder is not a current standalone DSM diagnosis; assess and diagnose each person individually under the appropriate syndrome. Shared content does not prove induction, identify a dominant person, or establish incapacity.

  • Do not assume a romantic partner, dominant inducer, passive recipient, coercion, or abuse. These are possibilities to assess, not facts created by the label.

  • A shared cultural belief, misinformation, conspiracy belief, or group agreement is not automatically delusion; distress, impairment, reality testing, context, and differential matter.

  • Validate distress without validating the belief, interview separately when safe, and ask what each person plans to do.

  • No medication treats the phenomenon itself, and separation is not automatic treatment; use individual diagnoses, current risk, safeguarding, law, and least-restrictive care.

Source Note

Original ANI nursing synthesis. WHO and DSM support individual psychotic-disorder assessment and medical, mood, substance, cognitive, and cultural differentials. APA's DSM-5 change summary documents removal of shared psychotic disorder as a separate diagnosis, and the DSM-5-TR clarification supports the individual other-specified relationship-context example without assuming a romantic partner or one source disorder. VA/DoD and NICE CG178 support comprehensive psychosis assessment and individualized treatment; VA communication guidance, NICE NG10, and SAFE-T support nonconfrontational communication, least-restrictive care, suicide assessment, and safety planning. No source makes separation or medication automatic.
