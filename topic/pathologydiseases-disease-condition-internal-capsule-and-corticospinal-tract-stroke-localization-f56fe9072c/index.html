Risk Factors

  • Long-standing hypertension and diabetes damage small penetrating arteries, increasing lacunar internal-capsule infarction.

  • Atrial fibrillation or large-artery plaque can still embolize to deep branches, so a small lesion does not prove small-vessel mechanism.

  • Prior stroke and diffuse cerebral small-vessel disease increase the chance of recurrent deep infarction and gait-cognitive burden.

Signs Symptoms

  • Contralateral face-arm-leg weakness without aphasia, neglect, field cut, or major sensory loss suggests a pure motor lacunar syndrome.

  • Dysarthria and clumsy-hand syndrome can occur when compact corticobulbar and hand-motor pathways are affected.

  • Hyperreflexia, spasticity, and Babinski are upper-motor-neuron signs that often evolve after the acute flaccid phase.

Diagnostics

  • Test face, arm, and leg power plus reflexes, tone, plantar response, sensation, language, gaze, fields, and neglect because the absence of cortical signs supports a deep localization.

  • MRI DWI is more sensitive than early CT for a small acute internal-capsule infarct.

  • CTA and cardiac evaluation may still be appropriate because lesion size alone does not determine the vascular mechanism.

Labs

  • Check glucose because a metabolic mimic can cause focal weakness.

  • Review vascular-risk labs and blood pressure because small-vessel injury is strongly associated with chronic vascular exposure.

  • Use anticoagulant, platelet, renal, and coagulation data for acute treatment safety rather than assuming a lacune is too small for reperfusion consideration.

Treatments

  • Use the acute stroke pathway for disabling sudden weakness because a small lesion can cause major functional loss.

  • Start early positioning, range-of-motion, shoulder protection, mobility, and task-specific rehabilitation because secondary injury and learned nonuse compound tract damage.

  • Control the confirmed vascular mechanism and risk factors because recurrence can accumulate into gait and cognitive disability.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess face, arm, and leg strength separately, pronator drift, tone, reflexes, sensation, speech clarity, eye movements, language, and coordination because dense proportional weakness can localize to compact descending fibers while cortical signs suggest another site.

  • Document exact onset and activate the stroke pathway even when weakness is the only finding because a small internal-capsule infarct can produce profound disability and remains time sensitive.

  • Support the weak shoulder, position joints neutrally, protect insensate skin, and use assisted transfers with fall precautions because early traction, pressure, and unsafe movement cause avoidable pain and injury.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, limb strength, speech, swallowing, consciousness, blood pressure, glucose, and mobility tolerance because evolving infarction, edema, aspiration, or recurrent ischemia can worsen an initially pure motor syndrome.

  • Escalate for new or worsening hemiparesis, NIH Stroke Scale increase, dysphagia with aspiration, reduced consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, seizure, or respiratory compromise because stroke progression, hemorrhage, and airway failure require immediate reassessment.

Complications

  • Falls, contracture, spasticity, shoulder pain, DVT, pressure injury, and loss of hand function can follow dense motor weakness.

  • Recurrent lacunes can produce vascular cognitive impairment and gait dysfunction because small injuries accumulate across networks.

  • Aspiration can occur when corticobulbar fibers are involved despite preserved comprehension.

Contraindications

  • Do not equate a small imaging lesion with a minor disability because compact fibers magnify functional effect.

  • Do not assume all contralateral weakness is internal capsule; cortical signs, sensory level, cranial-nerve pattern, or peripheral findings change localization.

  • Do not expect spasticity to be immediate in every patient because acute weakness may initially be flaccid.

Red Flags

  • Sudden dense weakness affecting the face, arm, and leg on one side

  • Progressive motor deficit, NIH Stroke Scale increase, or recurrent fluctuating weakness

  • Coughing, wet voice, inability to handle secretions, aspiration, or oxygen decline

  • Reduced consciousness, severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, or unequal pupils

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for sudden one-sided weakness even without pain or speech change because a small deep stroke can be severely disabling.

  • Never pull on the weak arm during transfers because unsupported traction can injure the shoulder before protective muscle control returns.

Nclex Traps

  • Internal-capsule stroke can be dense but lack aphasia or neglect.

  • Above the medullary crossing, weakness is usually contralateral.

  • Small-vessel appearance does not eliminate the need to evaluate embolic and large-artery causes.

Name

Internal capsule and corticospinal tract stroke localization

Category

Neuroanatomy and stroke localization

Definition

An internal-capsule stroke is a deep cerebral infarction that can injure tightly packed corticospinal and corticobulbar fibers, often causing sudden contralateral weakness of the face, arm, and leg without cortical signs such as aphasia, neglect, gaze deviation, or visual-field loss. Motor fibers from broad cortical regions converge through the corona radiata; corticobulbar fibers pass mainly through the genu and corticospinal fibers through the posterior limb before descending into the brainstem. Because these fibers occupy a compact space, even a small lacunar infarct can produce dense pure motor hemiparesis.

Etiology

Small penetrating-artery lipohyalinosis from chronic hypertension and diabetes is a classic cause, but embolism, parent-artery atherosclerosis, hemorrhage, demyelination, tumor, or inflammatory disease can also affect this compact pathway. The sudden onset and vascular pattern distinguish stroke from many chronic tract disorders.

Pathology

Upper motor-neuron fibers descend from motor cortex through subcortical white matter, internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, pons, and medullary pyramids. Most cross at the pyramidal decussation, so a lesion above the crossing causes weakness on the opposite side, while a spinal lesion below it causes weakness on the same side. Nearby thalamocortical sensory fibers can create a sensorimotor lacunar syndrome when involved.

Pathophysiology

Acute interruption of corticospinal signaling causes weakness and loss of fractionated movement. Early tone may be reduced because the pathway is suddenly disconnected; later hyperreflexia, spasticity, clonus, and a Babinski response emerge as descending inhibition is lost. A cortical lesion often adds cortical signs, while a compact deep lesion can affect face, arm, and leg proportionately without them. Brainstem lesions may add ipsilateral cranial-nerve findings with contralateral body weakness because cranial nuclei and long tracts lie together.
