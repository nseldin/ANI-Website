Definition

Apnea is an observed respiratory-cessation sign in which effective airflow stops. Chest movement may also stop, or obstructed effort may continue without moving air.

Plain Language

Apnea means effective breathing has stopped.

Why It Matters

Unexpected apnea can rapidly deprive the brain and heart of oxygen and may represent respiratory or cardiac arrest. An unresponsive person with no normal breathing or only gasping needs immediate emergency-response and resuscitation assessment.

Associated Conditions

  • complete cessation of effective airflow from cardiopulmonary arrest, severe airway obstruction, depressed central respiratory drive, drug effect, neurologic disease, neuromuscular failure, sleep-disordered breathing, or exhausted respiratory muscles

Assessment Method

First assess responsiveness and activate appropriate emergency support, then observe chest and abdominal movement and airflow while checking airway patency, pulse according to current resuscitation protocol, skin color, oxygenation, recent medication exposure, and whether only ineffective gasps are present.

Positive Finding

No effective airflow or breath occurs during the observed interval; the chest may be still or may show obstructed effort without air movement.

Mechanism

Ventilation stops when the brain does not generate respiratory drive, airflow is completely blocked, or respiratory muscles cannot create an effective breath, so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises rapidly.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Apnea finding raises concern for complete cessation of effective airflow from cardiopulmonary arrest, severe airway obstruction, depressed central respiratory drive, drug effect, neurologic disease, neuromuscular failure, sleep-disordered breathing, or exhausted respiratory muscles because ventilation stops when the brain does not generate respiratory drive, airflow is completely blocked, or respiratory muscles cannot create an effective breath, so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises rapidly. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Apnea does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Apnea to focus evaluation of complete cessation of effective airflow from cardiopulmonary arrest, severe airway obstruction, depressed central respiratory drive, drug effect, neurologic disease, neuromuscular failure, sleep-disordered breathing, or exhausted respiratory muscles and the reviewed alternatives: breath holding, agonal gasping, hypopnea, monitor artifact. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: First assess responsiveness and activate appropriate emergency support, then observe chest and abdominal movement and airflow while checking airway patency, pulse according to current resuscitation protocol, skin color, oxygenation, recent medication exposure, and whether only ineffective gasps are present. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Apnea as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

A brief voluntary breath hold or a scored sleep event is not interchangeable with sustained respiratory arrest; pulse oximetry can lag, and chest movement alone cannot distinguish obstructive from central apnea. Therefore, Apnea cannot confirm or exclude complete cessation of effective airflow from cardiopulmonary arrest, severe airway obstruction, depressed central respiratory drive, drug effect, neurologic disease, neuromuscular failure, sleep-disordered breathing, or exhausted respiratory muscles by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • breath holding

  • agonal gasping

  • hypopnea

  • monitor artifact

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as breath holding, agonal gasping, hypopnea, monitor artifact.

False Negative Situations

Apnea may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of complete cessation of effective airflow from cardiopulmonary arrest, severe airway obstruction, depressed central respiratory drive, drug effect, neurologic disease, neuromuscular failure, sleep-disordered breathing, or exhausted respiratory muscles.

Modern Status

Current emergency sign when unexpected in an awake or unresponsive patient; recurrent sleep events require sleep-specific measurement and diagnostic criteria. Safety Precautions

Observe Apnea with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: First assess responsiveness and activate appropriate emergency support, then observe chest and abdominal movement and airflow while checking airway patency, pulse according to current resuscitation protocol, skin color, oxygenation, recent medication exposure, and whether only ineffective gasps are present. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Treat unresponsiveness with absent or only gasping breathing as possible cardiac arrest and follow current emergency-response and resuscitation protocol immediately; do not wait for oxygen saturation to fall, and do not perform a blind finger sweep for suspected airway obstruction.
