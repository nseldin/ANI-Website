Used To Treat

CNS depression, vomiting, hemorrhagic gastritis, hypotension, respiratory compromise, and ketosis after isopropyl alcohol or acetone exposure while distinguishing it from methanol, ethylene glycol, alcoholic ketoacidosis, and diabetic ketoacidosis.

Description

Isopropanol is a sedative toxic alcohol that irritates the stomach and is converted by alcohol dehydrogenase to acetone, producing ketosis without the strongly acidic metabolites of methanol or ethylene glycol. Acetone prolongs sedation but does not generate their classic high-anion-gap acidosis, which explains the combination of intoxication, prominent vomiting or hemorrhagic gastritis, an early osmolar gap, and ketones without marked acidosis. Hypotension, hypothermia, respiratory depression, and very high measured creatinine can occur; acetone can interfere with some creatinine assays, producing apparent kidney injury that must be compared with urine output and alternative measurement. Treatment is usually supportive. Fomepizole is generally avoided in isolated isopropanol poisoning because blocking metabolism prolongs the more toxic parent alcohol. Hemodialysis is reserved for extraordinary severe cases such as refractory shock, profound coma, or inability to clear the exposure, not for a ketone result alone.

Mechanism

Isopropanol crosses the blood-brain barrier and depresses neuronal function more strongly than ethanol. Gastric irritation produces pain, vomiting, and sometimes bleeding. Hepatic alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes isopropanol to acetone; acetone contributes prolonged sedation and the fruity odor but is not metabolized into formic or oxalic acid. This explains ketosis without substantial anion-gap acidosis and why bicarbonate or fomepizole borrowed from other toxic-alcohol pathways may be the wrong reflex. Airway support, ventilation, isotonic fluid, temperature control, and treatment of GI bleeding preserve function while both compounds are eliminated through metabolism, lungs, and kidneys. Dialysis can remove isopropanol and acetone when life-threatening physiology persists, but most patients improve with supportive care alone.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for difficult arousal, slow breathing, collapse, severe vomiting or hematemesis, and call Poison Help for any significant ingestion. Remove external contamination, provide fresh air, protect the airway, check glucose and temperature, and treat shock with monitored fluids and vasopressors as needed.

  • Obtain measured osmolality, electrolytes, blood gas, ketones, renal studies, ethanol and other toxic-alcohol evaluation when the identity is uncertain. Treat the patient rather than the odor; methanol, ethylene glycol, diabetic ketoacidosis, sepsis, alcoholic ketoacidosis, and co-ingestants can coexist.

  • Consult toxicology and nephrology early for refractory hypotension, persistent severe coma, respiratory failure, very high burden, or declining elimination. Use hemodialysis selectively; do not start fomepizole for a confidently isolated isopropanol exposure without expert reason.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify the exact product and concentration because rubbing alcohol, hand sanitizer, industrial solvent, and nail-polish remover may contain different alcohols, acetone, denaturants, or co-solvents requiring different treatment.

  • Trend airway reflexes, respiratory effort, end-tidal carbon dioxide when available, blood pressure, temperature, ECG, urine output, abdominal pain, emesis, occult or visible blood, and serial mental status. Maintain aspiration and GI-bleeding precautions.

  • Record osmolar and anion gaps with collection times and coexisting ethanol. If creatinine is unexpectedly high without parallel urine or urea changes, alert the laboratory and consider acetone-related assay interference rather than assuming renal failure.

Key Labs

  • Bedside glucose, electrolytes, bicarbonate, blood gas, lactate, measured serum osmolality, serum and urine ketones or beta-hydroxybutyrate, creatinine and urea, CBC, ethanol, ECG, and targeted methanol or ethylene-glycol studies when the product or acid-base pattern is uncertain.

  • An early osmolar gap can fall as parent alcohol disappears, while acetone and ketones persist. Ketosis without high-anion-gap acidosis supports isopropanol but is not absolute when shock, ketoacidosis, or mixed ingestion adds lactate or other acids.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe poisoning can cause coma, apnea, aspiration, hemorrhagic gastritis, hypotension, hypothermia, dysrhythmia, shock, and death. Eye, skin, and airway irritation occur with direct or vapor exposure.

  • Excess fluids can cause overload, intubation can add hypotension, and inappropriate fomepizole can prolong intoxication. Dialysis carries access, bleeding, and hemodynamic risks and is justified by severe physiology rather than the name toxic alcohol alone.

Contraindications

  • Do not induce vomiting or give oral fluids to a drowsy or vomiting patient. Activated charcoal binds alcohols poorly and is not routine for isolated isopropanol.

  • Do not automatically give fomepizole because this is a toxic alcohol; in isolated isopropanol it blocks conversion to acetone and can prolong parent-alcohol toxicity. Do not diagnose diabetic ketoacidosis from ketones alone without glucose and acid-base assessment.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Refractory shock, severe GI bleeding, respiratory failure, persistent coma, worsening acidosis that suggests a mixed cause, or failure to improve requires ICU, toxicology, and nephrology with selective hemodialysis consideration.

Interactions

  • Ethanol, opioids, benzodiazepines, and sedating medicines deepen CNS and respiratory depression. Methanol or ethylene glycol in a mislabeled or mixed product changes the antidote and dialysis pathway completely.

  • Acetone can interfere with selected creatinine assays, while ketoacidosis, shock, and kidney disease can create real biochemical abnormalities; reconcile laboratory method with the whole clinical trend.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Small volumes of concentrated rubbing alcohol can produce profound CNS depression; product identification and measured-weight assessment are urgent.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Stabilize maternal airway, ventilation, glucose, and perfusion immediately, then coordinate obstetric assessment and exposure counseling.

  • Population

    Alcohol-use disorder or limited access

    Note

    Substitution of rubbing alcohol may coexist with withdrawal, malnutrition, alcoholic ketoacidosis, or methanol exposure and requires nonjudgmental broader assessment.

Evidence Limitations

  • Severe isopropanol treatment evidence comes largely from case reports and incident-management experience. UKHSA guidance supports supportive management; dialysis thresholds and rare mixed-exposure decisions require poison-center and nephrology context.

Nclex Traps

  • Isopropanol classically causes ketosis without the severe anion-gap acidosis of methanol or ethylene glycol.

  • Fomepizole can prolong isolated isopropanol toxicity by blocking conversion to acetone.

  • Acetone may falsely elevate creatinine in some assays, so interpret kidney injury with urine output and method.

Regulatory Status

No specific FDA-approved antidote exists. Supportive care is standard, and hemodialysis is a rare specialist rescue; fomepizole is used for other toxic alcohols and is generally inappropriate for isolated isopropanol.

Boxed Warning

ISOPROPANOL WARNING: call 911 for coma, slow breathing, collapse, or vomiting blood. Support airway and perfusion and distinguish ketosis without acidosis from methanol, ethylene glycol, or ketoacidosis; do not reflexively give fomepizole because it can prolong isolated isopropanol toxicity.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
