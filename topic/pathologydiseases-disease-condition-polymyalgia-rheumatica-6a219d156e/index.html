Definition

Polymyalgia rheumatica develops when an inflammatory syndrome affects shoulder and hip girdle bursae and periarticular structures in adults over fifty and is closely linked to giant-cell arteritis. The characteristic pattern is bilateral shoulder and hip aching with prolonged morning stiffness, rapid functional loss, elevated inflammatory markers, and no true primary muscle weakness. Confirm or stage it with ESR and CRP with CBC, metabolic, thyroid, CK, and medication context; distinguish it from elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis, myositis, hypothyroidism, statin myopathy, malignancy, and infection by joints, strength, CK, serology, and systemic findings. The priority is to use the lowest effective glucocorticoid regimen with slow individualized taper because permanent visual loss or stroke from associated arteritis can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Fever or hypothermia, chills, source pain/redness/drainage, cough, dysuria, diarrhea, rash, mucosal lesions, lymph nodes, mental-status change, hypotension, tachypnea, or immunosuppression cues reveal infection risk.

  • Escalate sepsis signs, neutropenic fever, rapidly spreading infection, airway swelling, meningitis signs, or shock.

Diagnostics

  • ESR and CRP with CBC, metabolic, thyroid, CK, and medication context

  • clinical response to an appropriate low-dose glucocorticoid supports but does not prove diagnosis

  • immediate giant-cell-arteritis symptom screen at every evaluation

  • Distinguish Polymyalgia rheumatica from elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis, myositis, hypothyroidism, statin myopathy, malignancy, and infection by joints, strength, CK, serology, and systemic findings.

Labs

  • WBC may rise or fall

  • lactate may rise with sepsis

  • CRP/ESR may rise with inflammation

  • organ labs can worsen in severe infection

Treatments

  • use the lowest effective glucocorticoid regimen with slow individualized taper

  • protect bone, glucose, blood pressure, infection, and eye health

  • treat suspected giant-cell arteritis with emergency high-dose therapy and specialist evaluation

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for visual symptoms or jaw claudication, severe new headache, neurologic deficit, aortic pain, fever with immunosuppression, or symptoms that worsen despite adequate treatment because GCA, infection, malignancy, or an alternative inflammatory diagnosis needs urgent action.

  • Assess bilateral shoulder and hip-girdle pain, morning stiffness duration, ability to dress and rise, fever, weight change, joint swelling, true muscle weakness, and ESR or CRP because PMR causes inflammatory stiffness rather than primary muscle damage and mimics must be reconsidered when the pattern is atypical.

  • Ask at every contact about new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw or tongue claudication, visual change, constitutional symptoms, and limb claudication because giant cell arteritis can coexist with PMR and cause irreversible blindness or large-vessel injury.

  • Administer glucocorticoid on the prescribed dose and taper and track symptom function and inflammatory markers without abrupt withdrawal because a rapid initial response supports the working diagnosis while individualized tapering limits relapse and adrenal suppression.

  • Monitor blood pressure, glucose, weight, mood, sleep, infection, eyes, skin, and bone protection and encourage graded strengthening and weight-bearing activity when safe because even low-dose long-term glucocorticoids can cause diabetes, osteoporosis, cataract, infection, and deconditioning.

Complications

  • permanent visual loss or stroke from associated arteritis

  • glucocorticoid infection can complicate Polymyalgia rheumatica.

  • fracture can complicate Polymyalgia rheumatica.

  • diabetes can complicate Polymyalgia rheumatica.

  • adrenal suppression can complicate Polymyalgia rheumatica.

  • relapse during taper can complicate Polymyalgia rheumatica.

Red Flags

  • Transient or persistent vision loss, diplopia, or new visual blurring

  • New severe headache, scalp tenderness, jaw or tongue claudication

  • Focal neurologic deficit, limb ischemia, or sudden chest, back, or abdominal pain

  • Fever, toxic appearance, true progressive weakness, or poor response to appropriate glucocorticoid

Patient Education

  • Report any new headache, scalp pain, jaw fatigue while chewing, or vision change the same day because these may be giant cell arteritis even when shoulder stiffness is controlled.

  • Follow the exact steroid taper and bone-protection plan and never stop suddenly because relapse and adrenal insufficiency can occur even after symptoms improve quickly.

Nclex Traps

  • New headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, or visual symptoms are an emergency

  • Normal CK helps distinguish pain and stiffness from destructive myositis
