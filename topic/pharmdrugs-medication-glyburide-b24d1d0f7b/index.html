Used To Treat

Adjunct to nutrition and activity for adults with type 2 diabetes who retain functioning pancreatic beta cells. Micronized and conventional glyburide formulations are not milligram-for-milligram bioequivalent. It is not used for type 1 diabetes or DKA.

Mechanism

Glyburide binds beta-cell SUR1 and closes the Kir6.2-containing ATP-sensitive potassium channel. Membrane depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels; calcium influx triggers insulin-vesicle fusion and secretion. Because the drug pharmacologically bypasses glucose-dependent KATP gating, insulin release can outlast a meal and continue as glucose falls. Hepatic metabolism produces metabolites with glucose-lowering activity that rely partly on renal elimination, explaining prolonged or recurrent hypoglycemia in kidney dysfunction. The drug requires functioning beta cells and does not correct absent insulin or ketogenesis in type 1 diabetes.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: All sulfonylureas can produce severe hypoglycemia with seizure, coma, neurologic injury, and death. Glyburide's duration and active metabolites make recurrence especially important in older, malnourished, renally impaired, or hepatically impaired patients. Hospital observation and repeated glucose assessment may be needed after severe exposure; dextrose alone can provoke more insulin release in overdose, so toxicology-directed octreotide may be required.

Contraindications

  • Type 1 diabetes, DKA, or active hypoglycemia

  • Hypersensitivity to glyburide/product components

  • Avoid or use a safer alternative in older adults and significant renal impairment because prolonged hypoglycemia is difficult to reverse

Nursing Essentials

  • After a severe low, continue serial glucose checks because the apparent recovery can end when rescue carbohydrate is consumed but glyburide remains active.

  • Verify conventional versus micronized product; they have different absorption and should not be exchanged by equal milligrams.

Interactions

  • Azole antifungals, fluoroquinolones, warfarin, alcohol, and many protein-binding/metabolic interactions can increase or destabilize glucose effects.

  • Beta blockers and other antiadrenergic agents can mask warning symptoms.

  • Insulin or other secretagogues add hypoglycemia; bosentan with glyburide increases liver-enzyme risk and is avoided.

Key Labs

  • Frequent glucose after symptoms/overdose and routine A1c

  • eGFR/creatinine and hepatic function

  • Electrolytes, mental status, meal intake, and recurrence window during severe hypoglycemia

Nclex Traps

  • One normal glucose after treatment does not end a glyburide emergency.

  • Glyburide and glipizide share SUR1 but not the same duration or renal safety profile.

  • Do not substitute micronized and conventional tablets one-for-one.

Description

Glyburide, also called glibenclamide, is a potent second-generation sulfonylurea that drives endogenous insulin release. Its longer action and active metabolites make hypoglycemia more persistent than with glipizide, especially when renal clearance is reduced, meals are missed, or the patient is older, frail, or malnourished. A first carbohydrate treatment may raise glucose temporarily while drug-driven insulin secretion continues, so recurrence is a defining danger. This is why glyburide is generally avoided in older adults and used cautiously or replaced in kidney impairment.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe, prolonged, or recurrent hypoglycemia

  • Weight gain, nausea, fullness, heartburn, rash, pruritus, and photosensitivity

  • Rare cholestatic jaundice/hepatitis, cytopenias, hyponatremia, severe skin reaction, and G6PD-related hemolysis
