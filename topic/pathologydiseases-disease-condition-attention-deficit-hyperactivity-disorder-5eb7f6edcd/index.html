Plain Language

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is a developmental condition that causes persistent problems with attention, organization, impulse control, or activity level that are out of step with a person's age and interfere in more than one part of life. ADHD begins in childhood and can continue through adolescence and adulthood.

Why It Matters

Untreated ADHD can contribute to injury, unsafe driving, school or job loss, substance misuse, conflict, missed health care, and low self-worth. Accurate multisetting assessment matters because sleep loss, anxiety, trauma, learning disorders, mood episodes, substances, and medical conditions can resemble ADHD and need different care.

Definition

DSM-5-TR ADHD is a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with function or development. Symptoms persist at least six months; several were present before age 12; several occur in two or more settings; and the pattern is not better explained by another disorder. DSM uses at least six symptoms in a domain through age 16 and at least five from age 17 onward. ICD-11 also requires childhood onset, cross-situational symptoms, and meaningful impairment but uses its own diagnostic requirements. A rating scale, poor grades, restlessness, or one distracted visit is not sufficient.

Pathology

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition involving variable regulation of attention, inhibition, motivation, time, working memory, and activity. It is not defined by laziness, poor parenting, a single brain scan, or one neurotransmitter level. Performance can improve during high-interest or urgent tasks and still be impaired during routine demands; that variability does not make symptoms intentional.

Pathophysiology

  • Difficulties sustaining effort, holding goals in mind, estimating time, inhibiting immediate responses, and shifting attention can disrupt multistep tasks even when knowledge and intelligence are intact.

  • Sleep loss, stress, sensory load, learning demands, mood symptoms, and substance exposure can amplify the same attention and impulse-control systems and must be assessed rather than assumed to be ADHD.

Etiology

ADHD is strongly influenced by genetic and developmental factors. Prematurity, selected prenatal and perinatal exposures, brain injury, sleep and environmental stress may contribute or modify expression, but no single factor explains every case. Sugar intake or parenting style does not independently cause ADHD.

Risk Factors

  • A first-degree relative with ADHD or another neurodevelopmental condition

  • Preterm birth, low birth weight, selected prenatal exposures, or neurologic injury

  • Coexisting learning, language, autism, tic, sleep, anxiety, depression, substance, or disruptive-behavior conditions

  • High-demand transitions that expose previously compensated organization and time-management difficulty

Signs Symptoms

  • Losing necessary items, missing details, drifting from tasks, forgetting responsibilities, poor time awareness, or difficulty organizing and completing multistep work

  • Fidgeting, internal restlessness, excessive talking or movement, difficulty waiting, interrupting, or acting before considering danger or consequences

  • A longstanding pattern across home, school, work, relationships, driving, finances, or health care with impairment beyond what age and context explain

  • Emotional dysregulation, rejection sensitivity, sleep difficulty, low confidence, and inconsistent performance may accompany ADHD but are not by themselves diagnostic criteria

Diagnostics

  • Obtain a developmental and longitudinal history plus information from more than one setting, such as the patient, caregivers, school, partner, or work records when appropriate and consented.

  • Use DSM or ICD requirements and validated rating scales as structured evidence, not as stand-alone diagnosis; assess functional impairment and examples, not symptom counts alone.

  • Screen for learning and language disorders, autism, tics, sleep apnea, anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, trauma, substance use, seizures, vision or hearing problems, and other medical explanations.

  • For adults first presenting later in life, establish evidence of childhood-onset symptoms while considering unavailable records and collateral; genuinely new adult inattention requires another explanation.

Labs

  • No blood test, genetic panel, EEG, imaging study, or continuous performance test independently confirms ADHD.

  • Check hearing, vision, sleep, medication and substance exposure, and use targeted thyroid, hematologic, metabolic, toxicology, neurologic, or other evaluation only when clinically indicated.

  • Before medication, document height and weight, pulse, blood pressure, cardiovascular history, current medicines, substance and diversion risk, and mental-health baseline; ECG is indication-dependent rather than universally required.

Assessment

Describe concrete impairment and baseline across school or work, home tasks, relationships, finances, driving, medication adherence, sleep, and self-care. Ask about childhood history, learning and language, mood elevation, anxiety, trauma, substances, eating, tics, seizures, cardiac symptoms and family sudden death, self-harm, suicide, risky behavior, and access to controlled medication. Record strengths and environmental supports as well as deficits.

Differential

  • Expected developmental activity, situational boredom, inadequate instruction, language difference, intellectual developmental disorder, specific learning disorder, autism, or developmental language disorder

  • Anxiety, major depression, bipolar mania or hypomania, PTSD, psychosis, oppositional behavior, substance use, or medication effect

  • Insufficient sleep, sleep apnea, restless legs, seizures, hearing or vision loss, thyroid disease, anemia, pain, traumatic brain injury, or another neurologic or medical condition

  • Acute delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, or new cognitive decline, which is not developmental ADHD

Treatments

  • Use environmental modifications, predictable routines, task breakdown, reminders, organizational coaching, school or workplace accommodations, and collaborative goal tracking across the lifespan.

  • For preschool-aged children, evidence-based parent training in behavior management is first-line; medication is considered only when impairment remains significant after appropriate behavioral intervention and specialist evaluation.

  • For school-aged children and adolescents, combine FDA-approved medication with parent training and classroom intervention when indicated. Representative options include methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine, or guanfacine extended release; selection depends on age, indication, duration needs, comorbidity, misuse risk, and adverse effects.

  • Adults may benefit from medication and structured ADHD-focused psychological support after environmental modifications and a specialist diagnostic assessment; titrate to functional benefit with tolerable adverse effects.

Contraindications

  • Do not start a stimulant during an acute psychotic or manic episode, and do not use it with an MAOI or within the product-label washout period.

  • Do not prescribe or continue a controlled stimulant without assessing cardiovascular history, current substances, misuse, diversion, secure storage, and the actual formulation's label and legal requirements.

  • Do not abruptly stop guanfacine extended release; taper under the prescribing plan because rebound hypertension can occur.

  • Do not use medication to replace needed learning, language, sleep, trauma, family, school, or workplace assessment.

Safety Considerations

  • Monitor symptom target and function along with appetite, weight, growth in children, sleep, pulse, blood pressure, mood activation, psychosis, tics, and adverse effects at titration and follow-up.

  • Stimulants carry abuse, misuse, addiction, and diversion risks; use secure storage, no sharing, prescription reconciliation, and formulation-specific counseling.

  • Guanfacine can cause sedation, hypotension, bradycardia, syncope, and conduction problems; check pulse and blood pressure and avoid dehydration or unsafe driving until effects are known.

  • Assess self-harm, suicide, dangerous driving, substance use, exploitation, and accidental injury rather than assuming risk comes only from hyperactivity.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm the prescribed formulation, timing, last dose, effect window, adherence, secure storage, and whether medicine is being shared, lost, sold, or taken differently than prescribed.

  • Measure and trend age-appropriate weight, height, pulse, blood pressure, sleep, appetite, mood, tics, and functional benefit; report sustained concerning change according to the treatment plan.

  • Give brief written and verbal instructions, one task at a time, with teach-back, reminders, low-distraction space, and a concrete follow-up plan.

  • Coordinate caregivers, school, work, behavioral treatment, pharmacy, and prescriber while protecting adolescent and adult confidentiality and autonomy.

Red Flags

  • Chest pain, exertional syncope, sustained marked tachycardia, severe hypertension, new arrhythmia, seizure, or acute focal neurologic change

  • New psychosis, mania, severe agitation, suicidal intent, dangerous intoxication, or rapidly escalating risky behavior

  • Suspected stimulant overdose, diversion, coercion, substance combination, or inability to store controlled medication safely

  • Severe weight loss, dehydration, prolonged inability to sleep, repeated fainting, or symptomatic bradycardia or hypotension with guanfacine

Complications

  • Academic failure, unemployment, financial loss, relationship conflict, missed care, low self-worth, anxiety, depression, and substance misuse

  • Motor-vehicle crashes, unsafe sex, accidental injury, legal problems, or medication misuse and diversion

  • Growth or weight effects, cardiovascular symptoms, sleep disruption, mood activation, psychosis, or rebound hypertension from poorly monitored medication

  • Delayed diagnosis of learning, language, sleep, mood, trauma, substance, neurologic, or medical conditions when all inattention is labeled ADHD

Prognosis

ADHD often continues into adulthood, although symptom form and support needs change. Evidence-based treatment can substantially improve safety, learning, work, relationships, and self-management. The goal is reliable function and reduced harm, not constant productivity or suppression of normal personality and energy.

Prevention

  • Identify developmental, sleep, hearing, vision, learning, language, and mental-health concerns early and provide school or workplace support before repeated failure.

  • Teach driving, substance, sexual-health, financial, medication-storage, and transition planning at developmentally appropriate stages.

  • Review medication at least annually and after major developmental, school, work, pregnancy, cardiovascular, or substance-use changes.

Patient Education

  • ADHD is a developmental condition, not laziness or lack of intelligence; symptoms must be longstanding, impairing, and present in more than one setting.

  • A rating-scale score supports assessment but does not diagnose ADHD by itself.

  • Take the exact prescribed formulation; do not share, crush, change timing, or stop guanfacine suddenly, and store controlled medicines securely.

  • Report chest pain, fainting, severe mood change, hallucinations, suicidal thoughts, major weight loss, or misuse promptly.

Special Populations

  • Preschool children need parent-training-first care; school-aged children need classroom and family intervention alongside any medication plan.

  • Adolescents require confidential assessment of substance use, diversion, driving, sexual risk, mood, sleep, and transition to adult services.

  • Adults, women, and people with predominantly inattentive symptoms may be missed; assess childhood history, caregiving and work demands, hormonal and sleep context, and compensatory effort.

  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding, congenital heart disease, hypertension, epilepsy, tics, autism, intellectual disability, and substance-use history require individualized specialist risk-benefit review rather than automatic exclusion or routine prescribing.

Nclex Traps

  • ADHD is not diagnosed from one rating scale, one setting, or new adult-onset inattention.

  • A normal ability to focus on a highly interesting activity does not exclude ADHD.

  • Tics naturally wax and wane and are not an automatic reason to stop effective ADHD treatment; assess timing, impairment, and alternatives.

  • Guanfacine is not stopped abruptly, and stimulant monitoring includes misuse, sleep, appetite, growth, pulse, blood pressure, and mood.
