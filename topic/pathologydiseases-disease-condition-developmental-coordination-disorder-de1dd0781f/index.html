Plain Language

Developmental coordination disorder is a condition that makes learning and carrying out coordinated movements much harder than expected for a person's age and opportunity to practice. Everyday tasks such as dressing, handwriting, using utensils, sports, driving, or work skills can be slow, effortful, or inaccurate.

Why It Matters

DCD can cause falls, injury, school difficulty, reduced physical activity, low confidence, bullying, and loss of independence, yet it is often dismissed as clumsiness. Nurses should recognize persistent functional impact, screen for neurologic red flags and coexisting ADHD or learning problems, and connect the person with task-specific occupational or physical therapy.

Definition

DSM-5-TR developmental coordination disorder requires acquisition and execution of coordinated motor skills substantially below age expectations despite opportunity for skill learning, with clumsiness, slowness, or inaccuracy that significantly interferes with daily living, school, work, leisure, or play; onset is in the early developmental period and the difficulty is not better explained by intellectual disability, visual impairment, or a neurologic motor condition. ICD-11 uses Developmental Motor Coordination Disorder with its own required features. A screening questionnaire, messy handwriting, or poor sports performance alone does not diagnose DCD.

Pathology

DCD is a neurodevelopmental motor-learning and coordination condition rather than muscle weakness from disuse or one visible brain lesion. Timing, prediction, feedback, balance, posture, and learning of new motor sequences may be inefficient in varied combinations. Strength and intelligence may be normal, and performance changes with task novelty, fatigue, attention, environment, and practice.

Pathophysiology

  • Difficulty building and updating internal movement plans can make new actions slow, variable, and effortful and increase reliance on conscious attention.

  • Avoidance after repeated failure reduces practice and fitness and can amplify fatigue, anxiety, weight gain, and participation loss without being the original cause.

Etiology

DCD has no single known cause. Genetic and developmental factors, prematurity, low birth weight, and coexisting neurodevelopmental conditions may contribute. The diagnosis requires that cerebral palsy, progressive neuromuscular disease, ataxia, visual impairment, intellectual disability, injury, and other explanations do not better account for the motor pattern.

Risk Factors

  • Prematurity, very low birth weight, or early developmental motor delay

  • ADHD, autism, developmental language disorder, specific learning disorder, or another neurodevelopmental condition

  • Persistent difficulty learning age-expected self-care, ball, bicycle, handwriting, keyboard, tool, or workplace skills despite practice

  • Avoidance of physical activity, repeated falls, fatigue, low confidence, or bullying related to motor performance

Signs Symptoms

  • Delayed or effortful gross-motor skills such as running, jumping, catching, balancing, swimming, or riding a bicycle

  • Slow or inaccurate fine-motor tasks such as buttons, shoelaces, utensils, handwriting, drawing, typing, or tool use

  • Frequent dropping, bumping, spilling, tripping, or difficulty learning a new movement sequence

  • Daily tasks require unusually high concentration and time and deteriorate with fatigue or divided attention

  • School, work, play, exercise, self-care, confidence, or social participation is meaningfully affected

Diagnostics

  • Use developmental and medical history, direct observation, standardized motor assessment by qualified clinicians, and evidence of real-world interference from the person, caregivers, school, or work.

  • Confirm adequate opportunity for learning and examine neurologic, musculoskeletal, visual, hearing, cognitive, and adaptive function; assess the person's cultural and environmental expectations.

  • Screen for ADHD, autism, language and learning disorders, anxiety, depression, pain, joint problems, and physical-activity barriers.

  • Questionnaires can identify risk and track function but do not replace examination, valid motor testing, and exclusion of another neurologic or medical cause.

Labs

  • No blood test, imaging study, genetic test, or EEG confirms DCD.

  • Use vision and hearing assessment and targeted neurologic, musculoskeletal, metabolic, genetic, or imaging evaluation when history, regression, weakness, tone, reflexes, pain, or examination is atypical.

  • Normal imaging does not prove DCD; diagnosis rests on the developmental motor pattern, functional impact, and exclusion of a better explanation.

Assessment

Ask which real tasks are difficult, how much time and effort they require, what has been practiced, and what environmental barriers exist. Examine gait, balance, tone, strength, reflexes, asymmetry, joint pain or hypermobility, vision, fine and gross motor function, handwriting or tool use, and fatigue. Assess falls, swimming and traffic safety, feeding and self-care, school or work impact, ADHD, learning, language, mood, bullying, and any loss of acquired skills.

Differential

  • Cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, peripheral neuropathy, ataxia, movement disorder, spinal or orthopedic condition, or traumatic brain injury

  • Vision impairment, vestibular disorder, hearing impairment, pain, joint hypermobility, medication effect, or severe deconditioning

  • Intellectual developmental disorder, autism, ADHD, developmental language disorder, specific learning disorder, or inadequate opportunity to learn the task

  • Acute or progressive motor loss, which is not developmental coordination disorder and requires medical evaluation

Treatments

  • Use person-centered occupational or physical therapy focused on meaningful real tasks, goal setting, repeated practice, feedback, and adaptation of the person, task, and environment.

  • Task-oriented approaches such as Cognitive Orientation to daily Occupational Performance or neuromotor task training may be used; evidence supports functional practice but does not establish one universal program for every person.

  • Provide school or workplace accommodations for handwriting, typing, time, physical education, equipment, seating, fatigue, and safe participation while continuing skill building.

  • No medication treats DCD. Treat confirmed ADHD, anxiety, pain, or another coexisting condition separately and encourage enjoyable, achievable physical activity.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose DCD without checking for progressive weakness, abnormal tone or reflexes, focal findings, vision loss, pain, or another neurologic condition.

  • Do not force unsafe competitive activities, punish slow motor performance, or use exercise as humiliation.

  • Do not rely only on nonspecific process or sensory exercises while ignoring practice of the person's actual functional goals.

  • Do not prescribe medication for the coordination disorder itself or assume ADHD treatment will directly teach motor skills.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess falls, stairs, traffic, water safety, burns, cutting tools, cooking, medication handling, driving, sports, and workplace hazards according to age and tasks.

  • Adapt activity to build fitness without repeated injury or humiliation; monitor pain, fatigue, obesity risk, and reduced participation.

  • Screen for bullying, anxiety, depression, low self-worth, school refusal, and self-harm when motor difficulty causes exclusion.

  • Escalate new weakness, loss of skill, abnormal neurologic findings, or rapidly worsening coordination.

Nursing Priorities

  • Compare current motor function with developmental and personal baseline and identify the exact self-care or safety tasks affected.

  • Allow extra time for dressing, eating, writing, transfers, and procedures; offer adaptive equipment without taking over tasks the person can do safely.

  • Assess falls, pain, fatigue, neurologic red flags, vision, medication effects, and coexisting ADHD, language, learning, anxiety, and mood concerns.

  • Coordinate occupational therapy, physical therapy, school or work supports, and primary or developmental care around measurable functional goals.

Red Flags

  • Sudden or progressive loss of walking, hand use, speech, swallowing, continence, or another acquired motor skill

  • Asymmetric weakness, abnormal tone or reflexes, new ataxia, severe headache, seizure, altered mental status, or significant head or spinal trauma

  • Frequent dangerous falls, drowning risk, choking, severe pain, or inability to complete essential self-care safely

  • Suicidal intent, severe depression, abuse, or bullying with immediate danger

Complications

  • Falls, fractures, sports or workplace injury, poor handwriting access, self-care dependence, and reduced driving or vocational options

  • Physical inactivity, low fitness, obesity, pain, fatigue, and avoidable exclusion from recreation

  • Anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, bullying, social isolation, school avoidance, and employment difficulty

  • Delayed diagnosis of a progressive neurologic or neuromuscular condition when worsening is mislabeled as DCD

Prognosis

DCD often persists into adolescence and adulthood, although skills and impact change with practice, environment, task choice, and support. Task-specific intervention and accommodations can improve independence, participation, confidence, and safety even when some coordination differences remain.

Prevention

  • Identify persistent motor and self-care difficulty early and support safe practice before avoidance and low confidence become entrenched.

  • Adapt physical education and recreation so the person can build fitness, competence, and enjoyment without humiliation.

  • Plan transitions for handwriting, technology, driving, work tools, home skills, and adult rehabilitation access.

Patient Education

  • DCD is a developmental coordination condition, not laziness or lack of intelligence.

  • Therapy works best when it practices meaningful real-life tasks and adapts the environment as needed.

  • A questionnaire can suggest DCD but diagnosis also requires motor assessment, functional impact, and exclusion of another cause.

  • New weakness or loss of an established skill is not typical DCD and needs prompt medical review.

Special Populations

  • Young children need developmentally appropriate observation and repeated assessment because motor opportunities and expectations change rapidly.

  • People with ADHD, autism, language or learning disorders may need integrated goals and communication adaptations rather than separate conflicting plans.

  • Adolescents and adults need support for driving, exercise, vocational tasks, fatigue, pain, self-care, and disclosure or accommodations.

  • People with visual, intellectual, neuromuscular, orthopedic, or acquired neurologic conditions require careful differential assessment before applying the DCD label.

Nclex Traps

  • DCD is not diagnosed from clumsiness or poor handwriting alone.

  • Normal intelligence and strength do not exclude DCD, but progressive weakness or focal findings require another evaluation.

  • Medication does not treat DCD; task-specific OT or PT and accommodations address function.

  • Do not remove all independence in the name of safety; adapt tasks and allow time while protecting from actual hazards.
