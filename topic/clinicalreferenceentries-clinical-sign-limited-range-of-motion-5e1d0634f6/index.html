Definition

Limited range of motion is a reduced ability to move a joint or body region through its expected safe arc. Active range is movement performed by the patient; passive range is movement produced by the examiner while the patient relaxes.

Plain Language

Limited range of motion means a joint or body part cannot move as far as expected. Comparing what the person can move with what can be moved gently for them helps identify the type of problem.

Why It Matters

Limited movement affects self-care, mobility, transfers, work, and fall risk. Restriction of active motion alone may reflect pain or weakness, while restriction of both active and passive motion raises concern for joint, effusion, contracture, or mechanical restriction; sudden loss after trauma or with a hot swollen joint needs urgent assessment.

Associated Conditions

  • pain, joint effusion, inflammation, contracture, weakness, tendon or muscle injury, joint destruction, mechanical block, or neurologic dysfunction

Assessment Method

Ask the patient to perform safe active movement first, then assess gentle passive movement only when appropriate, comparing sides and baseline; document the exact plane, degrees when useful, pain location, endpoint, strength, swelling, crepitus, instability, and whether active, passive, or both ranges are restricted.

Positive Finding

The joint or body region cannot move through the expected safe arc for the patient's baseline, with the limitation clearly identified as active, passive, or both.

Mechanism

Pain can inhibit effort, weakness can limit active motion, and effusion, inflammation, shortened tissue, joint damage, or a mechanical block can restrict passive as well as active movement.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Limited range of motion finding raises concern for pain, joint effusion, inflammation, contracture, weakness, tendon or muscle injury, joint destruction, mechanical block, or neurologic dysfunction because pain can inhibit effort, weakness can limit active motion, and effusion, inflammation, shortened tissue, joint damage, or a mechanical block can restrict passive as well as active movement. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Limited range of motion does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Limited range of motion to focus evaluation of pain, joint effusion, inflammation, contracture, weakness, tendon or muscle injury, joint destruction, mechanical block, or neurologic dysfunction and the reviewed alternatives: pain-limited motion, weakness, poor effort, mechanical block. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask the patient to perform safe active movement first, then assess gentle passive movement only when appropriate, comparing sides and baseline; document the exact plane, degrees when useful, pain location, endpoint, strength, swelling, crepitus, instability, and whether active, passive, or both ranges are restricted. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Limited range of motion as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Baseline anatomy, pain tolerance, effort, fear, positioning, prior surgery, measurement method, and examiner technique affect range; a normal single-plane test does not exclude functional limitation. Therefore, Limited range of motion cannot confirm or exclude pain, joint effusion, inflammation, contracture, weakness, tendon or muscle injury, joint destruction, mechanical block, or neurologic dysfunction by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • pain-limited motion

  • weakness

  • poor effort

  • mechanical block

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as pain-limited motion, weakness, poor effort, mechanical block.

False Negative Situations

Limited range of motion may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of pain, joint effusion, inflammation, contracture, weakness, tendon or muscle injury, joint destruction, mechanical block, or neurologic dysfunction.

Modern Status

Current functional finding; comparing active with passive range helps localize the problem but does not diagnose the cause, and expected range varies by joint, age, anatomy, and prior condition. Safety Precautions

Observe Limited range of motion with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Ask the patient to perform safe active movement first, then assess gentle passive movement only when appropriate, comparing sides and baseline; document the exact plane, degrees when useful, pain location, endpoint, strength, swelling, crepitus, instability, and whether active, passive, or both ranges are restricted. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Never force motion after trauma or through severe pain, resistance, or instability; acute loss of motion with deformity, a hot swollen joint, neurovascular change, new focal weakness, or suspected cervical or spinal injury requires urgent assessment and protection of the involved area.
