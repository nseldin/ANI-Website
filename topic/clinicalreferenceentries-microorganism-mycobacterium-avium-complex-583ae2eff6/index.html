Definition

Mycobacterium avium complex is a clinically important bacterium associated with chronic pulmonary MAC disease, disseminated MAC in advanced immunosuppression, and cervical lymphadenitis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Mycobacterium avium complex is a Gram-reaction indeterminate bacillus with a obligate aerobe oxygen profile. It is acid-fast, not known to form spores, nonmotile, and facultative intracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

  • Content

    No routine person-to-person isolation is needed; prevention focuses on immune restoration where applicable and individualized environmental-risk counseling. The reviewed route description is the reviewed waterborne transmission and environmental exposure transmission route categories. Environmental exposure is common and the interval to pulmonary or disseminated disease cannot usually be identified. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Environmental mycobacteria persist within macrophages and biofilms; structural lung disease or impaired cellular immunity permits progressive pulmonary or disseminated infection. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Mycobacterium avium complex is associated with chronic pulmonary MAC disease, disseminated MAC in advanced immunosuppression, and cervical lymphadenitis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.
