Definition

Systolic heart failure develops when impaired left-ventricular contraction reduces ejection fraction and activates sympathetic and renin-angiotensin responses that initially support pressure but promote remodeling and congestion. The characteristic pattern is fatigue, cool extremities, displaced apical impulse, S3, dyspnea, edema, and reduced ejection fraction. Confirm or stage it with echocardiography for ejection fraction, valves, wall motion, and chamber structure; distinguish it from preserved-ejection-fraction failure, lung disease, anemia, and deconditioning by echo, congestion, perfusion, and cause workup. The priority is to relieve acute congestion and stabilize perfusion because pulmonary edema can complicate Systolic heart failure. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • echocardiography for ejection fraction, valves, wall motion, and chamber structure

  • electrocardiogram, ischemic evaluation, natriuretic peptide, chest imaging, electrolytes, kidney function, and iron studies

  • cause assessment for coronary disease, toxin, myocarditis, arrhythmia, genetic, endocrine, or valvular disease

  • Distinguish Systolic heart failure from preserved-ejection-fraction failure, lung disease, anemia, and deconditioning by echo, congestion, perfusion, and cause workup.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • relieve acute congestion and stabilize perfusion

  • use the guideline-directed medication pillars and consider specific device therapy after eligibility review: an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) for primary sudden-death prevention in selected nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy or ischemic heart disease with LVEF at most 35%, NYHA class II or III symptoms on chronic guideline-directed therapy, at least 40 days after myocardial infarction for ischemic heart disease, and expected meaningful survival longer than 1 year; or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) for selected patients with LVEF at most 35%, sinus rhythm, left bundle branch block, QRS duration at least 150 ms, and NYHA class II, III, or ambulatory class IV symptoms on guideline-directed therapy

  • individualize device decisions when rhythm, conduction pattern, pacing need, comorbidity, frailty, post-infarction timing, or goals of care differ from these representative criteria

  • treat ischemic, valvular, rhythm, toxin, and adherence drivers and support rehabilitation

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for pink frothy sputum, rapidly rising oxygen need, hypotension with cool skin, new confusion, oliguria, syncope, chest pain, or sustained arrhythmia because pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock may be developing.

  • Assess dyspnea, orthopnea, lung sounds, jugular venous pressure, edema, weight, blood pressure, rhythm, perfusion, and functional change because reduced ejection and congestion affect both lungs and organ blood flow.

  • Measure daily weight on the same scale and track intake, output, urine response, and edema because weight trend detects retained fluid earlier and more reliably than appearance alone.

  • Administer prescribed diuretic and guideline-directed heart-failure medicines while monitoring blood pressure, potassium, magnesium, and kidney function because therapies improve congestion and remodeling but can cause hypotension or electrolyte injury.

  • Reconcile sodium, fluid, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, and adherence risks and coordinate pharmacy and heart-failure follow-up because common self-management barriers precipitate preventable decompensation.

Complications

  • pulmonary edema can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • cardiogenic shock can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • kidney injury can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • arrhythmia can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • stroke can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • sudden death can complicate Systolic heart failure.

  • progressive functional mitral regurgitation

  • hyperkalemia can complicate Systolic heart failure.

Red Flags

  • Pink frothy sputum or rapidly worsening pulmonary edema

  • Hypotension with cool skin, confusion, or oliguria

  • New chest pain, syncope, or sustained arrhythmia

  • Rapid weight gain with severe orthopnea despite prescribed diuretic

Patient Education

  • Teach daily weight, symptom and swelling checks, the individualized sodium and fluid plan, and the exact weight or breathing change that should trigger a call.

  • Explain that feeling better does not mean heart-failure medicines should be stopped; many protect the heart over time even when they do not produce an immediate sensation.

Nclex Traps

  • A better ejection fraction does not automatically justify stopping effective chronic therapy

  • Do not judge congestion from ankle edema alone
