Definition

Prerenal AKI is reduced filtration caused by inadequate effective renal perfusion while the kidney tissue is initially structurally intact. It is reversible when the hemodynamic problem is corrected early, but persistent hypoperfusion can progress to ischemic tubular injury.

Pathology

The glomeruli and tubules are not necessarily damaged at onset. Lower afferent flow and glomerular pressure reduce GFR, while sympathetic, RAAS, and ADH responses make intact tubules avidly reclaim sodium, urea, and water.

Pathophysiology

A fall in true or effective arterial filling activates baroreceptors, sympathetic tone, renin, aldosterone, and ADH. Afferent prostaglandin dilation and angiotensin-II-mediated efferent tone try to preserve glomerular pressure, while proximal and distal sodium-water reclamation lowers urine sodium and volume. These adaptations explain concentrated urine and a disproportionate BUN rise in some cases, but sepsis, CKD, diuretics, glucosuria, and mixed injury often blur the classic pattern. If low oxygen delivery persists, energy-dependent tubular cells fail and the syndrome transitions from functional hypofiltration to structural acute tubular injury.

Etiology

Causes include hemorrhage, GI or renal fluid loss, poor intake, sepsis-related vasodilation, low cardiac output, cirrhosis, nephrotic low-effective-volume physiology, renal-artery disease, excessive diuresis, and altered afferent or efferent tone from NSAIDs, RAAS blockade, or other drugs during vulnerable perfusion states.

Risk Factors

  • Vomiting, diarrhea, hemorrhage, burns, fever, poor intake, or excessive diuresis

  • Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, or severe systemic vasodilation

  • Renal-artery stenosis or a solitary functioning kidney

  • NSAID plus RAAS blockade plus diuretic exposure during acute illness

Signs Symptoms

  • Thirst, dry mucosa, orthostasis, tachycardia, poor skin perfusion, or low JVP with true volume loss

  • Edema, ascites, JVD, or pulmonary congestion despite low effective arterial filling

  • Oliguria or creatinine rise after illness or medication change

  • Symptoms of bleeding, infection, pump failure, or liver decompensation

Diagnostics

  • Assess the complete perfusion and congestion examination

  • Trend response to correction while avoiding an unsafe blind fluid challenge

  • Review urinalysis and sediment for a bland pattern versus emerging tubular or glomerular injury

  • Exclude obstruction and consider cardiac, hepatic, vascular, or septic causes

Labs

  • BUN/creatinine ratio may rise but lacks sufficient specificity to diagnose the cause

  • Low urine sodium or FeNa can reflect sodium avidity but also occurs in several intrinsic states

  • FeUrea is sometimes used during diuretic exposure but also has important limitations

  • Lactate, hemoglobin, liver findings, BNP context, and infection markers may identify the systemic driver

Treatments

  • Stop bleeding or replace true volume loss with the appropriate fluid or blood strategy

  • Treat sepsis, low cardiac output, cirrhosis physiology, or venous congestion rather than treating the creatinine alone

  • Review NSAIDs and hemodynamically active medicines during acute instability

  • Reassess frequently for transition to tubular injury or development of fluid overload

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess vomiting, diarrhea, poor intake, bleeding, fever, sepsis, burns, heart failure, cirrhosis, medications, blood pressure, perfusion, and volume findings because prerenal AKI begins with inadequate effective kidney blood flow.

  • Trend urine output, creatinine, BUN, blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, weight, lactate, electrolytes, lung sounds, and edema because restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion and evolving intrinsic injury.

  • Administer prescribed balanced crystalloid, blood products, antibiotics, or vasopressor according to the cause because hemorrhage, fluid loss, distributive shock, and low cardiac output require different perfusion rescue.

  • Review nephrotoxins and medicines that alter renal autoregulation with the treating team because prolonged hypoperfusion plus medication stress can convert reversible prerenal dysfunction into tubular injury.

  • Escalate for refractory hypotension, anuria, pulmonary edema, severe hyperkalemia, worsening acidosis, pericarditic pain, or encephalopathy because urgent critical care or dialysis assessment may be required.

Complications

  • Ischemic acute tubular injury

  • Shock and multiorgan hypoperfusion

  • Pulmonary edema from over-resuscitation

  • Hyperkalemia, acidosis, and medication accumulation if filtration continues to fall

Contraindications

  • Do not equate edema with adequate effective arterial filling

  • Do not use low FeNa as proof of dehydration

  • Avoid routine fluid loading in pulmonary congestion or cardiogenic physiology

Red Flags

  • Refractory hypotension, rising lactate, altered mental status, or shock

  • Anuria or rapidly falling urine output despite perfusion correction

  • Severe hyperkalemia with ECG changes or refractory metabolic acidosis

  • Pulmonary edema, pericarditic pain, seizure, or uremic encephalopathy

Patient Education

  • Explain that prerenal AKI means the kidneys initially receive too little effective blood flow; correcting the cause early can restore filtration before structural tubular damage develops.

  • Teach patients to seek advice during vomiting, diarrhea, fever, poor intake, or bleeding and not to stop or restart blood-pressure, diuretic, or kidney-risk medicines without an individualized plan.

Nclex Traps

  • Prerenal means a perfusion mechanism, not necessarily visible dehydration

  • Heart failure and cirrhosis can cause prerenal physiology while the patient is swollen

  • Persistent prerenal AKI can become ATN

  • A fluid bolus is not the universal answer
