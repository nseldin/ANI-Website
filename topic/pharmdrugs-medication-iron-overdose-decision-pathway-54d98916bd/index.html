Used To Treat

Acute iron ingestion with gastrointestinal injury, systemic toxicity, significant timed iron concentration, shock, acidosis, altered mental status, or retained tablets. Deferoxamine is the specific chelator, used as an adjunct to aggressive resuscitation and selected decontamination.

Description

Iron overdose injures in stages and can appear deceptively quiet between early gastrointestinal symptoms and later shock or hepatic failure. The clinically relevant dose is elemental iron, not the total salt weight, so ferrous sulfate, gluconate, fumarate, prenatal vitamins, and multivitamins cannot be compared tablet for tablet. Free iron directly burns gastrointestinal mucosa, disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, causes capillary leak and vasodilation, and injures liver and other organs. A timed serum concentration helps but is not a stand-alone clearance test: sampling too early, too late, after delayed-release products, or after deferoxamine can mislead. Visible radiopaque tablets may support whole-bowel irrigation when the airway and bowel are safe.

Mechanism

When transferrin binding is exceeded, circulating non-transferrin-bound iron catalyzes free-radical injury, interferes with cellular energy production, and causes vasomotor collapse. Deferoxamine binds ferric iron to form ferrioxamine, a water-soluble complex cleared mainly in urine. Chelation removes labile iron but cannot instantly reverse bowel necrosis, shock, or hepatic injury, and orange-red urine is neither required to start treatment nor a reliable endpoint. Whole-bowel irrigation is physical removal of unabsorbed tablets, not an antidote, and conventional hemodialysis does not efficiently remove tissue-bound iron itself.

Administration Timing

  • Call Poison Help immediately, calculate elemental iron from the exact product, assess for corrosive GI symptoms and shock, obtain an appropriately timed serum iron concentration and abdominal imaging when indicated, and repeat testing when timing or formulation makes one result unreliable.

  • Begin deferoxamine under the FDA label and toxicology protocol for systemic toxicity or other accepted criteria. Use the correct route and infusion rate for the patient's condition; rapid IV administration can cause hypotension, so titration and monitoring are part of antidote safety.

  • Consider whole-bowel irrigation for a substantial radiopaque tablet burden only when bowel function is intact and the airway is protected. Escalate early for severe acidosis, shock, GI hemorrhage, hepatic injury, or declining urine output rather than waiting for classic staging.

Nursing Essentials

  • Photograph the bottle and determine elemental iron per tablet or milliliter, number missing, formulation, co-ingredients, time, and patient weight. Prenatal products can contain enough iron to endanger a small child.

  • Trend blood pressure, perfusion, mental status, emesis or GI bleeding, urine output, glucose, and hepatic findings. Independently verify deferoxamine weight, rate, total daily exposure, renal function, and duration.

  • During lavage or whole-bowel irrigation, watch for aspiration, obstruction, ileus, distention, and electrolyte or fluid problems; stop and escalate when decontamination becomes more dangerous than retained tablets.

Key Labs

  • Timed serum iron, blood gas, bicarbonate, anion gap, glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, AST, ALT, bilirubin, PT/INR, CBC, lactate, and serial clinical assessment. Abdominal radiography can detect many tablets but a negative image does not exclude ingestion.

  • Total iron-binding capacity is unreliable as an overdose severity surrogate, especially at high concentrations. Interpret serum iron by time, symptoms, formulation, and whether chelation has begun.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause corrosive vomiting and diarrhea, GI bleeding, shock, metabolic acidosis, coma, hepatic failure, coagulopathy, kidney injury, later obstruction from scarring, and death.

  • Deferoxamine can cause hypotension with rapid infusion, hypersensitivity, pulmonary toxicity with prolonged high exposure, kidney injury, neurotoxicity, visual or auditory effects, and increased susceptibility to selected infections such as Yersinia and mucormycosis.

Contraindications

  • Do not rely on induced vomiting, charcoal, or a negative radiograph: charcoal binds iron poorly, emesis is hazardous, and not all iron is radiopaque.

  • Severe renal disease and anuria complicate ferrioxamine elimination and require specialist planning, but they do not justify abandoning a critically poisoned patient without an alternative rescue plan.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Shock, persistent acidosis, altered mental status, recurrent GI bleeding, rising INR or aminotransferases, hypoglycemia, oliguria, or large retained burden requires ICU, toxicology, gastroenterology or surgery, and possibly transplant-center consultation. Dialysis may support renal failure or remove circulating chelate in selected cases but is not a substitute for timely chelation.

Interactions

  • Vitamin products may contain acetaminophen or other minerals that need separate assessment. Antacids or food can alter absorption but do not neutralize a dangerous ingestion.

  • Prochlorperazine is contraindicated with deferoxamine in labeling because prolonged unconsciousness has been reported; other nephrotoxic or ototoxic exposures complicate monitoring.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Young children

    Note

    A few adult-strength tablets can deliver a large elemental dose per kilogram; treat missing pills as time sensitive even before symptoms.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Prenatal-product access is a common exposure scenario; maternal shock and acidosis threaten the fetus, and chelation decisions require toxicology and obstetric collaboration.

  • Population

    Kidney disease

    Note

    Because kidney dysfunction reduces ferrioxamine clearance and makes fluid-based decontamination harder to tolerate, monitor urine output and coordinate nephrology early to detect oliguria and limit ferrioxamine or fluid accumulation.

Evidence Limitations

  • Serum thresholds and chelation duration vary with timing, symptoms, formulation, and laboratory method. The FDA label supplies dosing boundaries, but real overdose treatment requires poison-center interpretation rather than a universal concentration-only rule.

Nclex Traps

  • Calculate elemental iron, not the weight of the iron salt.

  • Activated charcoal does not meaningfully bind iron.

  • A symptom-free latent interval does not prove recovery, and orange urine is not required to diagnose toxicity or prove adequate chelation.

Regulatory Status

Deferoxamine is FDA-approved as an adjunct to standard measures for acute iron intoxication. Whole-bowel irrigation is a selective decontamination procedure with limited outcome evidence, not an FDA-approved antidote and not routine for every ingestion.

Boxed Warning

IRON OVERDOSE WARNING: bloody vomiting, severe abdominal pain, lethargy, shock, acidosis, or a child with missing iron tablets requires 911 and Poison Help. Calculate elemental iron and obtain time-aware studies because serum iron can misrepresent severity when sampling is mistimed or absorption is delayed; do not substitute charcoal or home vomiting for monitored deferoxamine and selected bowel decontamination because charcoal binds iron poorly and induced emesis can cause aspiration.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
