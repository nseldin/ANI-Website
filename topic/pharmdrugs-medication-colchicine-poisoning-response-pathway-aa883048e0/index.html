Used To Treat

Acute or cumulative colchicine toxicity. No commercially available proven antidote exists; care depends on early poison-center-directed decontamination, aggressive fluid and organ support, serial marrow and metabolic surveillance, and early critical-care escalation because microtubule disruption can cause progressive gastrointestinal injury, delayed marrow failure, shock, and multiorgan dysfunction.

Description

Colchicine is a microtubule-disrupting alkaloid that injures intestinal, marrow, cardiac, and other metabolically active cells in poisoning, so early vomiting and diarrhea can precede delayed shock, marrow failure, and multiorgan collapse rather than mark a harmless GI illness. Leukocytosis may precede profound marrow suppression, and survivors can later develop rebound leukocytosis and alopecia. The dose associated with severe toxicity varies widely with timing, renal and hepatic function, chronic use, and CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein interactions, so a reported amount cannot replace serial clinical assessment. Hemodialysis is ineffective for toxin removal because colchicine distributes widely and binds tissues. Colchicine-specific Fab remains investigational or unavailable and must not be presented as an accessible antidote.

Mechanism

Colchicine binds beta-tubulin and prevents microtubule polymerization. Cells then cannot maintain mitosis, intracellular transport, secretion, cytoskeletal structure, or normal leukocyte function. Rapidly dividing GI epithelium and marrow are injured first, while myocardial depression, vasoplegia, capillary leak, hepatic injury, neuromuscular toxicity, and electrolyte loss drive later collapse. Supportive care replaces lost volume and electrolytes, supports circulation and ventilation, treats infection and bleeding during marrow failure, and bridges organs while surviving cells recover; it does not neutralize tissue-bound colchicine.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 and Poison Help for any intentional, symptomatic, pediatric, or uncertain significant ingestion. Assess airway and shock, obtain early ECG and laboratory baselines, and consider activated charcoal only when the exposure is early and airway and GI conditions are safe.

  • Do not induce vomiting or apply the older label's gastric-lavage language as a universal instruction. Any single- or multidose charcoal, aspiration, or lavage decision must be individualized by a medical toxicologist because vomiting, ileus, mucosal injury, and aspiration can make decontamination more harmful.

  • Admit significant or symptomatic exposures for serial monitoring and escalate before hypotension, dysrhythmia, respiratory failure, or cytopenias become established. Use vasopressors, ventilation, blood products, antimicrobial care, renal support, and advanced mechanical support by physiology; none is a colchicine antidote.

Nursing Essentials

  • Reconcile acute amount plus chronic dosing, renal and hepatic disease, recent macrolides or azoles, cyclosporine, verapamil or diltiazem, protease inhibitors, grapefruit, and other CYP3A4 or P-gp inhibitors. Therapeutic dosing can become toxic after an interaction.

  • Measure and replace GI fluid and electrolyte losses carefully while trending hemodynamics and pulmonary status, because capillary leak and cardiac dysfunction increase the risk that aggressive replacement will worsen pulmonary or peripheral edema.

  • Trend CBC with differential, platelets, ECG, core temperature, glucose, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, creatinine, liver tests, CK, lactate, blood gas, coagulation, urine output, and signs of infection or bleeding for delayed changes.

Key Labs

  • Serial CBC and differential, platelets, electrolytes, renal and hepatic function, glucose, CK, blood gas, lactate, coagulation, ECG, and infection studies. A normal initial count does not exclude impending marrow failure.

  • Colchicine concentrations are not routinely timely or validated to direct bedside care. Dose-per-kilogram reports describe risk groups but do not create a safe cutoff for an individual patient.

Adverse Effects

  • Toxicity can cause corrosive-like GI symptoms, hypovolemia, electrolyte derangement, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, sepsis, DIC, rhabdomyolysis, neuropathy, ARDS, cardiogenic or distributive shock, liver and kidney failure, dysrhythmia, alopecia, and death.

  • Charcoal can cause aspiration or obstruction; vasopressors and mechanical support add ischemic, bleeding, and circuit risks. These are rescue harms to balance, not reasons to wait through deterioration.

Contraindications

  • Do not use hemodialysis as though it clears colchicine; use renal replacement for kidney failure, acidosis, electrolyte, or fluid indications. Do not promise colchicine Fab, because it is not routinely available or approved.

  • Do not interpret early diarrhea as self-limited gastroenteritis or discharge based on a normal first CBC after a meaningful exposure.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent vomiting or diarrhea, hypotension, rising lactate, dysrhythmia, respiratory distress, falling counts, coagulopathy, weakness, or organ injury requires ICU, medical toxicology, and early advanced-support consultation. Deterioration can accelerate during the second phase even after GI symptoms lessen.

Interactions

  • Strong CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors can sharply increase exposure, especially in renal or hepatic impairment; some combinations are contraindicated in labeling because fatal toxicity has occurred.

  • Statins, fibrates, cyclosporine, and other myotoxic drugs add neuromuscular and rhabdomyolysis risk. Dialysis does not compensate for inhibited metabolism and tissue distribution.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    A small number of adult tablets can be lethal; all exploratory ingestions need immediate poison-center assessment and secure medication storage.

  • Population

    Kidney or liver impairment

    Note

    Reduced clearance and interaction sensitivity make therapeutic or modest excess dosing dangerous; dialysis does not efficiently remove colchicine.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal shock and multiorgan failure threaten the fetus; no proven antidote exists, so rapid maternal stabilization and obstetric-critical-care coordination are essential.

Evidence Limitations

  • The FDA label describes phases and no known specific antidote but includes older gastric-lavage language that should not be generalized without modern poison-center review. Fab fragments, G-CSF, hemoperfusion, ECMO, and other adjuncts have case-level or experimental evidence rather than proven routine benefit.

Nclex Traps

  • Early vomiting and diarrhea can be the first phase of fatal multiorgan poisoning, not evidence that the drug was expelled.

  • Hemodialysis does not effectively remove colchicine.

  • A normal early CBC can precede marrow aplasia; trend it.

Regulatory Status

No FDA-approved colchicine-overdose antidote exists. Colchicine-specific Fab is not routinely available or approved; decontamination and all organ-support adjuncts are specialist-directed supportive care.

Boxed Warning

COLCHICINE WARNING: early vomiting or diarrhea can precede shock, marrow failure, and death. Call 911 and Poison Help, review CYP3A4/P-gp interactions and kidney or liver disease, and monitor serially; dialysis does not clear colchicine and experimental Fab must not be promised as an available antidote.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
