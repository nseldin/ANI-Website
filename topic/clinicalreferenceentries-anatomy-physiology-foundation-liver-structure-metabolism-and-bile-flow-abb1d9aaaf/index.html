Summary

The liver is a metabolically active organ in the right upper abdomen that receives nutrient-rich portal venous blood and oxygenated hepatic arterial blood, processes both through sinusoids, and drains blood through hepatic veins. Hepatocytes regulate fuels, synthesize plasma proteins and clotting factors, transform ammonia and many drugs, process bilirubin, and secrete bile. Its organization explains why liver disease can simultaneously affect glucose, coagulation, drug handling, fluid distribution, cognition, digestion, and portal pressure.

Quick Answer

Portal venous and hepatic arterial blood enter microscopic hepatic units and mix in sinusoids, where hepatocytes exchange substances with plasma and Kupffer cells remove selected material. Blood flows toward central veins and ultimately hepatic veins; bile flows in the opposite general direction through canaliculi toward bile ducts, gallbladder, and duodenum. The liver stores or releases glucose, handles amino acids and lipids, makes albumin and many coagulation proteins, converts ammonia to urea, conjugates bilirubin, and modifies endogenous compounds and medicines. Injury markers such as AST and ALT are not the same as functional measures such as INR, albumin, glucose maintenance, and clinical detoxification.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The liver lies mainly beneath the right diaphragm and is divided grossly into lobes and functionally into vascular and biliary territories. The portal vein supplies most inflow volume from intestine, spleen, and pancreas; the hepatic artery provides oxygen-rich arterial inflow. Branches run with bile ducts in portal tracts. Fenestrated sinusoids pass between plates of hepatocytes toward central veins, then hepatic veins and the inferior vena cava. Kupffer macrophages line sinusoids, while stellate cells in the space of Disse store vitamin A and can produce fibrosis when activated. Hepatocyte canaliculi collect bile and lead toward progressively larger ducts, opposite the predominant direction of sinusoidal blood.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: metabolism and synthesis

    Text

    After a meal, insulin and substrate availability favor glycogen formation, lipid synthesis, and protein building; during fasting, the liver releases glucose through glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis. It packages and clears lipoproteins, converts amino nitrogen to urea, and makes albumin, transport proteins, and many coagulation and anticoagulation factors. Enzyme systems transform lipid-soluble compounds into forms that can be excreted, although metabolism may activate, inactivate, or toxify a substance. First-pass extraction means an oral drug may be substantially changed before reaching systemic blood. These jobs require viable hepatocytes, oxygen, substrates, and intact blood and bile flow; congestion or shock can impair them without primary biliary disease.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: bilirubin and bile flow

    Text

    Heme breakdown produces unconjugated bilirubin, which travels bound to albumin and cannot be excreted directly in urine. Hepatocytes take it up, conjugate it to improve water solubility, and secrete it into bile. Bile acids made from cholesterol help emulsify and absorb dietary fat, are concentrated in the gallbladder between meals, enter the duodenum, and are largely recovered in the terminal ileum for enterohepatic recycling. Obstruction raises conjugated bilirubin and limits bile reaching intestine, contributing to dark urine, pale stool, itching, and fat malabsorption. Excess production, impaired uptake or conjugation, hepatocyte injury, and obstruction create different bilirubin patterns.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Portal delivery connects intestinal absorption directly to hepatic processing; pancreatic hormones help determine whether the liver stores or mobilizes fuel. Bile acids recycle through ileum and portal blood, while cholecystokinin coordinates gallbladder contraction with a meal. The liver buffers glucose, but severe failure can cause hypoglycemia because stores and synthesis are inadequate. Albumin helps maintain plasma oncotic pressure and transports many compounds. Portal pressure depends on flow and resistance through hepatic tissue. Chronic inflammation and stellate-cell activation deposit scar, distort sinusoids, raise portal resistance, and create collateral vessels. The liver also influences immunity, hormone metabolism, iron handling, and drug exposure, so dysfunction crosses organ-system boundaries.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Hepatocyte injury may release aminotransferases, but widespread loss impairs synthesis and metabolism. Reduced albumin contributes to edema and ascites alongside portal hypertension, renal sodium retention, and hemodynamic changes. Reduced coagulation-factor synthesis prolongs clotting tests, yet cirrhosis can also reduce anticoagulants and increase thrombosis risk; it is not simple auto-anticoagulation. Portal hypertension produces splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, varices, and ascites. Ammonia and other neurotoxins, inflammation, and altered brain signaling contribute to hepatic encephalopathy. Cholestasis retains bile constituents and impairs fat absorption. Acute massive injury can produce hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, coagulopathy, cerebral edema, kidney injury, and multiorgan failure.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Acetaminophen overdose can overwhelm safe conjugation and generate toxic metabolite injury; timely antidotal therapy is mechanism-based and should not await dramatic symptoms. Alcohol, viral hepatitis, metabolic fatty liver disease, autoimmunity, ischemia, congestion, and toxins injure through different pathways. Lactulose reduces intestinal ammonia absorption and rifaximin alters ammonia-producing gut organisms in selected encephalopathy care; neither replaces evaluation of bleeding, infection, constipation, dehydration, or sedatives that precipitated decline. Diuretics may treat ascites but can worsen kidney function or electrolytes. Many drugs need hepatic dose or interaction review, while abrupt avoidance of every medication is also unsafe. Cholestatic and gallstone disease involve bile flow but require disease-specific assessment.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    AST and ALT primarily indicate hepatocellular injury; alkaline phosphatase and GGT help recognize a cholestatic pattern. Total and direct bilirubin help localize processing problems. INR or PT can reflect rapid change in hepatic synthetic capacity but is affected by vitamin K, anticoagulants, and laboratory context. Albumin changes slowly and is also altered by inflammation, renal or gastrointestinal loss, and dilution. Platelets may fall with portal hypertension. Ammonia can support selected reasoning but does not grade encephalopathy reliably by itself. Ultrasound with Doppler assesses anatomy, bile ducts, portal and hepatic flow; elastography estimates stiffness; CT, MRI, endoscopy, and biopsy answer narrower questions. Trends and clinical status matter more than one enzyme value.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess mentation and sleep pattern, asterixis, jaundice, itching, abdominal girth, ascites, edema, weight, intake, stool and urine color, bleeding, infection, pain, glucose, medicines, alcohol and supplement exposure, and hemodynamics. Compare neurologic findings with baseline because subtle inattention may precede coma. Track intake, output, daily weight, sodium-related teaching, and response to diuretics as ordered without equating rapid weight loss with safe improvement. Use bleeding and fall precautions appropriate to actual risk, protect fragile skin, support nutrition, and avoid sedatives or hepatotoxic products unless specifically evaluated. After paracentesis or invasive procedures, monitor circulation, leakage, bleeding, and infection according to the plan.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Emergency evaluation is required for new confusion or reduced consciousness, hematemesis or melena, hypotension, rapidly increasing abdominal pain or distention, fever with ascites, severe hypoglycemia, new major bleeding, jaundice with sepsis features, or suspected overdose. Acute liver failure can evolve even when the patient initially looks well; worsening INR, encephalopathy, acidosis, kidney injury, or hypoglycemia is dangerous. Fever and abdominal tenderness in ascites may indicate spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. Right-upper-quadrant pain, fever, jaundice, and instability suggest obstructed infected bile flow. Do not attribute altered mentation automatically to ammonia when intracranial, infectious, medication, glucose, oxygenation, and other causes remain possible.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Detoxification does not mean the liver harmlessly removes every toxin; metabolism can generate reactive products. High AST or ALT does not directly measure remaining liver function, and normal enzymes do not exclude advanced cirrhosis. A prolonged INR in liver disease does not guarantee protection from thrombosis. Albumin alone is not a nutrition diagnosis. Unconjugated bilirubin is not normally found in urine because it is albumin-bound; conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble. The portal vein is a vein carrying nutrient-rich but relatively oxygen-poor blood, not an artery. Bile and blood travel through different channels and generally opposite directions within the lobular organization.
