Definition

Transposition of the great arteries develops when the aorta arises from the right ventricle and pulmonary artery from the left, creating parallel rather than serial circulations unless blood can mix. The characteristic pattern is profound neonatal cyanosis with limited oxygen response, often with little respiratory distress and a single loud second heart sound. Confirm or stage it with urgent echocardiography to define ventriculoarterial connections and mixing sites; distinguish it from persistent pulmonary hypertension, truncus arteriosus, tetralogy, pulmonary atresia, and lung disease by echo anatomy and mixing physiology. The priority is to maintain ductal patency with prostaglandin because severe hypoxemia can complicate Transposition of the great arteries. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • urgent echocardiography to define ventriculoarterial connections and mixing sites

  • preductal and postductal saturation, blood gas, lactate, perfusion, and organ-function assessment

  • catheterization when balloon atrial septostomy is needed or anatomy remains uncertain

  • Distinguish Transposition of the great arteries from persistent pulmonary hypertension, truncus arteriosus, tetralogy, pulmonary atresia, and lung disease by echo anatomy and mixing physiology.

Labs

  • do not apply adult ranges without checking age

  • glucose, bilirubin, CBC, and electrolytes often require age-specific interpretation

Treatments

  • maintain ductal patency with prostaglandin

  • perform balloon atrial septostomy when atrial mixing is inadequate

  • complete early arterial-switch repair with coronary transfer in appropriate anatomy

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for profound cyanosis, apnea, rising lactate, severe acidosis, shock, or declining consciousness because emergency atrial septostomy or other cardiac rescue may be required.

  • Administer prescribed prostaglandin infusion continuously because ductal patency may improve mixing between otherwise parallel circulations.

  • Assess cyanosis, respiratory effort, pulses, perfusion, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, lactate, glucose, and temperature because worsening mixing causes systemic hypoxia and acidosis.

  • Prepare airway support for prostaglandin-related apnea because ventilation may be needed while circulation remains duct dependent.

  • Coordinate urgent echocardiography and catheter or surgical intervention because oxygen alone cannot correct severely inadequate intracardiac mixing.

Complications

  • severe hypoxemia can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

  • acidosis can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

  • shock can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

  • neurologic injury can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

  • coronary insufficiency can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

  • arrhythmia can complicate Transposition of the great arteries.

Red Flags

  • Profound cyanosis that does not improve with oxygen

  • Rising lactate or severe metabolic acidosis

  • Apnea during prostaglandin therapy

  • Shock, weak pulses, or declining consciousness

Patient Education

  • Explain that the major arteries are connected to opposite ventricles, so survival depends on blood mixing until repair.

  • Teach caregivers the cardiac team's feeding, medication, color, breathing, and follow-up plan after stabilization or surgery.

Nclex Traps

  • Oxygen alone cannot correct two parallel circuits without a mixing connection

  • A restrictive atrial septum can make deterioration immediate despite a patent ductus
