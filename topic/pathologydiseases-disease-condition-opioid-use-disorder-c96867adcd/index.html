Plain Language

Opioid use disorder is a treatable illness in which opioid use becomes hard to control and continues despite harm or serious risk. It can involve prescription opioids, heroin, illicit fentanyl, or other opioids; physical dependence from correctly prescribed treatment alone is not the diagnosis.

Why It Matters

Opioid use disorder can cause fatal breathing suppression, infection, injury, pregnancy complications, and recurrent overdose, especially after tolerance falls. Effective medications greatly reduce illicit opioid use and death risk, so recognizing the disorder should lead to treatment and naloxone access rather than a detox-only plan or stigma.

Definition

Opioid use disorder is a substance use disorder defined by a problematic pattern of opioid use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. DSM-5-TR diagnosis requires at least 2 of 11 features within a 12-month period, with severity based on the number present. Tolerance and withdrawal occurring only during appropriate medical treatment do not count by themselves; intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, or a positive drug test also does not independently establish the disorder.

Pathology

There is no single structural lesion or laboratory result that confirms opioid use disorder. Repeated exposure can produce tolerance, physical dependence, craving, cue-triggered use, and impaired control while respiratory depression, constipation, endocrine effects, injection injury, infection, and hypoxic brain or organ injury reflect the opioid, route, co-exposures, and episodes of toxicity.

Pathophysiology

  • Opioids activate mu-opioid and related receptors in reward, stress, pain, and breathing networks. Repeated exposure produces neuroadaptation, so stopping can cause withdrawal while returning to use after reduced tolerance can cause overdose at an amount previously survived.

  • Opioid intoxication typically causes sedation, small pupils, and slowed breathing, but pupil size is not reliable enough to rule toxicity in or out. Overdose is the dangerous end of intoxication and is defined clinically by impaired ventilation and consciousness, not by the diagnosis of opioid use disorder.

  • Withdrawal commonly causes craving, anxiety, yawning, sweating, runny nose, tearing, enlarged pupils, gooseflesh, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle aches, and insomnia. It is usually not directly fatal in a healthy adult, but dehydration, pregnancy, serious comorbidity, unsafe self-treatment, and rapid return to use can create major risk.

Etiology

Risk develops through an interaction of opioid exposure, potency and route, pain and medical treatment, genetic and neurobiologic vulnerability, trauma, psychiatric illness, social isolation, housing and economic instability, incarceration or treatment transitions, and drug-supply conditions. No amount of prescribed use, social circumstance, or one overdose proves the diagnosis, and the disorder is not a moral failure.

Risk Factors

  • Prior opioid overdose, escalating or nonmedical opioid use, fentanyl exposure, injection use, or a prior opioid use disorder

  • Loss of tolerance after abstinence, hospitalization, incarceration, residential treatment, or stopping medication

  • Concurrent alcohol, benzodiazepine, sedative, or other respiratory-depressant use; lung, liver, renal, or sleep-related breathing disease

  • Chronic pain, trauma, depression, suicide risk, unstable housing, limited treatment access, and pregnancy or postpartum transitions

Signs Symptoms

  • Using more or longer than intended, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, substantial time obtaining or recovering, craving, and continued use despite physical, emotional, relationship, work, school, or legal harm

  • Hazardous use, giving up important activities, inability to meet obligations, tolerance, or withdrawal when those pharmacologic features are not solely from appropriate supervised treatment

  • Intoxication: sleepiness, slowed speech or movement, impaired attention, small pupils, snoring or gurgling, and slowed or irregular breathing

  • Withdrawal: anxiety, restlessness, sweating, yawning, runny nose, tearing, enlarged pupils, gooseflesh, aches, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, and insomnia

Diagnostics

  • Ask privately and nonjudgmentally about each opioid, prescribed and nonprescribed source, route, frequency, last use, fentanyl exposure, overdose, naloxone response, withdrawal, prior treatment, pain, pregnancy possibility, other substances, suicide risk, and the 11 DSM-5-TR use-disorder features during the same 12 months.

  • Confirm at least 2 qualifying features and clinically significant impairment or distress; document mild, moderate, or severe only from the criterion count. Do not diagnose from tolerance or withdrawal caused solely by appropriate medical therapy.

  • Assess current intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, infection, trauma, pain, and psychiatric emergency separately because each changes the immediate plan. Use a validated withdrawal scale as a repeated clinical measure when appropriate, not as proof of the disorder.

  • Use toxicology to answer a defined clinical question and interpret the assay, detection window, prescribed medicines, fentanyl coverage, and possible false results. A positive, negative, or unexpected result does not replace history, examination, or consent-based discussion.

Labs

  • No laboratory test diagnoses opioid use disorder. In suspected overdose, bedside glucose, oxygenation and ventilation assessment, ECG, electrolytes, renal and liver measures, blood gas, creatine kinase, and imaging are selected from the presentation and co-exposures.

  • Offer HIV, hepatitis B and C, pregnancy, and sexually transmitted infection testing according to route, exposure, consent, and local guidance; assess injection sites, skin and soft tissue, murmurs, fever, and focal infection symptoms.

  • Before and during medication treatment, review organ function, interactions, ECG risk when relevant, pregnancy, current opioids, and product-specific monitoring rather than applying one universal panel.

Assessment

First assess responsiveness, airway, respiratory rate and effort, oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, glucose, temperature, trauma, co-ingestants, and need for naloxone and emergency support. Once stable, distinguish intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, pain, suicide attempt, and chronic disorder; then assess diagnostic criteria, treatment readiness, prior effective medication, overdose risk, infection, pregnancy, supports, and barriers to uninterrupted care.

Differential

Differentiate opioid intoxication from hypoglycemia, head injury, stroke, seizure, sepsis, carbon monoxide exposure, and other sedative poisoning; opioid withdrawal from gastroenteritis, influenza-like illness, anxiety, stimulant effects, and withdrawal from another substance. Distinguish opioid use disorder from expected physical dependence during appropriate pain or palliative treatment, while recognizing that prescribed use and a use disorder can coexist.

Treatments

  • Offer medication treatment with methadone, buprenorphine, or extended-release or oral naltrexone according to current opioid exposure, patient preference, access, pregnancy, contraindications, and the setting. Methadone and buprenorphine are opioid agonist treatments; naltrexone is an antagonist and requires an opioid-free transition.

  • Do not make counseling attendance or successful withdrawal a blanket prerequisite for medication. Pair medication with respectful recovery support, behavioral treatment when wanted, pain and psychiatric care, housing and social support, infection prevention, and continuity across hospital, jail, rehabilitation, and community transitions.

  • Treat overdose immediately with airway positioning, ventilation support, emergency response, and naloxone; repeat naloxone and observe according to clinical response, opioid duration, recurrence of sedation, and local protocol.

  • Treat withdrawal with a medication-based opioid use disorder plan when possible rather than withdrawal management alone. Symptom medicines may support selected complaints but do not provide the mortality protection or ongoing treatment of methadone or buprenorphine.

Contraindications

  • Do not start naltrexone while a patient is using opioids, is physiologically opioid-dependent, or is in acute opioid withdrawal; verify an adequate opioid-free interval and product-specific safety to avoid precipitated withdrawal.

  • Do not give routine full doses of an opioid agonist to a heavily sedated or inadequately ventilating patient; stabilize toxicity and use a monitored, protocol-based plan.

  • Do not abruptly discontinue effective methadone or buprenorphine because of pregnancy, hospitalization, incarceration, or benzodiazepine use. Co-use raises respiratory risk and requires coordinated risk management, not automatic denial of treatment.

  • Do not use a short detoxification episode, a negative urine test, or patient refusal of counseling as evidence that ongoing evidence-based medication is unnecessary.

Safety Considerations

  • Provide naloxone, teach rescue breathing and emergency activation, and include family or close supports with consent. More than one naloxone dose may be needed, and improvement can wear off before the opioid does.

  • Warn that tolerance falls quickly and overdose risk rises after abstinence, detoxification, hospitalization, incarceration, stopping agonist medication, or stopping naltrexone. Avoid using alone and avoid mixing opioids with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives.

  • Monitor sedation, breathing, QT and interaction risks when relevant, adherence, diversion concerns without punitive discharge, ongoing exposure, injection complications, and suicidal intent.

  • Use person-first, nonstigmatizing language and protect confidentiality. Fear of punishment can delay disclosure of pregnancy, fentanyl exposure, relapse, or overdose.

Nursing Priorities

  • In suspected overdose, stimulate and assess the patient, support airway and ventilation, administer naloxone per protocol, call emergency support, monitor continuously, and prepare for recurrent respiratory depression.

  • Document exact substances, route, timing, co-exposures, prescribed therapy, response to naloxone, withdrawal findings, pain, infection signs, pregnancy, suicide risk, and the patient’s treatment preference.

  • Verify the medication, formulation, last dose, dispensing program or prescriber, and transition plan before holding, duplicating, or restarting methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone.

  • Before discharge, confirm medication access, naloxone in hand when possible, safer-use teaching, wound and infection care, follow-up timing, transportation, and a plan for cravings or return to use.

Red Flags

  • Slow, irregular, or absent breathing; cyanosis; inability to awaken; gurgling; recurrent sedation after naloxone; or suspected fentanyl or long-acting opioid exposure

  • Chest pain, severe hypoxia, aspiration, pulmonary edema, seizure, focal neurologic deficit, major trauma, hyperthermia, or mixed overdose

  • Fever with injection-site infection, new murmur, severe back pain, focal weakness, severe headache, or other concern for invasive infection

  • Pregnancy with withdrawal, reduced fetal movement, bleeding, labor symptoms, overdose, or inability to access established medication treatment; suicidal intent or inability to remain safe

Complications

  • Fatal or nonfatal overdose, hypoxic brain injury, aspiration, pulmonary edema, rhabdomyolysis, pressure injury, and acute kidney injury

  • HIV, viral hepatitis, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, skin or soft-tissue infection, and vascular injury related to unsafe injection

  • Constipation, sleep-related breathing problems, endocrine or sexual effects, pain sensitization, injury, and impaired work, housing, parenting, or relationships

  • Pregnancy complications, neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, suicide, stigma, treatment interruption, incarceration, and overdose after lost tolerance

Prognosis

Opioid use disorder is often recurrent but treatable. Staying on an effective medication is associated with better retention and lower illicit opioid use and death risk; stopping treatment can raise risk, especially when tolerance has fallen. Recovery goals and duration of medication are individualized rather than limited by an arbitrary endpoint.

Prevention

  • Use evidence-based pain care, discuss opioid risks and alternatives, review interactions and overdose risk, and prescribe only for a supported indication without abandoning patients who develop problems.

  • Expand low-barrier access to methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone when appropriate, naloxone, sterile supplies, infection services, and nonpunitive prenatal and primary care.

  • Plan medication continuity and overdose prevention before hospital, jail, detoxification, or residential-care discharge.

Patient Education

  • Opioid use disorder is a medical illness, and methadone or buprenorphine treatment is treatment—not replacing one addiction with another.

  • Call emergency services for slow or absent breathing, blue or gray color, or inability to wake. Give naloxone, support breathing, and stay because symptoms can return.

  • After any period without opioids, an amount once tolerated can now be fatal. Avoid mixing opioids with alcohol or sedatives and carry naloxone.

  • Do not stop or restart methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone on your own; timing and current opioid exposure matter.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy: methadone or buprenorphine is recommended treatment; rapid opioid stopping is not routine care. Coordinate obstetric and addiction care, continue treatment postpartum, and monitor the newborn for an expected and treatable withdrawal syndrome.

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate consent, confidentiality, family involvement when safe, overdose prevention, and assessment of school, exploitation, trafficking, trauma, and co-occurring mental illness.

  • Older adults and patients with lung, sleep, liver, renal, cardiac, or cognitive disease need slower, interaction-aware assessment and careful monitoring for sedation, falls, and medication accumulation.

  • Patients leaving incarceration, hospital, detoxification, or residential treatment need medication continuity and naloxone because lost tolerance sharply increases overdose risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Prescribed tolerance or withdrawal alone does not diagnose opioid use disorder.

  • Arousal after naloxone does not end monitoring; respiratory depression can recur.

  • Withdrawal management without ongoing treatment does not provide the same protection as medication for opioid use disorder.

  • Do not abruptly stop methadone or buprenorphine in pregnancy; coordinate ongoing care.
