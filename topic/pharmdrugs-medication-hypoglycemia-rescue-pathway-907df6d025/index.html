Used To Treat

Low blood glucose ranging from mild symptomatic episodes in an awake patient who can swallow to severe neuroglycopenia with seizure, unconsciousness, or inability to protect the airway. Route depends on neurologic status, access, cause, and risk of recurrence.

Description

Hypoglycemia rescue uses oral carbohydrate, the glucagon hormone, or intravenous dextrose to raise plasma glucose quickly; the safe route depends on consciousness, swallowing, IV access, and available hepatic glycogen. An awake, cooperative patient who can swallow generally receives measured rapid oral glucose, whereas an unconscious or seizing patient receives nothing by mouth because aspiration can kill. Glucagon is useful when IV access is unavailable because it tells the liver to release stored glucose. IV dextrose supplies glucose directly and works even when hepatic glycogen is depleted. Every rescue is temporary until the cause and recurrence window are addressed.

Mechanism

Oral dextrose is transported across the intestine and directly raises plasma glucose. IV dextrose bypasses absorption and delivers substrate immediately. Glucagon activates hepatic Gs-coupled glucagon receptors, raising cAMP and PKA activity, which stimulates glycogen phosphorylase and glycogenolysis while supporting gluconeogenesis. It therefore depends on usable liver glycogen and may respond poorly after prolonged fasting, severe malnutrition, adrenal insufficiency, chronic alcohol-related glycogen depletion, or advanced liver dysfunction. Once awake after glucagon, oral carbohydrate replenishes glycogen and helps prevent recurrence. Sulfonylurea or long-acting insulin exposure can outlast the rescue and require prolonged monitoring or additional therapy.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Airway and route come first: never force oral carbohydrate into a patient who cannot swallow safely. Glucagon can cause vomiting, so place the patient on the side when feasible and continue emergency support; lack of response requires IV glucose and evaluation rather than repeated delay.

Adverse Effects

  • Oral/IV glucose: rebound hyperglycemia, phlebitis or tissue injury with extravasation depending on concentration

  • Glucagon: nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, transient blood-pressure change, and rare hypersensitivity

  • Recurrent hypoglycemia after short-lived rescue when the causative drug or illness persists

Contraindications

  • No oral treatment when airway protection or swallowing is impaired.

  • Glucagon is contraindicated in pheochromocytoma and insulinoma because it can provoke catecholamine crisis or secondary insulin release.

  • Concentrated IV dextrose requires secure access and protocol-appropriate concentration to reduce tissue injury.

Nursing Essentials

  • Treat first, then recheck on schedule and identify why it happened: insulin timing, absent meal, renal decline, alcohol, sepsis, adrenal failure, or secretagogue exposure.

  • After severe sulfonylurea hypoglycemia, anticipate recurrence and toxicology-directed octreotide rather than assuming one dextrose bolus solved ongoing insulin secretion.

Interactions

  • Beta blockers may hide adrenergic hypoglycemia symptoms and can cause a transient BP/pulse rise with glucagon.

  • Acarbose blocks sucrose digestion, so use dextrose for oral rescue.

  • Warfarin effect may increase with glucagon; indomethacin may blunt the glucose response.

Key Labs

  • Immediate and serial glucose

  • Potassium, renal/hepatic function, cortisol, infection studies, drug levels or toxicology as the cause requires

  • Mental status, airway, recurrence timing, nutrition, and medication reconciliation

Nclex Traps

  • Glucagon mobilizes stored hepatic fuel; it does not contain glucose.

  • A normal glucose after rescue does not prove the danger has ended.

  • The 15-15 approach is for an appropriate awake patient, not for seizure, coma, or unsafe swallowing.
