Contraindications

  • Renal insufficiency and prior hypersensitivity

  • Avoid unsupervised use in G6PD deficiency

  • Persistent dysuria, fever, flank pain, pregnancy, male UTI, recurrent symptoms or hematuria requires cause-directed evaluation

Nursing Essentials

  • Explain before the first dose that urine/tears become orange-red and can stain clothing and soft contact lenses.

  • Obtain urine testing before dye obscures color-based interpretation when possible; reassess if symptoms persist rather than extending therapy.

Interactions

  • Can interfere with colorimetric urinalysis and chemistry tests.

  • Other oxidizing drugs can add methemoglobinemia/hemolysis risk.

  • Renal impairment is the major exposure interaction.

Key Labs

  • Renal function

  • Urinalysis and culture before or early in therapy

  • CBC, methemoglobin, bilirubin and liver tests if cyanosis, hemolysis or toxicity is suspected

Nclex Traps

  • Orange urine is expected dye excretion, not hematuria or proof of cure.

  • Phenazopyridine relieves symptoms but cannot replace an antibiotic or obstruction evaluation.

  • The 2-day rule answers evidence and diagnostic-masking concerns as well as toxicity vigilance.

Used To Treat

Brief symptomatic relief of lower-urinary-tract pain, burning, urgency and frequency caused by infection, instrumentation, surgery, trauma or catheter irritation while the underlying cause is diagnosed and treated.

Mechanism

Renal excretion concentrates parent drug and metabolites in urine, producing local mucosal analgesia and the characteristic orange-red color. The exact receptor or enzyme responsible for pain relief has not been established. Renal impairment allows parent drug and oxidizing metabolites to accumulate, increasing methemoglobinemia, sulfhemoglobinemia, hemolysis, renal and hepatic toxicity; G6PD deficiency reduces red-cell protection from oxidative injury.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Contraindicated in renal insufficiency. Do not exceed 2 days with UTI antibacterial therapy: this reflects absent evidence of added benefit after 2 days and the danger of masking persistent disease, not a guaranteed safe-to-toxic switch at hour 49. Cyanosis, dyspnea, fatigue or low pulse oximetry unresponsive to oxygen suggests methemoglobinemia.

Description

Phenazopyridine is an azo-dye urinary analgesic that is excreted into urine and reduces burning and pain by acting locally on irritated lower-tract mucosa. Its exact molecular target is unknown. It is not an antibiotic and does not cure a UTI. When used with antibacterial treatment for UTI, labeling limits it to 2 days because benefit beyond 2 days has not been shown and continued masking can delay recognition of treatment failure or another diagnosis.

Adverse Effects

  • Orange-red urine and tears with staining of fabric or contact lenses

  • Headache, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, rash or pruritus

  • Methemoglobinemia, sulfhemoglobinemia and hemolytic anemia, especially with overdose, renal impairment or G6PD deficiency

  • Renal/hepatic toxicity, jaundice and rare aseptic meningitis
