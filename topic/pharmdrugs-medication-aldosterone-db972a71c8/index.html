Class

Adrenal zona-glomerulosa mineralocorticoid hormone

Used To Treat

This is a hormone-physiology and interpretation card. It explains primary aldosteronism, hypoaldosteronism, adrenal insufficiency, type 4 renal tubular acidosis, the aldosterone-to-renin ratio, and why ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aliskiren, spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, or triamterene change sodium, potassium, pressure, and acid-base balance.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Aldosterone is an endogenous hormone, not a dispensed drug with a boxed warning. The high-alert issue is interpretation: severe hypertension with hypokalemia may indicate autonomous excess, while hyperkalemia with hypotension or acidosis may indicate deficiency, resistance, or medication blockade.

Mechanism

Aldosterone diffuses into principal cells in the late distal nephron and collecting duct and binds the intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor, NR3C2. The receptor complex enters the nucleus and increases serum/glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 and other proteins. SGK1 inhibits NEDD4-2-mediated removal of epithelial sodium channels, so more ENaC remains active on the apical membrane; basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase activity also rises. Sodium entry makes the tubular lumen relatively negative and increases potassium secretion through ROMK and BK channels. In alpha-intercalated cells, mineralocorticoid signaling supports hydrogen-ion secretion, helping explain the alkalosis of aldosterone excess and acidosis of deficiency. The mineralocorticoid receptor also binds cortisol, but 11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 normally converts cortisol to cortisone in target tissue, allowing aldosterone selectivity. Licorice-mediated 11-beta-HSD2 inhibition can therefore mimic mineralocorticoid excess even when aldosterone itself is suppressed.

Nursing Essentials

  • Connect the pattern before memorizing a number: high aldosterone plus suppressed renin suggests autonomous adrenal production; high aldosterone plus high renin suggests a secondary response to low renal perfusion, diuresis, or volume loss; low aldosterone with hyperkalemia suggests deficiency, impaired synthesis, or receptor/pathway blockade.

  • For possible primary aldosteronism, measure aldosterone and renin under the ordered protocol, check potassium at the same time, document posture, collection time, sodium intake, blood pressure, kidney function, and medications, and avoid inventing one universal ratio cutoff because assays and units differ.

Contraindications

  • This is a physiology card, so medication contraindications do not apply. Do not interpret aldosterone alone without renin, potassium, sodium status, posture, time of collection, kidney function, and interfering medications.

  • Do not assume a high aldosterone value proves primary aldosteronism: secondary RAAS activation can raise both renin and aldosterone. The defining primary pattern is aldosterone that is inappropriately high relative to suppressed renin.

Interactions

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aliskiren, beta blockers, diuretics, mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists, ENaC blockers, NSAIDs, sodium intake, potassium status, and kidney function can alter renin, aldosterone, or their ratio and may produce false reassurance or false suspicion.

  • Spironolactone and eplerenone block the mineralocorticoid receptor; amiloride and triamterene block ENaC downstream. Both strategies reduce sodium retention and potassium secretion, but they interrupt different steps.

Key Labs

  • Serum or plasma aldosterone, plasma renin activity or direct renin concentration, aldosterone-to-renin ratio, potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, creatinine/eGFR, blood pressure, and volume status. Interpret using the local assay and specialist protocol.

  • The 2025 Endocrine Society guideline emphasizes suppressed renin plus aldosterone that is inappropriately high relative to renin and warns that hypokalemia can falsely lower aldosterone; potassium should be corrected and testing repeated when appropriate.

Nclex Traps

  • Aldosterone retains sodium but wastes potassium and hydrogen. ADH primarily retains water; confusing those jobs makes sodium and osmolality questions much harder.

  • Primary aldosteronism suppresses renin through volume expansion. Secondary hyperaldosteronism usually raises renin because reduced effective perfusion is driving the response.

Description

Aldosterone is the principal mineralocorticoid steroid hormone made in the adrenal cortex's zona glomerulosa. It binds intracellular mineralocorticoid receptors in aldosterone-sensitive distal-nephron cells, increasing ENaC-mediated sodium reabsorption while promoting potassium and hydrogen secretion to defend effective circulating volume. Angiotensin II and an increased extracellular potassium concentration are its major acute stimulators; ACTH has a smaller, usually transient role. Aldosterone does not simply pull free water into blood by itself. It increases sodium reabsorption, and water follows that retained sodium when intake, thirst, antidiuretic hormone, and kidney function permit. Excess autonomous aldosterone expands volume and raises blood pressure while promoting potassium loss and metabolic alkalosis; deficiency or resistance causes renal salt wasting, hypotension or volume depletion, hyperkalemia, and a normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis tendency. That opposite-pattern logic makes the hormone clinically memorable rather than a disconnected lab value.

Adverse Effects

  • Pathologic excess causes sodium retention, volume expansion, hypertension, renal potassium wasting, weakness or arrhythmia from hypokalemia, hydrogen loss, and metabolic alkalosis. Potassium may remain normal in milder primary aldosteronism, so normal potassium does not exclude it.

  • Deficiency or resistance causes sodium wasting, low effective volume, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and type 4 renal-tubular-acidosis physiology. Severe hyperkalemia can produce conduction abnormalities and cardiac arrest.
