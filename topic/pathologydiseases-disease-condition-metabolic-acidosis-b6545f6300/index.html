Pathology

Acid consumes bicarbonate and lowers the bicarbonate-to-PaCO2 ratio. Hydrogen buffering and ion exchange can raise serum potassium, but potassium direction also depends on insulin, catecholamines, kidney function, GI loss, and the specific acid, so acidosis does not guarantee hyperkalemia. Severe acidemia impairs contractility and vascular responsiveness, while the work of compensatory breathing can exhaust patients with limited respiratory reserve.

Etiology

High-gap causes include lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, advanced kidney failure, salicylate and toxic-alcohol metabolites, and selected drug or metabolic acids. Normal-gap causes include diarrhea and other GI bicarbonate loss, renal tubular acidosis, chloride-rich fluid, urinary diversions, carbonic anhydrase inhibition, and earlier impaired kidney ammonium excretion. Several causes often coexist in critical illness.

Signs Symptoms

  • Deep rapid Kussmaul breathing, nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, weakness, headache, confusion, hypotension, or dysrhythmia can occur.

  • Symptoms of the cause may dominate, including fever and shock, polyuria and dehydration, tinnitus, visual change, renal failure, or profuse diarrhea.

  • Falling respiratory rate or depth in a severely acidotic patient can signal fatigue and impending decompensation.

Diagnostics

  • Confirm the primary metabolic pattern, calculate expected PaCO2, and assess oxygenation and clinical stability.

  • Calculate anion gap using the local interval, consider albumin, and use delta analysis if the gap is high.

  • Select lactate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, glucose, renal function, salicylate, measured osmolality, toxin studies, urinalysis, urine electrolytes, cultures, imaging, and perfusion assessment according to the suspected mechanism.

Treatments

  • Restore perfusion and oxygen delivery, stop ketogenesis with insulin when safe, replace volume and electrolytes, treat infection or seizure, remove offending drugs, and correct GI or renal mechanisms.

  • Use antidotes and dialysis for selected toxins or refractory kidney-related acid, electrolyte, volume, and uremic indications.

  • Reserve sodium bicarbonate for selected severe or mechanism-specific situations because it adds sodium, can lower ionized calcium, generates CO2, and requires adequate ventilation; routine correction of every low bicarbonate can cause harm.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess breathing pattern, perfusion, mental status, glucose, ketones, renal function, diarrhea, medications, toxins, infection, and fluid losses because low bicarbonate reflects multiple mechanisms that require different treatments.

  • Interpret pH, bicarbonate, carbon dioxide, anion gap, albumin, chloride, lactate, ketones, electrolytes, and expected respiratory compensation together because a near-normal pH can conceal a dangerous mixed acid-base disorder.

  • Treat the identified driver with prescribed fluids, insulin, perfusion support, toxin antidote, renal replacement, or electrolyte therapy because bicarbonate replacement alone does not stop ongoing acid generation or loss.

  • Monitor serial blood gas, bicarbonate, anion gap, potassium, glucose, lactate, creatinine, urine output, respiratory effort, rhythm, and consciousness because worsening acidemia impairs circulation while treatment can shift potassium rapidly.

  • Escalate for pH at or below the unit's critical threshold, rising potassium with electrocardiographic change, shock, deep respiratory fatigue, oliguria, seizure, or suspected toxic ingestion because severe acidemia, dysrhythmia, ventilatory failure, and dialyzable poisoning require urgent intervention.

Complications

  • Shock, reduced cardiac output, vasopressor resistance, dysrhythmia, hyperkalemia, coma, and death can occur in severe acidemia.

  • Respiratory-muscle fatigue can add acute respiratory acidosis and cause abrupt pH collapse.

  • Therapy can produce hypokalemia, hypernatremia, volume overload, hypoglycemia, cerebral injury, or overshoot alkalosis.

Patient Education

  • Follow the cause-specific sick-day and medication plan because dehydration, missed insulin, kidney failure, and toxins require very different responses.

  • Seek urgent care for deep rapid breathing, confusion, persistent vomiting, fainting, or inability to hydrate because severe acidemia can deteriorate quickly.

Nclex Traps

  • Kussmaul breathing is compensation for metabolic acidosis, not primary respiratory alkalosis.

  • A normal pH does not exclude metabolic acidosis when another alkalosis coexists.

  • Serum potassium can be high while total-body potassium is depleted in DKA.

Definition

Metabolic acidosis is a primary process that lowers bicarbonate through addition of nonvolatile acid, reduced renal acid excretion, or loss of bicarbonate. It usually causes acidemia, but pH can be normal or high when respiratory alkalosis or metabolic alkalosis coexists. The lungs respond by increasing ventilation and lowering PaCO2; that response is compensation and does not make the primary disorder respiratory.

Pathophysiology

Organic or retained mineral acids dissociate, their hydrogen is buffered by bicarbonate, and the accompanying anion either widens the anion gap or is excreted/replaced by chloride. Direct bicarbonate loss lowers HCO3 and preserves electroneutrality through chloride retention, producing normal-gap hyperchloremic acidosis. Chemoreceptors increase minute ventilation, and expected PaCO2 can be screened with Winter's formula: 1.5 x HCO3 + 8 +/-2. A PaCO2 above that range means added respiratory acidosis and threatened compensation; below it means added respiratory alkalosis. Kidneys normally increase ammonium excretion, but kidney or tubular disease prevents that response.

Risk Factors

  • Shock, sepsis, severe hypoxemia, seizure, liver dysfunction, and mitochondrial toxins increase lactate burden.

  • Diabetes, missed insulin, SGLT2 inhibitors, fasting, alcohol use, and pregnancy increase ketoacidosis risk.

  • Kidney disease, diarrhea, urinary diversion, large chloride loads, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, salicylates, and toxic alcohol exposure create other mechanisms.

Labs

  • Low HCO3 with an appropriately low PaCO2 supports metabolic acidosis; pH establishes whether acidemia is present.

  • Potassium may be high before treatment yet total-body depleted in DKA, so serial potassium and ECG monitoring are essential.

  • Direct lactate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are more specific than assuming the anion gap identifies the acid.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose the cause from a low serum CO2 alone; confirm context and exclude respiratory alkalosis lowering bicarbonate through compensation.

  • Do not give bicarbonate automatically for lactic acidosis or DKA without a specific indication and ventilation plan.

  • Do not start insulin in DKA with dangerously low potassium until potassium is addressed under protocol.

Red Flags

  • Critical acidemia at the laboratory threshold or a rapidly falling arterial pH

  • Hyperkalemia with peaked T waves, widening QRS, bradycardia, or another dysrhythmia

  • Hypotension, rising lactate, cool mottled skin, oliguria, or worsening consciousness

  • Deep breathing with fatigue, seizure, coma, or suspected methanol, ethylene glycol, or salicylate exposure
