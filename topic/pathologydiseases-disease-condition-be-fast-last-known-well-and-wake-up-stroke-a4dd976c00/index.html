Risk Factors

  • Atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, vascular disease, and prior TIA raise stroke risk but absence of risk factors does not exclude stroke.

  • Sleep-disordered breathing and overnight blood-pressure or rhythm changes may coexist with wake-up stroke without revealing exact onset.

  • Living alone or unwitnessed symptoms can make timeline reconstruction difficult, so phone calls, messages, cameras, medication times, and family observations may help.

Signs Symptoms

  • Sudden balance failure, visual loss or diplopia, face droop, unilateral arm or leg weakness, or abnormal speech activates BE-FAST.

  • Other urgent signs include sudden severe headache, neglect, numbness, dysphagia, ataxia, vomiting, seizure, or reduced consciousness.

  • Symptoms that resolve still require emergency evaluation because TIA or transient recanalization predicts ongoing risk.

Diagnostics

  • Record separate fields for last-known-well, symptom discovery, and emergency arrival because they answer different questions.

  • Check glucose and perform focused neurologic scoring while arranging NCCT and vascular imaging because mimic exclusion and vessel identification run in parallel.

  • Use DWI-FLAIR or perfusion-based mismatch only through the appropriate unknown- or extended-window protocol because advanced imaging selects tissue, not certainty.

Labs

  • Bedside glucose is immediate because a reversible mimic must be treated.

  • Acute safety labs and anticoagulant history support reperfusion decisions but should not create avoidable imaging delay.

  • Do not invent onset from a laboratory or scan age estimate; imaging supports selection but cannot replace a carefully documented timeline.

Treatments

  • Call emergency services and activate the stroke system immediately because private transport loses prearrival notification and en-route assessment.

  • Treat eligible disabling stroke rapidly within the applicable IVT or EVT pathway because every delay allows core expansion.

  • Continue evaluation for wake-up or unknown-onset stroke because advanced imaging may identify treatable mismatch rather than assuming the window is closed.

Nursing Priorities

  • Use Balance, Eyes, Face, Arm, Speech, and Time to identify sudden focal neurologic change because posterior, visual, and coordination strokes may be missed by a face-arm-speech screen alone.

  • Establish the exact last time the patient was known normal from the best witness, records, calls, or device data because discovery time is not onset time and treatment pathways depend on last-known-well.

  • Activate emergency stroke transport, check glucose, keep the patient nothing by mouth, and bring medication and anticoagulant information because imaging and rapid contraindication review must occur without avoidable delay.

  • Monitor airway, oxygenation, neurologic deficits, blood pressure, rhythm, glucose, swallowing, and time milestones because deterioration and missed handoffs can erase the benefit of a rapid first recognition.

  • Escalate immediately for any sudden BE-FAST sign, even if it resolves or begins on waking, and for worsening consciousness, headache, vomiting, or seizure because TIA, wake-up stroke, hemorrhage, and large-vessel occlusion remain emergencies.

Complications

  • Incorrectly late last-known-well can expose a patient to bleeding risk, while incorrectly early timing can deny beneficial treatment.

  • Waiting for symptoms to resolve can lose reperfusion opportunity and allow a fluctuating occlusion to worsen.

  • Posterior signs can be missed when recognition stops at FAST without balance and eye assessment.

Contraindications

  • Do not use awakening time as onset time unless the patient was known normal then.

  • Do not give food, drink, or oral medication before swallowing safety is established.

  • Do not tell a wake-up-stroke patient that treatment is impossible before imaging-based assessment.

Red Flags

  • Sudden balance loss, vision change, facial droop, arm weakness, or speech difficulty

  • Unknown onset or awakening with a new focal neurologic deficit

  • Worsening consciousness, severe headache, repeated vomiting, or unequal pupils

  • Seizure, airway compromise, aspiration, severe hypoxemia, or hemodynamic instability

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for any sudden BE-FAST sign even when it disappears because transient symptoms may precede a disabling stroke.

  • Write down the last time the person was definitely normal, not merely when found ill, because this detail guides treatment eligibility.

Nclex Traps

  • Last-known-well is not discovery time.

  • Wake-up stroke is not automatically outside every treatment pathway.

  • BE-FAST recognizes stroke; it does not distinguish ischemia from hemorrhage.

BE-FAST, last-known-well, and wake-up stroke

Category

Stroke recognition and emergency timeline

Definition

BE-FAST is an emergency stroke-recognition aid: sudden Balance loss, Eye or vision change, Face droop, Arm weakness, or Speech trouble can occur when a brain network abruptly loses blood flow or is compressed by bleeding, and Time means call emergency services immediately. Last-known-well is the most recent time the person was known to be at neurologic baseline, not the time symptoms were discovered. A wake-up stroke has symptoms first noticed after sleep, so onset is unknown and last-known-well may be bedtime. These distinctions matter because treatment pathways use both elapsed clock time and imaging evidence of viable tissue.

Etiology

The recognition tool applies to ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, TIA, and important mimics because the public cannot safely determine stroke type. Wake-up strokes occur when vascular occlusion happens at an unknown point during sleep; some retain favorable tissue mismatch and may still benefit from imaging-selected reperfusion.

Pathology

In ischemia, interrupted blood flow deprives neurons of oxygen and glucose and allows threatened penumbra to become infarcted; in hemorrhage, escaped blood directly injures and compresses brain networks. Time documentation anchors how long tissue may have been threatened, while advanced imaging can sometimes show that biologic tissue time is more favorable than the clock suggests.

Pathophysiology

Face-arm-speech captures common anterior strokes, while balance and eye symptoms widen recognition toward posterior circulation. Last-known-well avoids falsely shortening the window: if a patient wakes abnormal at 0700 but was normal at 2200, 0700 is discovery time and 2200 is last-known-well. The 2026 guideline supports standard-window IV thrombolysis for eligible disabling deficits within 4.5 hours and advanced-imaging selection for some unknown-onset or 4.5-to-9-hour patients; selected EVT can extend farther, including up to 24 hours in defined pathways, so unknown onset is an imaging question rather than an automatic dismissal.
