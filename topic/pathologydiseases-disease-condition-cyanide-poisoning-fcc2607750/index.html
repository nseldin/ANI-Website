Definition

Cyanide poisoning develops when cyanide blocks mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase, preventing cellular oxygen use despite oxygen delivery and causing profound lactic acidosis. The characteristic pattern is collapse, altered consciousness, seizure, hypotension, severe lactic acidosis, or soot exposure after an enclosed-space fire. Confirm or stage it with exposure-based recognition with lactate, blood gas, electrolytes, ECG, and co-oximetry; distinguish it from carbon monoxide poisoning, methemoglobinemia, hydrogen sulfide, sepsis, seizure, and shock by exposure, co-oximetry, odor-independent clues, and response. The priority is to remove from exposure with responder protection and provide high-concentration oxygen because cardiac arrest can complicate Cyanide poisoning. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • exposure-based recognition with lactate, blood gas, electrolytes, ECG, and co-oximetry

  • cyanide concentration when available but never as a prerequisite for antidote

  • simultaneous carbon-monoxide and trauma assessment in fire victims

  • Distinguish Cyanide poisoning from carbon monoxide poisoning, methemoglobinemia, hydrogen sulfide, sepsis, seizure, and shock by exposure, co-oximetry, odor-independent clues, and response.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • remove from exposure with responder protection and provide high-concentration oxygen

  • give hydroxocobalamin promptly when clinical suspicion is high

  • support airway, circulation, seizure control, and burn or smoke-inhalation care

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for smoke exposure with coma, severe lactic acidosis, hypotension, seizure, bradycardia, or cardiac arrest because toxicology consultation and full resuscitation are time-critical.

  • Remove the patient from exposure using responder protection and decontaminate as appropriate because secondary exposure can injure rescuers and continued absorption worsens cellular hypoxia.

  • Give high-concentration oxygen and administer ordered hydroxocobalamin without waiting for a confirmatory cyanide level when severe poisoning is suspected because cellular oxygen use is blocked despite a measurable saturation.

  • Monitor mental status, blood pressure, rhythm, lactate, acid-base status, and response to antidote to detect persistent histotoxic hypoxia and cardiovascular collapse.

Complications

  • cardiac arrest can complicate Cyanide poisoning.

  • severe neurologic injury can complicate Cyanide poisoning.

  • shock can complicate Cyanide poisoning.

  • kidney injury can complicate Cyanide poisoning.

  • multiorgan failure can complicate Cyanide poisoning.

Red Flags

  • Enclosed-space smoke exposure with altered consciousness

  • Severe lactic acidosis, hypotension, or cardiovascular collapse

  • Seizure, coma, bradycardia, or cardiac arrest

  • Persistent instability after initial antidote therapy

Patient Education

  • Teach that suspected cyanide exposure requires emergency care and poison-center guidance even if the person initially feels better.

  • Explain that hydroxocobalamin can discolor skin, urine, and some laboratory samples so later clinicians should be told it was given.

Nclex Traps

  • A normal pulse-oximeter reading does not show that cells can use oxygen

  • Do not wait for a cyanide level in a crashing smoke-inhalation patient
