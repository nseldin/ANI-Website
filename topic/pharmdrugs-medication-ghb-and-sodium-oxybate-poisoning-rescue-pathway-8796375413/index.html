Used To Treat

Fluctuating agitation, coma, apnea, bradycardia, hypothermia, emesis, myoclonus, or seizure after illicit GHB, prescription sodium oxybate, or related precursor exposure.

Description

GHB and sodium oxybate are rapidly acting CNS depressants that activate GABA-B receptors at toxic concentrations and suppress arousal and breathing, so small dose changes can cause abrupt coma, apnea, vomiting, and aspiration. A patient may fluctuate between agitation, combativeness, ataxia, and deep coma because blood and brain concentrations rise and fall rapidly across a steep dose-response curve. Vomiting can occur while the patient is profoundly obtunded, so aspiration and loss of airway protection are central hazards. Breathing may range from adequate to slow, irregular, Cheyne-Stokes, or apneic; bradycardia, hypothermia, hypotonia, myoclonus, and seizure can accompany coma. There is no dependable receptor antagonist in routine clinical use. Naloxone and flumazenil do not reverse pure sodium-oxybate depression, although naloxone remains appropriate when opioid co-exposure could explain respiratory depression. Many patients recover quickly with meticulous supportive care, but that characteristic must not be used to delay ventilation, dismiss trauma, or overlook alcohol, opioids, sedatives, assault, hypoglycemia, or another cause of coma.

Mechanism

GHB activates GHB-specific sites and, at higher concentrations, inhibitory GABA-B receptors. Neuronal hyperpolarization reduces arousal and respiratory drive, while effects on dopaminergic and other circuits help explain alternating agitation and sedation. The steep concentration-response relationship means a modest additional dose or co-depressant can move a patient from euphoria to apnea. Rapid metabolism through succinic-semialdehyde pathways often permits abrupt awakening as concentration falls, but supportive ventilation is what preserves oxygen delivery during the dangerous interval. Naloxone targets opioid receptors and flumazenil targets benzodiazepine sites, so neither addresses GABA-B-mediated depression. Hemodialysis is usually unattractive because endogenous clearance is rapid; extraordinary severe cases with persistent acidosis or an alternative diagnosis require specialist reassessment rather than a routine dialysis reflex.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for difficult arousal, slow or irregular breathing, blue or gray color, seizure, repeated vomiting, injury, or suspected drug-facilitated assault. Position the patient to protect the airway, suction as needed, ventilate promptly, and check glucose, temperature, oxygenation, and ECG.

  • Intubate when ventilation or airway protection cannot be maintained, while anticipating that stimulation can provoke abrupt combativeness even in a deeply unconscious patient. Use the safest local rapid-sequence plan and avoid adding unnecessary sedative burden.

  • Observe until consciousness, ventilation, temperature, gait, oral safety, and co-ingestant risk are reliably resolved. Obtain time-sensitive forensic or safeguarding support with consent when assault is possible, but never delay medical stabilization for specimen collection.

Nursing Essentials

  • Trend respiratory rate, depth, end-tidal carbon dioxide when available, oxygen saturation, consciousness, gag and cough, emesis, heart rate, blood pressure, and temperature continuously through the fluctuating phase.

  • Use lateral positioning and suction readiness before intubation when appropriate, keep the patient from falls or self-extubation during abrupt awakening, and reassess restraints frequently because neurologic state can reverse within minutes.

  • Ask about prescription sodium oxybate concentration, both nightly doses, illicit liquid amount, precursors such as gamma-butyrolactone or 1,4-butanediol, alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, and the timeline of possible assault or trauma.

Key Labs

  • Bedside glucose, electrolytes, bicarbonate, creatinine, blood gas and lactate when ventilation or perfusion is impaired, acetaminophen and salicylate levels in intentional or unknown ingestion, pregnancy testing when relevant, and ECG. Imaging follows trauma or focal findings.

  • Routine toxicology screens often omit GHB, and confirmatory concentrations may return after clinical recovery. A negative screen therefore does not exclude exposure, and a positive result does not eliminate co-ingestants or medical mimics.

Adverse Effects

  • Major harms include aspiration pneumonitis, apnea, hypoxemia, bradycardia, hypothermia, seizure, trauma, coma, and death, especially with alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives.

  • Intubation and sedatives can cause hypotension or prolong depression, while premature discharge can miss a long-acting co-ingestant. Abrupt awakening can cause falls, line removal, aggression, or loss of forensic opportunity unless the environment remains safe.

Contraindications

  • Do not force oral charcoal or induce vomiting in a drowsy patient; rapid coma and spontaneous emesis make aspiration risk high. Gastric decontamination is not routine for isolated rapidly absorbed GHB.

  • Do not expect naloxone or flumazenil to reverse pure GHB toxicity because neither drug antagonizes GABA-B-mediated depression, and do not use a brief naloxone response as proof of a single agent because it may reveal only an opioid component. Do not postpone ventilation while waiting for spontaneous awakening because oxygen delivery must be preserved during the depressed interval.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent apnea, shock, seizure, severe acidosis, focal deficit, failure to awaken along the expected clinical course, or recurrent depression requires ICU and toxicology, with renewed evaluation for co-ingestion, precursor, trauma, hypoxic injury, infection, or another neurologic cause.

Interactions

  • Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines, antipsychotics, and other CNS depressants can deepen and prolong respiratory depression. Naloxone treats an opioid component only.

  • Sodium-oxybate products contain clinically relevant sodium or mixed salts depending on formulation; in overdose, fluid, sodium, and acid-base interpretation should use the exact product rather than assuming all oxybate products are identical.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Accidental access to concentrated prescription solution can produce rapid coma and apnea; use exact product concentration and measured weight.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal ventilation and oxygenation are the immediate fetal treatment; stabilize first, then coordinate obstetric assessment and safeguarding.

  • Population

    Drug-facilitated assault

    Note

    Memory loss, rapid clearance, and delayed presentation require trauma-informed consent, time-sensitive forensic consultation, STI and pregnancy risk assessment, and psychological support.

Evidence Limitations

  • Overdose knowledge comes mainly from product labeling, case reports, and observational experience in mixed illicit exposures. The expected rapid recovery is common but not guaranteed and cannot establish a safe universal observation time.

Nclex Traps

  • GHB coma can alternate rapidly with agitation, so airway risk and staff safety change minute to minute.

  • Naloxone and flumazenil do not reliably reverse pure sodium-oxybate depression.

  • Spontaneous vomiting can occur during deep coma, making aspiration precautions essential.

Regulatory Status

Prescription sodium-oxybate labels describe overdose care as supportive. No FDA-approved specific reversal drug exists; naloxone remains indicated for a plausible opioid component, not for pure GHB effect.

Boxed Warning

GHB/OXYBATE WARNING: call 911 for difficult arousal, apnea, seizure, or repeated vomiting. Protect and ventilate the airway immediately because coma can deepen abruptly and vomiting can occur while unconscious; naloxone and flumazenil do not reliably reverse pure GHB toxicity.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
