Pathology

CKD progressively reduces functioning nephron mass through glomerular sclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and vascular injury. Remaining nephrons enlarge and hyperfilter, preserving total GFR initially while increasing single-nephron workload and pressure.

Etiology

Diabetes and hypertension are common drivers, but glomerular disease, inherited disorders, tubulointerstitial disease, vascular injury, recurrent obstruction or infection, congenital anomalies, nephrotoxins, and incomplete recovery from AKI are also important. Cause, GFR category, and albuminuria category together describe the disease better than creatinine alone.

Signs Symptoms

  • Often asymptomatic in early stages

  • Nocturia, fatigue, edema, hypertension, pruritus, nausea, poor appetite, restless legs, sleep or cognitive change as disease advances

  • Dyspnea from anemia or volume overload

  • Bone pain, fracture, neuropathy, muscle cramps, sexual dysfunction, or uremic symptoms in advanced disease

Diagnostics

  • Confirm chronicity with prior values or repeat testing over at least 3 months unless structural chronicity is already clear

  • Classify by cause, GFR category, and urine albumin category

  • Use urinalysis, sediment, imaging, serology, genetic testing, or biopsy to identify cause when appropriate

  • Assess cardiovascular risk, medication safety, nutrition, anemia, potassium, acid-base, and CKD-MBD complications

Treatments

  • Treat the cause and control blood pressure, diabetes, albuminuria, and cardiovascular risk

  • Use kidney-protective RAAS, SGLT2, mineralocorticoid-pathway, lipid, and glucose therapies when individual indications and safety permit

  • Avoid nephrotoxins, adjust drug dosing, prevent AKI, support nutrition and activity, and treat smoking

  • Manage anemia, acidosis, potassium, edema, CKD-MBD, symptoms, and timely transplant, dialysis, or comprehensive conservative-care planning

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify estimated filtration and urine-albumin category, cause, rate of change, pressure, diabetes status, cardiovascular disease, medicines, diet, access barriers, and symptoms because CKD risk depends on both filtration loss and kidney damage rather than creatinine alone.

  • Trend pressure, weight, edema, intake and output when unstable, creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, hemoglobin, iron, parathyroid hormone, urine albumin, nutrition, cognition, and pruritus because CKD affects volume, acid-base, bone, blood, nerve, and cardiovascular systems.

  • Administer prescribed kidney-protective, pressure, glucose, anemia, acidosis, mineral-bone, and diuretic therapy while checking sick-day instructions, potassium, pressure, renal dosing, and adverse effects because benefits require surveillance for predictable hemodynamic and electrolyte changes.

  • Preserve veins in patients likely to need hemodialysis, avoid blood pressure cuffs and venipuncture on a mature access, and coordinate education about dialysis, transplant, and conservative care early because rushed kidney-failure decisions reduce safe choices.

  • Escalate for severe dyspnea or pulmonary edema, hyperkalemic rhythm change, pericardial chest pain, new confusion or seizure, uncontrolled hypertension, major bleeding, or rapidly worsening filtration because urgent dialysis or treatment of an acute superimposed process may be required.

  • In advanced CKD, urgently escalate new pain out of proportion to the skin examination, a deeply tender indurated plaque, branching purple discoloration, or black eschar. Do not label every dialysis wound as calciphylaxis, but do not dismiss this pattern as ordinary cellulitis or a pressure injury; early nephrology, dermatology, wound, pain, and infection assessment matters because necrosis can progress to sepsis.

Complications

  • Kidney failure and need for replacement therapy or conservative kidney care

  • Cardiovascular disease, stroke, arrhythmia, and sudden death

  • Anemia, acidosis, hyperkalemia, volume overload, malnutrition, and uremia

  • CKD-MBD, fracture, vascular calcification, infection, cognitive and functional decline

  • Calciphylaxis (calcific uremic arteriolopathy) is a rare, life-threatening small-vessel ischemic complication seen most often in advanced Chronic kidney disease, especially with kidney failure or dialysis. Mineral deposition, intimal narrowing, endothelial injury, and thrombosis reduce blood flow through dermal and subcutaneous arterioles. That mechanism explains the severe pain that can precede visible injury and the progression from induration or retiform purple change to necrosis and black eschar. CKD-mineral and bone disorder, inflammation, diabetes, malnutrition, obesity, and warfarin exposure can increase risk, but no calcium, phosphate, or PTH result proves or excludes the disease.

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to ask before taking NSAIDs, contrast, supplements, salt substitutes, or over-the-counter medicines because kidney clearance and potassium content can change safety.

  • Explain the personal eGFR and urine-albumin trend in plain language and provide a written sick-day plan so the patient knows which changes require early contact.

  • Report a new extremely painful firm, purple, mottled, ulcerated, or black skin area promptly. Most people with CKD never develop Calciphylaxis, but early assessment matters because tissue ischemia and secondary infection can become life-threatening.

Nclex Traps

  • CKD requires chronicity

  • GFR and albuminuria are independent risk axes

  • Normal urine output does not exclude advanced CKD

  • Kidney failure planning should begin before an emergency, but planning does not mean immediate dialysis

Definition

Chronic kidney disease is an abnormality of kidney structure or function present for at least 3 months with implications for health. It includes an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or persistent markers such as albuminuria, abnormal sediment, structural disease, tubular disorders, or prior transplant; G1 or G2 filtration alone does not establish CKD without a damage marker.

Labs

  • eGFR estimates filtration and is less reliable during rapidly changing creatinine or at extremes of muscle mass

  • UACR adds prognostic information and should be confirmed when transient causes are possible

  • Potassium, bicarbonate, hemoglobin, iron indices, calcium, phosphorus, PTH, and albumin reveal complications

  • Cystatin C or combined creatinine-cystatin equations can refine selected decisions when creatinine is misleading

Stages

  • G1: eGFR >=90 with kidney damage marker

  • G2: eGFR 60-89 with kidney damage marker

  • G3a: eGFR 45-59

  • G3b: eGFR 30-44

  • G4: eGFR 15-29

  • G5: eGFR <15 or kidney failure

Nursing Interventions

  • Monitor fluid status, weight, edema, lung sounds, blood pressure, electrolytes, anemia, and medication renal dosing.

Red Flags

  • Pulmonary edema, severe hypoxemia, or rapidly increasing volume overload

  • Hyperkalemia with weakness, bradycardia, wide QRS, or other electrocardiographic change

  • Pericarditic chest pain, friction rub, encephalopathy, seizure, or severe uremic bleeding

  • Abrupt creatinine rise, anuria, malignant hypertension, or signs of access infection or thrombosis

Nclex Pearls

  • CKD stage is not just creatinine. eGFR and albuminuria matter.

  • Anemia in CKD is commonly related to low erythropoietin.

Pathophysiology

Compensatory hyperfiltration and RAAS activation help maintain filtration but can increase glomerular wall stress, protein leak, tubular inflammation, and fibrosis. As reserve falls, sodium-water, potassium, acid, drug, and uremic-solute clearance narrow; erythropoietin production, calcitriol activation, and mineral regulation also fail. Albuminuria reflects barrier injury and predicts kidney and cardiovascular risk independent of eGFR. CKD therefore behaves as a systemic vascular, hematologic, metabolic, bone, medication-safety, and cardiovascular disorder rather than an isolated laboratory diagnosis.

Risk Factors

  • Diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and smoking

  • Prior AKI, preeclampsia, recurrent stones, obstruction, infection, or nephrotoxin exposure

  • Autoimmune, glomerular, inherited, congenital, or family kidney disease

  • Older age, low birth weight, social barriers, and limited access to preventive care

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose CKD from one low eGFR during AKI

  • Do not call G1 or G2 CKD without another kidney-damage marker

  • Do not use serum creatinine alone to stage risk

Causal Links

  • Nephron loss -> single-nephron hyperfiltration -> glomerular stress -> further nephron loss

  • Protein leak -> tubular inflammatory signaling -> interstitial fibrosis -> progression

  • Reduced clearance -> electrolyte, acid, fluid, and drug accumulation -> systemic complications

  • Reduced endocrine function -> anemia and CKD-MBD -> cardiovascular and functional burden
