Used To Treat

Acute or acute-on-chronic valproate toxicity causing CNS depression, hyperammonemic encephalopathy, respiratory failure, hypotension, metabolic acidosis, cerebral edema, hepatotoxicity, thrombocytopenia, or pancreatitis.

Description

Valproate is a GABA-enhancing antiseizure drug whose overdose disrupts mitochondrial beta oxidation and the urea cycle, causing CNS depression, hyperammonemia, cerebral edema, and hepatic injury. Rising concentrations can saturate albumin binding, causing the unbound pharmacologically active fraction to increase disproportionately; a total concentration can therefore underrepresent free toxic exposure in hypoalbuminemia, kidney failure, pregnancy, or very high overdose. CNS depression can progress to coma and respiratory failure. Valproate also disrupts mitochondrial beta oxidation and depletes carnitine, which impairs the urea cycle and contributes to hyperammonemia, cerebral edema, acidosis, and hepatic injury. Ammonia can be high even when aminotransferases are not, and encephalopathy must be treated clinically rather than dismissed as a liver-test issue. Delayed-release and enteric-coated products can produce late peaks. Rescue combines airway and circulatory support, serial total and sometimes free valproate levels, ammonia and organ monitoring, selective decontamination, specialist-directed IV levocarnitine for serious features, and hemodialysis when EXTRIP severe-poisoning criteria are met.

Mechanism

Therapeutically, valproate increases inhibitory GABA signaling and modulates voltage-gated ion channels. In overdose, broad neuronal depression impairs consciousness and ventilation. Mitochondrial metabolism generates toxic metabolites and consumes carnitine, reducing fatty-acid transport and acetyl-CoA availability; the urea cycle then clears ammonia less effectively. Hyperammonemia disturbs astrocyte osmotic balance and neurotransmission, which can produce encephalopathy and cerebral edema even without fulminant hepatitis. Levocarnitine replenishes a depleted cofactor and promotes less-toxic mitochondrial metabolism, but poisoning use is based on limited evidence and does not replace supportive care. At high concentrations, protein binding saturates and the free fraction rises, making valproate more dialyzable than at therapeutic levels. Hemodialysis removes free drug, corrects acidosis and ammonia-related metabolic disturbance, and supports severe shock or cerebral edema.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for difficult arousal, slow breathing, seizure, shock, or severe confusion. Stop valproate, support airway and circulation, check glucose and ECG, and call Poison Help for any significant delayed-release ingestion or symptomatic chronic patient.

  • Trend valproate and ammonia with blood gas, electrolytes, liver function, CBC, coagulation, and neurologic status. Consider activated charcoal or whole-bowel irrigation only in selected protected-airway, functioning-bowel delayed-release cases; do not allow decontamination to delay stabilization.

  • Use IV levocarnitine under toxicology protocol for serious hyperammonemia, encephalopathy, hepatotoxicity, or substantial poisoning. Contact nephrology early when shock, cerebral edema, severe acidosis, ventilated coma, marked hyperammonemia, or EXTRIP concentration criteria suggest hemodialysis.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify valproic acid versus divalproex, immediate versus delayed or extended release, total amount, time, chronic dose, recent changes, co-ingestants, topiramate, urea-cycle disease, liver disease, pregnancy, and nutrition or carnitine risk.

  • Trend consciousness, pupils, respiratory effort, temperature, blood pressure, ECG, urine output, abdominal pain, vomiting, bruising or bleeding, and signs of cerebral edema. Use aspiration, fall, seizure, and bleeding precautions.

  • Verify levocarnitine product concentration, measured weight, loading and maintenance plan, line, and stop criteria independently. Coordinate dialysis sampling so total and free concentrations, ammonia, and clinical response are interpretable around treatment.

Key Labs

  • Serial total valproate concentration, free valproate when protein binding may be altered, ammonia, blood gas, lactate, electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, AST and ALT, bilirubin, PT/INR, CBC and platelets, lipase for compatible pain, pregnancy testing, and ECG.

  • A falling total level does not end care when ammonia, cerebral status, liver function, acidosis, shock, or free fraction remains abnormal. Ammonia collection and processing errors are common, so repeat an unexpected value without delaying treatment of a convincing syndrome.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe toxicity can cause coma, hypoventilation, aspiration, hypotension, metabolic acidosis, hypernatremia from some formulations, hyperammonemic encephalopathy, cerebral edema, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, kidney injury, and death.

  • Levocarnitine can cause nausea, diarrhea, odor, and rare hypersensitivity; charcoal and whole-bowel irrigation can cause aspiration or obstruction; dialysis carries hemodynamic and access risks.

Contraindications

  • Do not equate a normal aminotransferase with absence of hyperammonemic encephalopathy, because disrupted mitochondrial and urea-cycle handling can raise ammonia and impair the brain before substantial hepatocellular enzyme release. Do not use the total valproate level alone when albumin binding is saturated or reduced, because the unbound active fraction can rise disproportionately and drive toxicity despite a less dramatic total value.

  • Do not give oral decontamination through an unprotected airway or ileus. Do not let levocarnitine delay ventilation, shock treatment, cerebral-edema care, or EXTRIP-directed dialysis.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Cerebral edema, shock, pH near EXTRIP severe criteria, rising ammonia, ventilated coma, severe hepatic injury, recurrent seizure, or very high or rising concentration requires ICU, toxicology, nephrology, and early extracorporeal treatment planning. Recheck after dialysis for redistribution and clinical recurrence.

Interactions

  • Topiramate increases hyperammonemic encephalopathy risk. Other sedatives deepen coma, while aspirin and hypoalbuminemia can increase free valproate despite a less dramatic total concentration.

  • Carbapenem antibiotics can rapidly lower therapeutic valproate concentrations and destabilize seizure control after recovery; medication reconciliation is essential when restarting long-term therapy.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Young age, metabolic disease, weight-based dosing errors, and liquid or sprinkle formulations require exact product reconstruction and early metabolic consultation.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal toxicity requires immediate rescue; valproate is teratogenic, so obstetric and teratology follow-up is important after stabilization.

  • Population

    Liver, mitochondrial, or urea-cycle disease

    Note

    Reduced metabolic reserve increases hyperammonemia and hepatic failure risk and lowers the threshold for specialist rescue.

Evidence Limitations

  • Levocarnitine is widely recommended in serious valproate poisoning, but optimal thresholds, dose duration, and outcome benefit rely mainly on observational and mechanistic evidence. EXTRIP dialysis recommendations are evidence graded but mostly low quality.

Nclex Traps

  • Valproate hyperammonemic encephalopathy can occur without major aminotransferase elevation.

  • Protein-binding saturation makes free valproate rise disproportionately in severe overdose.

  • Levocarnitine supports mitochondrial metabolism but does not replace airway care or dialysis when severe criteria are met.

Regulatory Status

Levocarnitine use for valproate poisoning is off-label and toxicology directed. EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment for defined severe presentations; no single concentration substitutes for the clinical syndrome.

Boxed Warning

VALPROATE-TOXICITY WARNING: call 911 for coma, slow breathing, seizure, shock, or severe confusion. Check ammonia and organ injury even when liver enzymes are modest because disrupted urea-cycle clearance can raise ammonia and injure the brain without major aminotransferase elevation. Use specialist-directed levocarnitine to support disrupted mitochondrial metabolism, and arrange early hemodialysis consultation for cerebral edema, shock, severe acidosis, or other EXTRIP criteria because severe unbound-drug burden and metabolic injury can progress quickly.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
