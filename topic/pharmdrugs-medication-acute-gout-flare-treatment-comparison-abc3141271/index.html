Used To Treat

Acute inflammatory pain, swelling, warmth, and disability caused by monosodium urate crystals. Low-dose colchicine, an appropriate NSAID, or a systemic/intra-articular corticosteroid can be selected from timing, kidney/liver function, GI/bleeding risk, diabetes, infection concern, interactions, and number of joints. Urate-lowering therapy prevents future crystal burden but is not the analgesic for the current flare.

Description

A gout flare is not simply high serum urate at that moment. Crystals already deposited in tissue activate macrophage NLRP3 inflammasomes and IL-1 beta, which recruit intensely reactive neutrophils. Colchicine disrupts microtubule-dependent neutrophil trafficking and inflammasome amplification; NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin production through cyclooxygenase inhibition; corticosteroids repress multiple inflammatory genes. These drugs calm the inflammatory response but do not remove the urate crystal reservoir. Early treatment works better because it interrupts amplification before the neutrophil wave is fully established.

Mechanism

Monosodium urate crystals are phagocytosed, triggering NLRP3 assembly, caspase-1 activation, and cleavage of pro-IL-1 beta into active IL-1 beta. Endothelium and chemokines recruit neutrophils, which release proteases, oxidants, and extracellular traps that magnify pain and edema. Colchicine binds beta-tubulin and prevents microtubule polymerization, impairing neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, degranulation, and inflammasome organization. NSAIDs inhibit COX-derived prostaglandins; glucocorticoid-receptor signaling represses NF-kappaB/AP-1 and cytokine transcription. None directly inhibits xanthine oxidase or increases urate excretion.

Boxed Warning

Choice is constrained by toxicity. Colchicine can become fatal when renal or hepatic impairment is combined with strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibition. NSAIDs can cause GI bleeding, kidney injury, hypertension, heart failure, and cardiovascular events. Corticosteroids can worsen glucose, infection, mood, and fluid effects. Suspected septic arthritis, especially a hot single joint with systemic illness or immunosuppression, requires aspiration and urgent evaluation rather than automatic gout treatment.

Adverse Effects

  • Colchicine: diarrhea, vomiting, cytopenias, neuromyopathy, rhabdomyolysis, multiorgan toxicity; NSAIDs: bleeding, renal and cardiovascular injury

  • Corticosteroids: hyperglycemia, fluid retention, mood or sleep change, infection worsening, and rebound risk if the clinical course is mismatched

Contraindications

  • Review kidney/liver function, CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors, anticoagulation, ulcer/bleeding history, heart failure, blood pressure, diabetes, and infection concern.

  • Do not use colchicine as a general analgesic or exceed the low-dose flare regimen because pain persists.

Nursing Essentials

  • Treat early, reassess pain and joint function, protect hydration, and identify fever, bacteremia risk, prosthetic joint, immunosuppression, or atypical presentation that requires aspiration.

  • If established urate-lowering therapy is already being taken, a flare is generally treated without reflexively stopping the preventive drug.

Interactions

  • Clarithromycin, azoles, protease inhibitors, cyclosporine, verapamil/diltiazem, grapefruit, and P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibition can raise colchicine exposure.

  • NSAIDs interact with anticoagulants, antiplatelets, RAAS blockers, and diuretics; corticosteroids add hyperglycemia and immunosuppression.

Key Labs

  • Renal and liver function, CBC when colchicine toxicity is possible, glucose with steroids, bleeding context with NSAIDs, and synovial fluid when infection or crystal diagnosis is uncertain

  • Serum urate is useful longitudinally but can be normal during an acute flare and does not replace joint evaluation

Nclex Traps

  • Colchicine is anti-inflammatory, not a pain receptor drug and not a urate-lowering drug.

  • A normal serum urate during the attack does not exclude gout, and crystals do not exclude simultaneous septic arthritis.
