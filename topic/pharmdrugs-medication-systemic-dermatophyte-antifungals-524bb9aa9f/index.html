Class Example Names

  • Terbinafine

Used To Treat

Dermatophyte infections requiring systemic therapy, especially nail or scalp disease and extensive/refractory tinea after organism and site are confirmed.

Description

Terbinafine and griseofulvin reach newly forming keratin but attack fungi differently. Terbinafine inhibits fungal squalene epoxidase, depleting ergosterol and accumulating toxic squalene; it is fungicidal against many dermatophytes and is central to onychomycosis. Griseofulvin binds fungal microtubules, disrupts mitosis, and deposits in keratin precursor cells; infected keratin must grow out, and it remains useful for selected tinea capitis organisms and pediatric contexts.

Mechanism

Terbinafine acts early in ergosterol synthesis independently of fungal CYP51, so its CYP2D6 inhibition is a human pharmacokinetic interaction rather than its antifungal target. Griseofulvin binds tubulin and the mitotic spindle, arrests fungal division, and creates newly keratinized tissue resistant to invasion. Neither drug treats bacteria or non-dermatophyte fungi automatically; KOH, culture, or nail pathology prevents months of wrong therapy.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Oral terbinafine is contraindicated in chronic or active liver disease and can cause liver failure, severe skin reactions, permanent taste/smell disturbance, cytopenias, depression, lupus, or thrombotic microangiopathy. Griseofulvin is contraindicated in pregnancy, porphyria, and hepatocellular failure and is a CYP inducer that can reduce contraceptive and warfarin effects.

Adverse Effects

  • Terbinafine: hepatotoxicity, taste/smell loss, rash, GI effects, depression and cytopenias

  • Griseofulvin: headache, GI upset, photosensitivity, rash, hepatotoxicity and leukopenia

  • Treatment failure from wrong organism, poor adherence, reinfection, or slow nail/hair outgrowth

Contraindications

  • Terbinafine: chronic or active liver disease and prior serious allergy

  • Griseofulvin: pregnancy, porphyria and hepatocellular failure

  • Clarify non-dermatophyte infection, major CYP interactions, alcohol use and reproductive plans

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm fungal diagnosis and site before long systemic courses; culture or nail biopsy matters when appearance is nonspecific.

  • Explain that clinical cure follows growth of healthy hair/nail and may lag microbiologic killing by months.

Interactions

  • Terbinafine inhibits CYP2D6 and can raise tricyclic, SSRI, beta-blocker, antiarrhythmic and other substrate exposure.

  • Griseofulvin induces CYP enzymes, reducing warfarin and hormonal-contraceptive effect; alcohol can cause a disulfiram-like reaction.

  • Fat improves absorption of some griseofulvin formulations; product and particle size matter.

Key Labs

  • Baseline liver tests for systemic therapy and repeat when symptomatic or clinically directed

  • CBC for prolonged treatment or infection symptoms

  • KOH, fungal culture or nail pathology; pregnancy testing for griseofulvin

Nclex Traps

  • Terbinafine blocks squalene epoxidase, not fungal CYP51.

  • Griseofulvin protects newly made keratin; old infected nail does not instantly look normal.

  • A thick yellow nail can be trauma or psoriasis, so appearance alone does not justify months of hepatotoxic therapy.
