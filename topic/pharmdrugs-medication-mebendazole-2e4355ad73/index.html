Used To Treat

Enterobius vermicularis pinworm, Trichuris whipworm, Ascaris roundworm, and hookworm infections according to product and regimen, with guideline use for selected other helminths. Reinfection control is often as important as the tablet.

Mechanism

Mebendazole occupies the colchicine-sensitive binding site on helminth beta-tubulin and prevents polymerization with alpha-tubulin. Parasite intestinal cells lose microtubule-dependent glucose transport and secretory vesicle movement. Glycogen stores decline, oxidative phosphorylation and fumarate-linked ATP production falter, and mitosis fails. Mammalian tubulin has lower drug affinity and oral bioavailability is low because of poor absorption and first-pass metabolism, producing selective luminal exposure. High-fat food and hepatic impairment can raise systemic concentrations. The drug kills susceptible larvae and adults more reliably than eggs; surviving environmental eggs can be swallowed again, which is why repeat treatment and environmental control are mechanistically necessary for pinworm.

Contraindications

  • Serious benzimidazole hypersensitivity and avoid concurrent metronidazole because of severe skin-reaction reports.

  • Do not assume persistent itching means resistance; evaluate reinfection, incomplete household control, dermatitis, or another diagnosis.

Nursing Essentials

  • For pinworm, explain environmental persistence and repeat-dose timing. Treating the patient while ignoring bedding, hands, nails, and close contacts can recreate infection.

  • For prolonged or off-label systemic courses, monitor CBC and liver tests; short single-dose intestinal regimens do not justify careless extension.

Interactions

  • Metronidazole should be avoided; cimetidine can raise systemic mebendazole exposure, while carbamazepine or phenytoin can lower it.

  • Hepatic impairment and high-fat intake can increase concentrations during prolonged therapy.

Key Labs

  • Usually no routine labs for brief uncomplicated use; CBC and liver tests for prolonged/high-dose therapy or hepatic risk

  • Stool or tape-test strategy, symptom recurrence, household exposure, hygiene, rash/mucosal findings, and neurologic symptoms

Nclex Traps

  • Repeat pinworm treatment is aimed at worms emerging after eggs survive, not because the first dose necessarily failed.

  • Mebendazole and albendazole share beta-tubulin inhibition, but mebendazole is generally more lumen-focused.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Prolonged or high-dose therapy can cause neutropenia, agranulocytosis, liver injury, alopecia, and glomerulonephritis. Concomitant metronidazole has been associated with Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis and should be avoided. Treat new mucosal lesions, blistering rash, fever, sore throat, jaundice, or dark urine as urgent. Pregnancy use requires risk-benefit assessment.

Description

Mebendazole is a benzimidazole anthelmintic that remains largely in the gastrointestinal lumen and binds parasite beta-tubulin. Loss of microtubules stops glucose uptake, intracellular transport, and cell division, depleting glycogen and ATP until intestinal worms immobilize and die. Lower systemic absorption makes it well suited to common luminal nematodes but less reliable than albendazole for many tissue infections. Pinworm eggs survive in the environment and drugs do not reliably kill every egg, so repeat dosing, hand hygiene, laundering, short nails, morning bathing, and household assessment explain why symptoms can recur despite an initially effective dose.

Adverse Effects

  • Abdominal pain, diarrhea, flatulence, nausea, vomiting, and rash, especially with heavy worm burden

  • Neutropenia/agranulocytosis, severe skin reaction, hepatitis, alopecia, seizure in young children, and glomerulonephritis during prolonged high-dose use
