Plain Language

Alcohol withdrawal is the body's overactive response when a person who has become physically dependent on alcohol suddenly stops drinking or sharply cuts down. It can begin with shaking, sweating, anxiety, nausea, and poor sleep, but severe withdrawal can cause seizures, dangerous confusion called delirium, and death.

Why It Matters

Alcohol withdrawal can worsen quickly even when the first symptoms look mild or alcohol is still measurable in the blood. Early risk assessment and correctly monitored treatment prevent seizures, delirium, aspiration, injury, abnormal heart rhythms, and death while creating a bridge to treatment for alcohol use disorder.

Definition

Alcohol withdrawal is a substance-withdrawal syndrome that follows cessation of, or a substantial reduction in, heavy and prolonged alcohol use. The DSM-5-TR diagnostic frame requires at least two characteristic symptoms developing within hours to a few days, such as autonomic hyperactivity, hand tremor, insomnia, nausea or vomiting, transient hallucinations, agitation, anxiety, or a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Withdrawal severity is separate from the diagnosis: a symptom scale helps monitor an established syndrome but does not by itself prove that alcohol is the cause.

Pathology

Long-term alcohol exposure strengthens inhibitory gamma-aminobutyric acid signaling and drives compensatory excitatory signaling. When alcohol is abruptly removed, the adapted brain is left in a hyperexcitable state, producing tremor, autonomic activation, anxiety, hallucinations, and seizures. Repeated withdrawals can increase later severity through kindling. Dehydration, malnutrition, liver disease, infection, trauma, and simultaneous sedative withdrawal can magnify the danger without being caused by withdrawal itself.

Pathophysiology

  • Alcohol acutely enhances inhibitory signaling and suppresses excitatory signaling. With sustained exposure, the nervous system adapts; rapid reduction then unmasks excess excitation and sympathetic activity.

  • Symptoms often begin about 6 to 24 hours after reduction or cessation, but timing varies with the drinking pattern, other sedatives, age, liver function, and treatment. Withdrawal seizures most often occur within 8 to 48 hours, while delirium may emerge later; a patient does not have to move through each stage in order.

  • A seizure can occur without dramatic earlier symptoms, and a positive blood alcohol concentration does not exclude withdrawal when the concentration has fallen substantially from the person's usual level.

Etiology

The immediate cause is a marked fall in alcohol exposure after heavy, prolonged use has produced physical dependence. Hospitalization, surgery, illness, incarceration, inability to obtain alcohol, vomiting, an attempted home detoxification, or an abrupt personal decision to stop may trigger the fall. Alcohol withdrawal is not the same as a hangover, alcohol intoxication, or alcohol use disorder, although a patient with true withdrawal needs formal assessment for alcohol use disorder and other substance use.

Risk Factors

  • Previous alcohol-withdrawal seizure or alcohol-withdrawal delirium, which strongly raises concern for another complicated withdrawal

  • Many prior withdrawal episodes, long duration of heavy regular drinking, or a prior need for unusually intensive medication

  • Current marked autonomic symptoms, seizure, delirium, hallucinations, or a high-risk prediction score interpreted within a clinical assessment

  • Concurrent benzodiazepine, barbiturate, gabapentinoid, or other sedative dependence or withdrawal

  • Older age, pregnancy, significant liver, cardiac, neurologic, or pulmonary disease, acute infection or trauma, poor nutrition, or major electrolyte disturbance

  • Limited supervision, inability to take oral medication, unreliable follow-up, active suicide risk, or an unsafe recovery environment

Signs Symptoms

  • Hand tremor, sweating, anxiety, irritability, headache, insomnia, vivid dreams, nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, and sensitivity to light or sound

  • Tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, fever, hyperreflexia, restlessness, agitation, and worsening symptoms as the alcohol level falls

  • Transient visual, tactile, or auditory hallucinations with clear awareness, or progressive disorientation and inattention when delirium develops

  • Generalized tonic-clonic seizure, which may occur before other symptoms appear

  • Dehydration, orthostasis, weakness, ataxia, malnutrition, and coexisting signs of injury, infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, liver disease, pancreatitis, or another withdrawal syndrome

Diagnostics

  • Establish the time of the last drink and last major reduction, usual type and amount, duration and daily pattern, prior withdrawals and seizures, prior delirium, treatment history, other sedatives or substances, medications, pregnancy, and medical and psychiatric illness. Collateral history is valuable when cognition is impaired.

  • Use DSM-5-TR clinical requirements to diagnose the syndrome and use a validated scale such as CIWA-Ar only to quantify and trend symptoms in an appropriate communicative patient. CIWA-Ar is not a diagnostic test and is unreliable when delirium, severe communication barriers, intubation, or major confounding illness is present; use an appropriate objective or delirium-capable protocol instead.

  • Perform serial mental-status, neurologic, cardiopulmonary, hydration, trauma, infection, abdominal, and skin examinations. A first seizure, focal finding, unusual seizure pattern, persistent altered state, or time course inconsistent with withdrawal requires evaluation for another cause.

  • Determine the level of care from predicted complication risk, current severity, comorbidity, pregnancy, medication and substance exposure, ability to take oral therapy, monitoring capacity, social support, and ability to return promptly if symptoms worsen.

Labs

  • No blood alcohol level or laboratory test confirms or excludes alcohol withdrawal. A positive alcohol concentration does not provide reassurance when the level is falling and symptoms fit the history.

  • Depending on severity and setting, evaluate bedside glucose, electrolytes including magnesium and phosphate, renal and liver function, complete blood count, acid-base status, pregnancy, ECG, toxicology, and alcohol concentration without delaying urgent treatment.

  • Order infection studies, lipase, ammonia, head imaging, EEG, lumbar puncture, cardiac testing, or other studies only when the history or examination raises a competing emergency; abnormalities may coexist with withdrawal rather than replace it.

Assessment

Start with airway, breathing, circulation, bedside glucose, vital-sign trend, orientation, attention, tremor, sweating, hallucinations, agitation, hydration, gait, trauma, and seizure precautions. Ask exactly when and how much alcohol was reduced, whether seizures or delirium occurred before, what sedatives are also used, and whether the patient is pregnant, suicidal, medically ill, or unable to remain safely monitored. Reassess after each treatment step for symptom control, oversedation, respiratory depression, and a new diagnosis that the withdrawal label could hide.

Differential

  • Alcohol intoxication, hangover, alcohol-induced psychotic disorder, and primary psychotic or mood illness

  • Benzodiazepine, barbiturate, or other sedative-hypnotic withdrawal; stimulant intoxication; anticholinergic or sympathomimetic toxicity; or mixed substance exposure

  • Sepsis, meningitis or encephalitis, hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, thyrotoxicosis, hypoxia, hepatic encephalopathy, uremia, electrolyte disturbance, and medication toxicity

  • Traumatic brain injury, intracranial hemorrhage, stroke, epilepsy, nonconvulsive seizure, and Wernicke encephalopathy

  • Postoperative or ICU delirium, pain, panic, essential tremor, gastrointestinal illness, and any condition that independently causes tachycardia, sweating, fever, agitation, vomiting, or confusion

Treatments

  • Treat the clinical syndrome and predicted risk without waiting for the alcohol level to reach zero. Benzodiazepines are first-line medication for moderate, severe, seizure-complicated, or delirium-complicated withdrawal; diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, and lorazepam are representative agents selected and administered through a setting-specific symptom-triggered, fixed-dose, or front-loading protocol.

  • Choose the agent, route, interval, and level of monitoring from severity, prior response, age, liver function, respiratory risk, co-sedatives, and ability to take oral medication. Lorazepam or another agent with less hepatic metabolism may be preferred when substantial liver impairment is present; do not convert this principle into an unsupervised home regimen.

  • Phenobarbital can be an alternative or adjunct only for clinicians experienced with its narrow safety margin and in a setting able to monitor sedation and breathing. Carbamazepine or gabapentin may be protocol-specific alternatives for selected mild or moderate, low-risk withdrawal, but they do not replace adequate seizure- and delirium-preventive treatment in a high-risk patient.

  • Give thiamine to prevent or treat Wernicke encephalopathy, particularly when nutrition is poor or symptoms are severe. Correct glucose promptly when low; glucose must not be delayed while waiting for thiamine. Replace fluids, potassium, magnesium, phosphate, and nutrition according to measured deficits and clinical status rather than by routine blanket replacement.

  • After acute stabilization, diagnose and treat alcohol use disorder with a patient-centered plan that may include naltrexone, acamprosate, or disulfiram when appropriate, evidence-based behavioral treatment, mutual-support options, harm reduction, and a direct follow-up handoff. Withdrawal management alone is not continuing treatment.

Contraindications

  • Do not use beverage alcohol or intravenous alcohol to prevent or treat withdrawal.

  • Do not use antipsychotic medication alone for alcohol-withdrawal delirium or hallucinations; it may be an adjunct only after adequate withdrawal treatment because it does not prevent seizures or correct the core hyperexcitable state.

  • Do not use clonidine, dexmedetomidine, or a beta blocker alone to prevent or treat withdrawal seizures or delirium. These agents can mask autonomic signs while the underlying withdrawal remains active.

  • Do not use CIWA-Ar as a diagnostic test or as the sole guide in delirium, severe communication impairment, intubation, or major medical confounding.

  • Avoid unsupervised ambulatory detoxification when there is prior seizure or delirium, current severe or complicated withdrawal, unstable medical or psychiatric illness, pregnancy-related risk, inability to take oral medication, unsafe supports, or inadequate monitoring.

Safety Considerations

  • Monitor respiratory rate, oxygenation, arousal, blood pressure, falls, and cumulative sedative exposure during benzodiazepine or phenobarbital treatment; alcohol, opioids, and other sedatives increase oversedation and respiratory-depression risk.

  • Use seizure, fall, aspiration, and elopement precautions matched to the patient's state. Maintain a calm, well-lit, low-stimulation environment with frequent orientation, but never let a quiet room substitute for close observation.

  • A withdrawal scale can be falsely high from pain, fever, panic, or infection and falsely low from beta blockers, clonidine, sedation, or inability to report symptoms. Treat the patient and trend, not one score.

  • Confusion, eye movement abnormality, ataxia, or memory disturbance may signal Wernicke encephalopathy and requires prompt parenteral thiamine and medical evaluation rather than assuming ordinary withdrawal.

  • Restraint can worsen agitation and injury and is reserved for immediate safety under law and policy after de-escalation and adequate medical treatment; it never treats withdrawal.

Nursing Priorities

  • Record the last drink and last major reduction, usual amount and pattern, prior seizure or delirium, other sedatives, pregnancy status, comorbid illness, and baseline cognition before symptoms escalate.

  • Trend vital signs, orientation and attention, tremor, sweating, hallucinations, agitation, intake and output, gait, sleep, and the protocol-appropriate withdrawal measure at the ordered interval.

  • After each medication administration, reassess withdrawal symptoms together with arousal, respiratory rate, oxygenation, blood pressure, gait, and cumulative dose so undertreatment and oversedation are both recognized.

  • Provide thiamine, glucose correction, fluids, electrolytes, nutrition, quiet orientation, and seizure, fall, and aspiration precautions as ordered; inspect for hidden trauma, infection, bleeding, or pressure injury.

  • Use nonstigmatizing language and complete a warm handoff to alcohol-use treatment before discharge, including medication access, transportation, supports, emergency instructions, and a plan for return to drinking or worsening withdrawal.

Red Flags

  • Any seizure, repeated seizure, focal seizure, new neurologic deficit, head injury, or failure to return toward baseline

  • Disorientation, severe inattention, fluctuating consciousness, dangerous agitation, hallucinations with autonomic instability, or rapidly increasing medication need

  • Fever, severe tachycardia or hypertension, hypotension, chest pain, dysrhythmia, hypoxia, respiratory depression, or marked oversedation

  • Persistent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe dehydration, oliguria, critical electrolyte or glucose abnormality, jaundice, or severe abdominal pain

  • Pregnancy with withdrawal, suicidal or homicidal intent, suspected mixed sedative withdrawal or overdose, or an unsafe attempt to leave care

Complications

  • Generalized seizures, alcohol-withdrawal delirium, aspiration, traumatic injury, rhabdomyolysis, severe dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, and dysrhythmia

  • Respiratory depression, hypotension, delirium, falls, or aspiration from excessive or interacting sedative treatment

  • Unrecognized Wernicke encephalopathy, infection, head injury, gastrointestinal bleeding, pancreatitis, liver failure, or another simultaneous withdrawal syndrome

  • Return to drinking, recurrent withdrawal with kindling, repeated emergency care, and missed treatment for alcohol use disorder

  • Death from complicated withdrawal or a coexisting medical emergency

Prognosis

Uncomplicated symptoms commonly improve over several days with appropriately monitored treatment, but the course is not reliably predicted from the first examination. Prior complicated withdrawal, repeated episodes, and serious comorbidity increase risk. Successful detoxification resolves the immediate hyperexcitable state; it does not by itself treat alcohol use disorder, reverse chronic organ injury, or prevent another dangerous withdrawal.

Prevention

  • Identify heavy regular alcohol use and prior withdrawal before surgery, admission, incarceration, or a planned reduction so a supervised plan can begin before symptoms become severe.

  • Avoid advising a physically dependent patient to stop abruptly without risk assessment and access to an appropriate level of care.

  • Treat alcohol use disorder after withdrawal with evidence-based medication and behavioral options, recovery supports, and follow-up matched to patient goals.

  • Address nutrition, thiamine deficiency, housing, transportation, co-occurring mental illness, other substance use, and access to emergency care because these conditions influence recurrence and harm.

Patient Education

  • Withdrawal is the body's reaction to a rapid fall in alcohol after dependence; it is not simply a hangover and can become life-threatening.

  • Do not try to detox alone if you have had a withdrawal seizure or delirium, drink heavily every day, use benzodiazepines or other sedatives, are pregnant, or have serious medical or psychiatric illness.

  • A positive alcohol level does not make worsening tremor, sweating, hallucinations, or confusion safe; seek urgent care for seizure, severe confusion, chest pain, repeated vomiting, fainting, or trouble breathing.

  • Take only the prescribed withdrawal medicine and do not combine it with alcohol, opioids, sleep medicines, or someone else's medication.

  • Detoxification is the first step. Ask for a direct treatment plan for alcohol use disorder before discharge so the next crisis is less likely.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy requires prompt obstetric and addiction consultation and generally a lower threshold for inpatient monitoring because maternal seizure, delirium, dehydration, and hypoxia threaten both patient and fetus; medication decisions remain individualized.

  • Older adults may show less obvious autonomic activation, develop delirium from multiple causes, clear sedatives more slowly, and fall more easily; use cautious protocol-based treatment and broader medical evaluation.

  • Significant liver disease changes sedative selection and clearance and can mimic withdrawal through hepatic encephalopathy; trend arousal and use an agent and protocol appropriate to hepatic function.

  • Adolescents require confidential substance and safety assessment, guardian involvement according to law and safety, pediatric dosing expertise, and protection from an unsafe home environment.

  • ICU, postoperative, trauma, and intubated patients may be unable to complete CIWA-Ar; use an objective, setting-appropriate monitoring strategy and continue evaluating pain, infection, hypoxia, medication effects, and delirium.

Nclex Traps

  • A measurable blood alcohol concentration does not exclude withdrawal; a rapid fall from the person's usual level can trigger symptoms.

  • CIWA-Ar measures symptom severity after clinical diagnosis; it does not diagnose alcohol withdrawal and is not appropriate for an uncommunicative or delirious patient.

  • Benzodiazepines treat the core withdrawal syndrome and prevent seizures; antipsychotics, clonidine, and beta blockers are not seizure-preventing substitutes.

  • Give glucose immediately for hypoglycemia and give thiamine promptly; do not delay lifesaving glucose while waiting for thiamine.

  • Completing detoxification is not completion of alcohol use disorder treatment.
