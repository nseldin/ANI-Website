Used To Treat

Life-threatening muscarinic manifestations of organophosphate, nerve-agent, or severe carbamate cholinesterase-inhibitor poisoning, especially bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, bradycardia, and secretions that impair ventilation. It also has labeled and routine uses for selected bradycardia and secretion control, but this card emphasizes toxicologic rescue.

Mechanism

Atropine reversibly occupies muscarinic acetylcholine receptors without activating them. Cardiac M2 blockade reduces Gi signaling, restores adenylyl-cyclase and cAMP activity, decreases acetylcholine-activated potassium current, and steepens pacemaker depolarization at the SA node while improving conduction through the AV node. This nodal mechanism explains why atropine can help vagally mediated or nodal bradycardia but cannot repair diseased infranodal His-Purkinje tissue. At other parasympathetic effector organs, muscarinic blockade reduces glandular secretion, vagal slowing, bronchial smooth-muscle constriction, gastrointestinal hyperactivity, and bladder contraction. Atropine crosses the blood-brain barrier and can reduce some central muscarinic effects, but its decisive toxicology benefit is often a drier, more ventilatable airway. Nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction are a different receptor family, which explains why fasciculations and respiratory-muscle weakness can persist after the lungs sound drier. Because toxin may remain at the enzyme or continue to absorb, atropine may need rapid repeated escalation and then continued dosing.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): There is no ordinary contraindication that should delay atropine in a life-threatening cholinergic crisis. Glaucoma, urinary retention, and tachyarrhythmia concerns are secondary to restoring ventilation. Persistent wet lungs, hypoxemia, rising carbon dioxide, bradycardia, shock, seizures, or weakness despite treatment requires immediate atropine escalation, pralidoxime for qualifying organophosphate poisoning, ventilation, and critical-care toxicology support.

Contraindications

  • There is no ordinary contraindication that should delay atropine in a life-threatening cholinergic crisis. Glaucoma, urinary retention, and tachyarrhythmia concerns are secondary to restoring ventilation.

  • Atropine is not the antidote for an anticholinergic overdose; that toxidrome already has too little muscarinic signaling. Do not confuse 'cholinergic' with 'anticholinergic.'

Nursing Essentials

  • Wear the incident-specific personal protective equipment and prevent contaminated clothing, vomit, or secretions from exposing staff because organophosphate can be absorbed through skin or inhaled and create a second patient.

  • Use continuous suction, oxygenation, capnography, ECG, blood pressure, temperature, urine output, lung examination, and frequent neuromuscular assessment. Drying the mouth alone is not adequate; the reason to titrate is to restore gas exchange.

  • Prepare pralidoxime, benzodiazepines for seizures/agitation, and definitive airway support in parallel because atropine cannot reverse enzyme inhibition, nicotinic weakness, or all central toxicity.

  • Watch for over-atropinization after control: agitation or delirium, hyperthermia, absent bowel sounds/urinary retention, flushed dry skin, and marked tachycardia matter more than mydriasis alone.

  • For symptomatic bradycardia with poor perfusion, apply pacing pads and reassess both electrical rhythm and mechanical perfusion after atropine because infranodal block may not respond; begin protocol-directed transcutaneous pacing and advanced support without waiting through ineffective repeated doses.

Interactions

  • Succinylcholine and mivacurium can have prolonged effects when cholinesterase is inhibited because the same enzyme system helps terminate their neuromuscular blockade; airway medication selection should therefore follow an experienced toxicology or anesthesia protocol.

  • Other antimuscarinic medicines, including strongly anticholinergic antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, and antiparkinson drugs, add to atropine's thermal, urinary, bowel, ocular, cardiac, and delirium burden because their receptor effects converge.

Key Labs

  • Blood gas, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, renal/hepatic function, CK, ECG, chest imaging when aspiration or pulmonary edema is suspected, and continuous oxygenation/ventilation measures. Correct hypoxemia before attributing dysrhythmia solely to atropine.

  • Plasma butyrylcholinesterase and red-cell acetylcholinesterase can support diagnosis and trend exposure, but results vary by assay and must never delay atropine. Document clinical endpoints, cumulative dose, secretion burden, and recurrence.

Nclex Traps

  • Titrate to drying of bronchial secretions and improved ventilation, not to pupil size or a normal heart rate.

  • Atropine treats muscarinic effects; pralidoxime can reactivate organophosphate-inhibited acetylcholinesterase before aging and better addresses nicotinic weakness.

  • Protect rescuers first: contaminated skin and clothing can poison staff.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Children can develop hyperthermia and central antimuscarinic toxicity quickly because muscarinic blockade reduces sweating and small dosing errors create a larger weight-adjusted exposure; use indication-specific weight verification while never withholding lifesaving poisoning rescue.

  • Population

    Older adults and patients with anticholinergic vulnerability

    Note

    Older adults and patients with glaucoma, urinary outflow obstruction, ileus risk, or cognitive impairment are more susceptible to delirium, retention, constipation, blurred vision, and falls because age and comorbidity reduce reserve against antimuscarinic effects; these risks shape observation after immediate rescue rather than delaying it.

  • Population

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Life-threatening maternal cholinergic poisoning or unstable bradycardia threatens both maternal and fetal oxygen delivery, so emergency benefit takes priority while formulation, dose, fetal assessment, and follow-up use the applicable obstetric, toxicology, and product guidance.

Description

Atropine is a competitive muscarinic receptor antagonist that rapidly blocks excess acetylcholine signaling to dry dangerous bronchial secretions and relieve bronchospasm and bradycardia during cholinergic crisis. At cardiac M2 receptors, it removes parasympathetic braking at the SA and AV nodes, which can raise sinus rate and improve nodal conduction when excessive vagal tone is causing symptomatic bradycardia. Cholinesterase inhibition allows acetylcholine to accumulate, producing wet airways, wheeze, vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, miosis, bradycardia, and other muscarinic effects, plus nicotinic weakness and central seizures or coma. The treatment endpoint in poisoning is improving oxygenation and ventilation with drying of clinically dangerous secretions, not a normal pupil or a predetermined heart rate. Atropine does not reactivate acetylcholinesterase and does not reverse nicotinic paralysis; pralidoxime and ventilatory support address those parts of severe organophosphate poisoning. Atropine can fail in high-grade infranodal or His-Purkinje block because the diseased conduction tissue lies below the vagally influenced AV node, so transcutaneous pacing and advanced bradycardia support must not be delayed when perfusion is deteriorating.

Adverse Effects

  • Tachycardia, dysrhythmia, dry hot skin, hyperthermia, mydriasis, urinary retention, ileus, delirium, agitation, hallucination, and seizures can occur with excessive antimuscarinic effect.

  • Inadequate atropinization leaves bronchorrhea and bronchospasm untreated; this is often more immediately dangerous than tachycardia. Dose decisions therefore follow ventilation and secretion control.

Administration Timing

  • Protect staff from secondary contamination, remove the patient from exposure, begin airway suction and oxygenation, and give atropine immediately for life-threatening muscarinic findings; the AHA 2025 recommendation is Class 1, Level A and treatment must not wait for a cholinesterase result.

  • Repeat and rapidly escalate protocol-directed dosing until bronchorrhea and bronchospasm improve and ventilation becomes effective. Large cumulative doses may be necessary; fixed small bradycardia doses can be dangerously inadequate in a heavily contaminated patient.

  • Once control is achieved, continue a protocol-directed infusion or repeated dosing and titrate to recurrent secretions and ventilation. Wean only after the exposure is controlled and atropine requirements have remained stable.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent wet lungs, hypoxemia, rising carbon dioxide, bradycardia, shock, seizures, or weakness despite treatment requires immediate atropine escalation, pralidoxime for qualifying organophosphate poisoning, ventilation, and critical-care toxicology support.

  • Recurrent secretions during weaning mean active toxin effect persists. Resume the last effective rate and reassess dermal or gastrointestinal absorption and decontamination.

Evidence Limitations

  • Atropine needs are driven by bedside physiology and can vary by orders of magnitude. Auto-injector instructions apply to the specific product and mass-casualty context, while hospital treatment uses a monitored titration protocol.

  • Atropine corrects muscarinic physiology but not nicotinic paralysis or the poisoned enzyme, so apparent drying must not create false reassurance about respiratory strength.

Regulatory Status

FDA-labeled atropine products include use for organophosphorus or nerve-agent poisoning. Follow the exact stocked formulation, auto-injector instructions, and emergency protocol.
