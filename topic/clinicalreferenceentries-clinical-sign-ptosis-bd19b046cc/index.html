Definition

Ptosis means abnormal drooping of the upper eyelid when the person looks straight ahead. It can affect one or both eyes and may be constant or worsen with fatigue.

Plain Language

Ptosis is a drooping upper eyelid.

Why It Matters

New ptosis can signal a problem with the third cranial nerve, the sympathetic pathway, muscle strength, or the eyelid itself. Sudden ptosis with unequal pupils, double vision, abnormal eye movement, severe headache, neck or eye pain, or another neurologic change is urgent; ptosis with swallowing or breathing weakness can signal myasthenic crisis.

Associated Conditions

  • age-related or aponeurotic change, congenital levator dysfunction, myasthenia gravis, third-nerve or sympathetic pathway injury, muscle disease, trauma, or a mechanical eyelid mass

Assessment Method

Inspect both upper lids in primary gaze and downgaze, compare with prior photographs, measure lid position and levator movement when trained, and assess pupils in bright and dim light, extraocular movements, diplopia, fatigability, visual fields, brow compensation, trauma, medications, and bulbar or limb weakness.

Positive Finding

One upper eyelid margin sits lower than expected and may partly cover the pupil or visual axis; the finding may be unilateral, bilateral, constant, or fatigable.

Mechanism

Failure of the levator palpebrae muscle, its tendon-like aponeurosis, its oculomotor innervation, or sympathetic input to the smaller Muller muscle reduces upper-lid elevation; added weight or scarring can also pull the lid down.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Ptosis finding raises concern for age-related or aponeurotic change, congenital levator dysfunction, myasthenia gravis, third-nerve or sympathetic pathway injury, muscle disease, trauma, or a mechanical eyelid mass because failure of the levator palpebrae muscle, its tendon-like aponeurosis, its oculomotor innervation, or sympathetic input to the smaller Muller muscle reduces upper-lid elevation; added weight or scarring can also pull the lid down. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Ptosis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Ptosis to focus evaluation of age-related or aponeurotic change, congenital levator dysfunction, myasthenia gravis, third-nerve or sympathetic pathway injury, muscle disease, trauma, or a mechanical eyelid mass and the reviewed alternatives: brow ptosis, dermatochalasis, pseudoptosis, contralateral lid retraction, blepharospasm. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect both upper lids in primary gaze and downgaze, compare with prior photographs, measure lid position and levator movement when trained, and assess pupils in bright and dim light, extraocular movements, diplopia, fatigability, visual fields, brow compensation, trauma, medications, and bulbar or limb weakness. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Ptosis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Brow position, dermatochalasis, globe position, fatigue, photographs, and examiner technique affect apparent lid height; pseudoptosis and opposite-side lid retraction can create false asymmetry, and a normal pupil does not make sudden ptosis benign. Therefore, Ptosis cannot confirm or exclude age-related or aponeurotic change, congenital levator dysfunction, myasthenia gravis, third-nerve or sympathetic pathway injury, muscle disease, trauma, or a mechanical eyelid mass by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • brow ptosis

  • dermatochalasis

  • pseudoptosis

  • contralateral lid retraction

  • blepharospasm

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as brow ptosis, dermatochalasis, pseudoptosis, contralateral lid retraction, blepharospasm.

False Negative Situations

Ptosis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of age-related or aponeurotic change, congenital levator dysfunction, myasthenia gravis, third-nerve or sympathetic pathway injury, muscle disease, trauma, or a mechanical eyelid mass.

Modern Status

Current descriptive sign, not a diagnosis; new ptosis can localize neurologic disease, while persistent childhood ptosis can obstruct vision and contribute to amblyopia. Interpret Ptosis in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Brow position, dermatochalasis, globe position, fatigue, photographs, and examiner technique affect apparent lid height; pseudoptosis and opposite-side lid retraction can create false asymmetry, and a normal pupil does not make sudden ptosis benign.

Safety Precautions

Observe Ptosis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect both upper lids in primary gaze and downgaze, compare with prior photographs, measure lid position and levator movement when trained, and assess pupils in bright and dim light, extraocular movements, diplopia, fatigability, visual fields, brow compensation, trauma, medications, and bulbar or limb weakness. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not assume new ptosis is age-related; sudden onset with severe headache, unequal pupils, diplopia, abnormal eye movement, facial or limb deficit, neck or eye pain, trauma, or reduced consciousness requires urgent neurologic or ophthalmic evaluation, and ptosis with dysphagia, weak cough, breathing difficulty, or rapidly worsening fatigable weakness requires emergency assessment.
