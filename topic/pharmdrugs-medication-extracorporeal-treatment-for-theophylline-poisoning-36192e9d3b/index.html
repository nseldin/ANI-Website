Used To Treat

Severe theophylline poisoning with seizure, life-threatening dysrhythmia, shock, clinical deterioration, a rising concentration despite treatment, or EXTRIP concentration and risk criteria. Chronic toxicity can be life-threatening at lower concentrations than an acute ingestion.

Description

Theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index and produces a dangerous catecholamine-dominant syndrome when clearance fails or a large dose is absorbed. Adenosine-receptor antagonism and phosphodiesterase inhibition drive tachycardia, vomiting, tremor, agitation, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, lactic acidosis, dysrhythmia, and seizures. Once seizures or unstable rhythms begin, deterioration can be abrupt and difficult to control. The drug's relatively small distribution volume and modest protein binding make intermittent hemodialysis highly effective. EXTRIP recommends ECTR for severe clinical findings and selected high or rising concentrations, with lower concern thresholds in chronic exposure and vulnerable patients. MDAC should continue when safe because it enhances theophylline elimination through the gut.

Mechanism

Hemodialysis exposes blood to a high-flux membrane, allowing unbound theophylline to diffuse rapidly into dialysate. Its low molecular weight, limited distribution volume, and substantial circulating fraction permit high clearance. As plasma falls, drug returns from tissues and remains available for removal. MDAC supplies a second clearance route by adsorbing residual gut drug and capturing theophylline that diffuses from blood into the intestinal lumen, maintaining a favorable gradient. ECTR also helps correct acid-base and electrolyte abnormalities, but potassium can shift rapidly as catecholamine effects abate, so replacement must be careful. Neither dialysis nor charcoal directly stops an active seizure; benzodiazepines and critical-care stabilization proceed simultaneously.

Administration Timing

  • Call Poison Help/medical toxicology early, stop theophylline, control airway and seizures, obtain continuous ECG and serial concentrations, correct volume depletion, and use MDAC when airway and bowel function are safe.

  • Start ECTR without waiting for another level when seizures, life-threatening dysrhythmia, or shock is present. Also apply current EXTRIP acute versus chronic concentration, age, comorbidity, rising-level, and clinical-deterioration criteria because chronic toxicity becomes severe at lower levels.

  • Intermittent hemodialysis is preferred. Continue MDAC during ECTR when safe, repeat levels during and after treatment, and monitor for rebound from sustained-release absorption or tissue redistribution.

Nursing Essentials

  • Treat any theophylline-associated seizure as a high-risk ECTR trigger, give benzodiazepines promptly, and avoid assuming a short convulsion is benign because recurrent seizures and acidosis can amplify dysrhythmia risk.

  • Use continuous rhythm/12-lead ECG, blood pressure, temperature, mental status, glucose, potassium, magnesium, acid-base, and urine-output monitoring. Replace potassium cautiously because intracellular shift, rather than total-body depletion alone, can reverse quickly as toxicity improves.

  • Identify acute ingestion versus chronic accumulation, formulation, dosing error, smoking change, fever, liver/heart disease, interacting antibiotics, age, and all serial levels because the same concentration has different risk in chronic and acute exposure.

  • Protect the airway for charcoal, watch bowel function, and independently document MDAC and dialysis timing. Be prepared to adjust doses of anticonvulsants and other medicines cleared by ECTR.

Key Labs

  • Serial theophylline concentration with clear acute/chronic context; potassium, magnesium, calcium, glucose, blood gas, lactate, creatinine, liver tests, CK, CBC and serial ECG/QRS/QT/rhythm.

  • During and after ECTR, repeat concentration, electrolytes, glucose, pH/lactate, hemodynamics and neurologic status until there is no rebound and the patient remains clinically stable.

Adverse Effects

  • ECTR can cause hypotension, access bleeding/infection, hypothermia, dysrhythmia, electrolyte change, and unintended drug removal. MDAC can cause aspiration, vomiting, obstruction, or electrolyte disturbance.

  • Aggressive potassium replacement can cause rebound hyperkalemia after beta-adrenergic intracellular shift resolves; use frequent levels and ECG rather than a one-time deficit calculation.

Contraindications

  • ECTR is not for routine therapeutic monitoring or a stable patient with a mildly high concentration. Conversely, do not withhold it from a seizing or shocked patient while waiting for a numerical threshold.

  • MDAC is unsafe with an unprotected airway, ileus, obstruction, or perforation risk. Unstable hemodynamics require modality and resuscitation planning, not abandonment of indicated toxin removal.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Seizure, ventricular dysrhythmia, severe hypotension/shock, rapidly rising concentration, worsening acidosis, or clinical deterioration requires immediate ECTR-capable critical care.

  • Post-dialysis rebound or renewed vomiting, tachycardia, tremor, hypokalemia, acidosis, or neurologic symptoms requires repeat levels, renewed MDAC when safe, and possible additional ECTR.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP combines pharmacokinetic evidence, observational data, and case experience; acute and chronic thresholds are not interchangeable, and clinical severity can override concentration.

  • Caffeine is another methylxanthine but should not automatically inherit theophylline concentration thresholds. Use its own toxicology guidance.

Nclex Traps

  • Chronic theophylline toxicity can cause severe seizure or dysrhythmia at a lower concentration than an acute ingestion.

  • Seizure is an urgent dialysis indication; do not wait for a second level.

  • Continue MDAC when safe because it enhances elimination while HD clears the circulating compartment.

Regulatory Status

EXTRIP consensus rescue, not an FDA indication. Acute/chronic criteria, modality, and stopping decisions require medical toxicology and nephrology.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): ECTR is not for routine therapeutic monitoring or a stable patient with a mildly high concentration. Conversely, do not withhold it from a seizing or shocked patient while waiting for a numerical threshold. Seizure, ventricular dysrhythmia, severe hypotension/shock, rapidly rising concentration, worsening acidosis, or clinical deterioration requires immediate ECTR-capable critical care.
