Definition

Head trauma develops when external force causes primary brain, skull, vascular, and soft-tissue injury followed by preventable secondary damage from hypoxia, hypotension, edema, or bleeding. The characteristic pattern is loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, severe headache, seizure, focal deficit, unequal pupils, anticoagulant use, or a dangerous mechanism. Confirm or stage it with serial Glasgow Coma Scale, pupils, motor examination, glucose, and oxygenation; distinguish it from intoxication, hypoglycemia, stroke, seizure, syncope, and isolated facial injury by event history, examination, glucose, and imaging. Signs Symptoms

  • Pain, swelling, deformity, wound depth, drainage, odor, tissue color, blistering, numbness/tingling, pulses, movement, sensation, capillary refill, fever, or mechanism of injury shapes tissue-risk assessment.

  • Escalate absent pulses, compartment syndrome signs, evisceration, circumferential burn compromise, spreading infection, necrosis, or pain out of proportion.

Diagnostics

  • serial Glasgow Coma Scale, pupils, motor examination, glucose, and oxygenation

  • noncontrast head CT according to validated risk and urgent neurologic findings

  • cervical-spine and vascular imaging when mechanism or deficits indicate

  • Distinguish Head trauma from intoxication, hypoglycemia, stroke, seizure, syncope, and isolated facial injury by event history, examination, glucose, and imaging.

Labs

  • CK and potassium may rise with muscle breakdown

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • albumin/prealbumin may reflect nutrition risk

Treatments

  • protect airway, oxygenation, perfusion, and cervical alignment

  • reverse clinically important anticoagulation and obtain neurosurgical care for significant hemorrhage

  • control pain, agitation, temperature, glucose, and seizures while avoiding hypotension

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for a Glasgow Coma Scale decline, unequal pupils, new deficit, seizure, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, clear ear or nose drainage, or anticoagulant-associated symptoms because external force causes primary brain, skull, vascular, and soft-tissue injury followed by preventable secondary damage from hypoxia, hypotension, edema, or bleeding.

  • Maintain cervical-spine protection while assessing airway, breathing, circulation, and major bleeding because a head injury may accompany an unstable spine or extracranial hemorrhage.

  • Record the mechanism, loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, seizure, anticoagulants, alcohol or drug exposure, and last-known-normal state because these factors change imaging and observation urgency.

  • Trend Glasgow Coma Scale components, pupils, speech, limb strength, behavior, headache, and vomiting with consistent sedation documentation because deterioration may be the first sign of an expanding intracranial lesion.

  • Prevent hypoxemia, hypotension, fever, and marked glucose derangement and keep the head midline when ordered because injured brain tissue is especially vulnerable to reduced oxygen delivery.

  • Use seizure, aspiration, fall, and agitation precautions; provide ordered analgesia and antiemetics while avoiding unnecessary oversedation that masks the examination because external force causes primary brain, skull, vascular, and soft-tissue injury followed by preventable secondary damage from hypoxia, hypotension, edema, or bleeding.

Complications

  • or intraparenchymal hemorrhage can complicate Head trauma.

  • herniation can complicate Head trauma.

  • seizure can complicate Head trauma.

  • hydrocephalus can complicate Head trauma.

  • cognitive disability can complicate Head trauma.

  • missed cervical or blunt vascular injury

Red Flags

  • Declining alertness, inability to awaken, or new confusion

  • Unequal pupils, new weakness, slurred speech, or seizure

  • Repeated vomiting, worsening severe headache, or fluid or blood from the ear or nose

  • Any deterioration in a patient taking an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug

Patient Education

  • Explain that a normal early appearance does not exclude delayed bleeding; written observation instructions and a responsible adult are important after discharge.

  • Seek emergency care for worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, weakness, seizure, unusual behavior, or difficulty waking, and avoid driving, alcohol, and risky activity until cleared.

Nclex Traps

  • A temporarily lucid interval can precede epidural deterioration

  • Do not attribute declining consciousness to alcohol until intracranial injury and hypoglycemia are assessed
