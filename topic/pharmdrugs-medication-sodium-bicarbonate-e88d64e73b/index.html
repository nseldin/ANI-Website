Used To Treat

Life-threatening cardiotoxicity from sodium-channel-blocking poisons, especially QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia, hypotension, or arrest, under poison-center and resuscitation protocols. Other acid-base and electrolyte uses are separate indications.

Mechanism

The sodium load increases the extracellular sodium gradient, while alkalemia reduces protonated drug binding to sodium channels and can increase protein binding of some toxins. Together these effects improve phase-zero depolarization, narrow the QRS, and support contractility. Excess treatment causes hypernatremia, alkalemia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, and volume burden; therefore the ECG and physiology, not an arbitrary number of ampules, define response. Ventilation matters because retained carbon dioxide crosses cells and can undermine intended alkalinization.

Contraindications

  • No simple absolute contraindication overrides life-threatening channel blockade, but established alkalemia, severe hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, or inability to ventilate requires immediate specialist adjustment rather than blind repeat dosing.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm IV formulation and concentration, line patency, weight, toxin, ECG, and blood gas because concentrated bicarbonate is hypertonic and extravasation can injure tissue.

  • Trend ECG and electrolytes frequently and communicate cumulative mEq because improvement in QRS can occur before laboratory overshoot becomes obvious.

  • Coordinate benzodiazepines for seizures, vasopressors, temperature control, and advanced support because bicarbonate treats one mechanism rather than the whole poisoning.

Interactions

  • Do not mix bicarbonate in the same line with calcium-containing solutions because precipitation can occur.

  • Acidifying drugs or inadequate ventilation can oppose alkalinization, while diuretics and insulin can worsen treatment-associated hypokalemia.

Key Labs

  • Serial ECG, arterial or venous pH, sodium, potassium, chloride, ionized calcium, bicarbonate, glucose, lactate, creatinine, and toxin-specific studies guide efficacy and toxicity.

Nclex Traps

  • Bicarbonate treats toxic sodium-channel blockade through sodium loading and alkalemia, not by neutralizing the swallowed drug.

  • Do not give it routinely for every acidosis, hyperkalemia, or arrest.

  • A narrower QRS does not eliminate recurrence while the toxin remains present.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Use weight-based mEq and verify concentration because small-volume errors with hypertonic solutions can cause major sodium and pH shifts.

  • Population

    Heart or kidney failure

    Note

    Sodium and volume loads can worsen pulmonary edema, while impaired clearance makes electrolyte overshoot harder to correct.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal resuscitation takes priority, but fetal and maternal acid-base effects require obstetric and toxicology monitoring.

Boxed Warning

HYPERTONIC RESCUE WARNING: IV sodium bicarbonate for a wide-QRS poisoning requires ECG, ventilation, pH, sodium, potassium, and ionized-calcium monitoring. Do not substitute household baking soda or give repeated ampules without poison-center or resuscitation guidance.

Description

Sodium bicarbonate delivers concentrated sodium and raises serum pH to counteract toxic fast cardiac sodium-channel blockade from drugs such as tricyclic antidepressants, flecainide, or cocaine. AHA toxicology guidance describes 50 to 150 mEq for adults and 1 to 3 mEq/kg for children with life-threatening sodium-channel-blocker poisoning, repeated and titrated to ECG, perfusion, pH, and sodium under specialist protocol. It is not a routine drug for every cardiac arrest, acidosis, hyperkalemia, or overdose. The same ingredient name appears in baking soda, but household ingestion is not equivalent to monitored IV therapy.

Administration Timing

  • For life-threatening sodium-channel-blocker cardiotoxicity, AHA guidance describes an initial adult 50-150 mEq or pediatric 1-3 mEq/kg IV dose, with repeat dosing titrated under poison-center or toxicology direction.

  • Reassess QRS width, terminal R in aVR when relevant, rhythm, blood pressure, sodium, potassium, chloride, and pH after each intervention because both under-treatment and alkalemia are dangerous.

  • Continue infusion or repeat boluses only through an explicit protocol; secure ventilation and treat seizures concurrently because acidosis worsens channel blockade.

Adverse Effects

  • Hypernatremia, metabolic alkalosis, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hyperosmolality, volume overload, paradoxical intracellular acidosis, and tissue injury after extravasation can occur.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent wide-complex dysrhythmia, hypotension, seizure, acidosis, or arrest despite sodium loading requires toxicology-directed additional therapy and consideration of extracorporeal life support.

Evidence Limitations

  • Dose targets and infusion strategies vary by toxin and protocol; AHA ranges are not a bedside self-treatment recipe or proof of benefit for every sodium-channel blocker.
