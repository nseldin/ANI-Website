Definition

Pituitary apoplexy is an acute endocrine and neurologic emergency caused by hemorrhage and/or ischemic infarction, usually within a pituitary adenoma. Sudden sellar expansion can compress the optic pathways and cavernous-sinus cranial nerves while abruptly destroying ACTH secretion and causing life-threatening cortisol deficiency.

Pathology

Blood or infarcted swollen tissue expands inside the rigid sella turcica. Upward pressure threatens the optic chiasm, lateral pressure affects ocular-motor nerves in the cavernous sinus, and loss of corticotroph function removes cortisol support for vascular tone, glucose regulation, and the stress response.

Pathophysiology

  • Acute mass effect explains sudden headache, bitemporal field loss, reduced visual acuity, ptosis, diplopia, and ophthalmoplegia. Meningeal irritation from blood or necrotic tissue can mimic subarachnoid hemorrhage or meningitis.

  • ACTH deficiency can produce hypotension, shock, hypoglycemia, and hyponatremia. Other pituitary axes may also fail, and evolving water-balance disturbance can cause diabetes insipidus or SIADH during the acute course or after intervention.

Etiology

Many cases are spontaneous in a previously unrecognized adenoma. Reported precipitants include major surgery, anticoagulation or coagulopathy, pregnancy, head trauma, hypertension, and dynamic pituitary testing. These associations increase suspicion but are neither required nor sufficient by themselves.

Risk Factors

  • Known pituitary adenoma, especially a macroadenoma

  • Anticoagulation, coagulopathy, or a major hemodynamic stress

  • Pregnancy or the postpartum period

  • Major surgery, head trauma, hypertension, or recent pituitary stimulation testing

Signs Symptoms

  • Sudden severe headache with nausea, vomiting, photophobia, fever, or neck stiffness

  • Reduced visual acuity, bitemporal field loss, or sudden blindness

  • Diplopia, ptosis, anisocoria, or ophthalmoplegia from cranial nerve III, IV, or VI involvement

  • Altered consciousness, hypotension, weakness, hypoglycemia, or hyponatremia from acute adrenal insufficiency

  • Symptoms of an underlying pituitary mass or hormone excess may precede the event, but many patients have no known tumor

Diagnostics

  • Stabilization and empiric glucocorticoid treatment take priority when severe apoplexy with adrenal crisis or major neuro-ophthalmic findings is suspected; do not delay for imaging or a complete endocrine panel.

  • MRI of the pituitary and sellar region is preferred. A dedicated sellar CT is an alternative when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated; a routine head CT may miss the lesion.

  • Perform urgent visual acuity, pupils, fields, and extraocular-movement assessment, with formal neuro-ophthalmic examination when the patient is stable.

  • Obtain urgent endocrinology and neurosurgery input. Surgical decompression is considered after stabilization for severe or worsening visual loss, major persistent field deficit, or declining consciousness.

Labs

  • Check bedside glucose, electrolytes, renal and liver function, CBC, and coagulation studies immediately.

  • If blood can be drawn without delaying steroids, obtain cortisol, TSH and free T4, prolactin, IGF-1, LH and FSH, and appropriate sex hormones before glucocorticoid administration.

  • Trend sodium, serum and urine osmolality when indicated, urine output, and glucose because adrenal deficiency, SIADH, and diabetes insipidus can evolve rapidly.

  • A cortisol obtained after hydrocortisone cannot be interpreted as the untreated baseline; document timing precisely.

Assessment

Treat sudden severe headache with visual or ocular-motor change as a combined neurologic and endocrine emergency. Reassess airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness, pupils, acuity, fields, extraocular movements, blood pressure, glucose, sodium, urine output, and signs of adrenal crisis at short intervals, documenting change before and after steroid and hemodynamic support.

Differential

Differentiate pituitary apoplexy from aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, cavernous-sinus thrombosis, brainstem stroke, acute angle-closure glaucoma, migraine, and other sellar or optic lesions. It is distinct from Sheehan syndrome, which is postpartum pituitary ischemia associated with severe obstetric blood loss rather than acute expansion of a pituitary tumor.

Treatments

  • Stabilize airway, breathing, and circulation; treat hypoglycemia; obtain IV access; and provide protocol-based fluid and hemodynamic support.

  • Give empiric stress-dose hydrocortisone promptly when the patient is hemodynamically unstable, has impaired consciousness, or has severe visual acuity or field loss suggesting significant apoplexy.

  • Arrange urgent multidisciplinary endocrinology, neurosurgery, and neuro-ophthalmology management. Severe or deteriorating neuro-ophthalmic deficits may require decompression, while selected stable patients can be monitored conservatively.

  • After the emergency, evaluate every pituitary axis and replace persistent deficiencies in the correct sequence, with glucocorticoid coverage established before thyroid hormone when both cortisol and thyroid axes are deficient.

Contraindications

  • Do not delay lifesaving hydrocortisone for cortisol sampling, MRI, transfer, or a complete pituitary workup in an unstable patient.

  • Do not start thyroid hormone before glucocorticoid coverage when concurrent ACTH deficiency is possible because increased metabolic demand can precipitate adrenal crisis.

  • Lumbar puncture is not routine and may be unsafe with mass effect, coagulopathy, or another contraindication.

  • Anticoagulation continuation, holding, or reversal requires urgent individualized assessment; do not apply a routine pathway without weighing hemorrhage and thrombosis risks.

Safety Considerations

  • A normal general head CT does not exclude pituitary apoplexy.

  • Visual function and consciousness can worsen after the initial assessment; stable vital signs alone do not establish stability.

  • Steroids, surgery, and evolving pituitary injury can change sodium and water balance, requiring continued monitoring beyond the first hours.

  • Persistent hormone deficits may be lifelong, and missed glucocorticoid replacement can be fatal during future illness.

Nursing Priorities

  • Activate urgent endocrine and neurosurgical pathways while stabilizing ABCs, checking glucose, securing IV access, and administering ordered hydrocortisone without avoidable delay.

  • Perform and document serial Glasgow Coma Scale, pupils, visual acuity and fields, extraocular movements, ptosis, vital signs, and headache or vomiting pattern.

  • Trend sodium, glucose, intake and output, urine specific gravity or osmolality when ordered, and hemodynamic response to identify adrenal crisis, DI, or SIADH.

  • Prepare safely for urgent MRI, transfer, or surgery and maintain medication timing, especially glucocorticoid coverage.

  • After intervention, monitor for CSF leak, new visual change, polyuria, hyponatremia, hypotension, hypoglycemia, and other hormone deficiencies and teach emergency steroid planning when indicated.

Red Flags

  • Sudden severe headache with visual loss, field defect, diplopia, ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, or unequal pupils

  • Hypotension, shock, hypoglycemia, severe hyponatremia, or reduced consciousness

  • Rapid neurologic or visual deterioration

  • New high-volume dilute urine with rising sodium or severe symptomatic hyponatremia

  • Known pituitary tumor with an acute headache pattern unlike prior symptoms

Complications

  • Adrenal crisis, refractory shock, and death

  • Permanent visual loss or cranial neuropathy

  • Panhypopituitarism requiring long-term hormone replacement

  • Diabetes insipidus, SIADH, or dangerous sodium disturbance

  • Residual or recurrent pituitary tumor and postoperative CSF leak

Prognosis

Early recognition, hydrocortisone when indicated, and timely specialist management improve survival and visual outcome. Ocular-motor deficits often improve, but visual loss or endocrine deficits can persist. Long-term pituitary imaging, visual, and hormone follow-up remains necessary even after conservative recovery.

Prevention

There is no population prevention strategy. Patients with known pituitary tumors should know the emergency symptom pattern, maintain endocrine follow-up, and have anticoagulation, pregnancy, and major-surgery risks reviewed. People with ACTH deficiency need a current sick-day and emergency hydrocortisone plan.

Patient Education

  • A sudden severe headache with new visual or eye-movement change is an emergency, not a symptom to watch at home.

  • Take replacement hormones exactly as prescribed and never omit glucocorticoid sick-day instructions during illness.

  • Carry steroid emergency identification and supplies when adrenal insufficiency persists.

  • Report excessive thirst and urination, severe fatigue, vomiting, dizziness, fainting, or new visual symptoms promptly.

Special Populations

  • Pregnant and postpartum patients need immediate multidisciplinary endocrine, obstetric, neurologic, and neurosurgical assessment.

  • Anticoagulated or coagulopathic patients need urgent reversal-risk and thrombosis-risk balancing.

  • Children require pediatric endocrine and neurosurgical protocols and age-appropriate hormone assessment.

  • Older adults or people with cardiac disease need careful fluid and steroid monitoring during resuscitation.

Nclex Traps

  • The priority is hydrocortisone in suspected severe apoplexy with adrenal compromise; do not wait for cortisol or MRI.

  • A general CT can be negative; MRI of the pituitary is the preferred study.

  • Give glucocorticoid coverage before thyroid replacement when both axes may be deficient.

  • Serial visual and neurologic findings are more useful than a single reassuring examination.
