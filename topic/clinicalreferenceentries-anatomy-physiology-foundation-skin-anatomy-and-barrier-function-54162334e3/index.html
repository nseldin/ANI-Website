Summary

Skin is a layered organ that creates a water-resistant antimicrobial barrier, senses the environment, regulates heat exchange, participates in immune defense and vitamin D biology, and protects deeper tissue from mechanical and chemical injury. The avascular epidermis renews from basal cells; the vascular dermis supplies strength, sensation, appendages, and temperature control; subcutaneous tissue cushions and stores energy beneath it.

Quick Answer

The epidermis is the outer epithelial barrier, the dermis is the collagen-rich vascular support layer, and the hypodermis is subcutaneous tissue beneath the skin rather than a true skin layer. Barrier failure causes more than a surface problem: fluid loss, heat loss, infection, pain, impaired sensation, and systemic inflammation can follow.

Sections

    • Layers and location

    • The epidermis is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Basal keratinocytes divide, migrate outward, produce keratin and lipids, flatten, and become the anucleate corneocytes of the stratum corneum. Melanocytes transfer pigment, Langerhans cells participate in immune surveillance, and Merkel cells contribute to touch. The dermis has a superficial papillary layer and deeper reticular collagen-elastin network containing vessels, nerves, sensory receptors, follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands. The hypodermis contains adipose and connective septa that anchor, insulate, cushion, and permit skin mobility.

    • Barrier mechanism

    • The stratum corneum works like protein-rich bricks embedded in organized lipid mortar. Corneocytes resist mechanical stress, while extracellular lipids restrict uncontrolled water loss and entry of irritants or microbes. Tight junctions and antimicrobial peptides add protection. Sebum and sweat influence surface chemistry, and normal resident microbes compete with pathogens. The barrier is selective rather than sealed: transdermal drugs exploit lipid solubility and concentration gradients, while damaged, inflamed, very young, or thin skin absorbs some substances more readily. Repeated cleansing, adhesives, friction, moisture, and solvents can remove lipids or disrupt cells.

    • Thermoregulation, sensation, and relationships

    • Dermal vessels dilate to increase heat transfer and constrict to conserve heat. Eccrine sweat cools by evaporation, which becomes less effective in high humidity or severe dehydration. Subcutaneous fat reduces heat loss. Sensory endings detect touch, pressure, vibration, pain, itch, and temperature, prompting protective behavior. Hair and arrector pili have limited thermoregulatory value in humans but follicles remain important anatomic units. Skin interacts with circulation, autonomic nerves, endocrine signals, immunity, nutrition, mobility, continence, and cognition, which is why skin breakdown often reflects a whole-patient problem rather than poor topical care alone.

    • Pressure and wound physiology

    • External pressure, shear, and friction deform tissue and small vessels. Prolonged or intense deformation reduces perfusion, impairs lymph flow, and can injure muscle and subcutaneous tissue before surface discoloration appears. Moisture macerates the barrier and increases friction; edema increases diffusion distance; neuropathy removes warning pain; malnutrition reduces repair substrate; hyperglycemia impairs leukocyte and healing function; vascular disease limits oxygen delivery. Wound healing proceeds through hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation with granulation and epithelial migration, and remodeling. Infection, ischemia, repeated trauma, corticosteroids, or inadequate protein can stall the sequence.

    • Failure consequences

    • Superficial barrier loss causes pain, water loss, irritant entry, and infection risk. Deep burns lose fluid, temperature control, and immune protection while generating edema and systemic inflammatory stress. Pressure injury can extend into fat, muscle, tendon, or bone and lead to osteomyelitis or sepsis. Dermatitis increases transepidermal water loss and itch-scratch injury. Reduced sensation permits unnoticed heat, chemical, footwear, or pressure trauma. Extensive skin detachment from severe drug reactions can resemble burn physiology. Scar restores coverage but may contract, restrict movement, alter sensation, or lack glands and follicles.

    • Clinical connections, labs, and diagnostics

    • Inspecting skin helps identify perfusion, oxygenation, bleeding, infection, allergy, liver or renal disease, endocrine change, medication reactions, and nutritional problems. Blanching erythema differs from nonblanchable pressure injury. A Wood lamp, dermoscopy, scraping, culture, biopsy, vascular study, or imaging may clarify selected lesions or deep infection. CBC, glucose, renal and liver function, inflammatory markers, cultures, albumin context, and nutritional assessment can support a complex wound evaluation, but no single lab stages a pressure injury. Topical corticosteroids, retinoids, antimicrobials, emollients, adhesives, anticoagulants, vasopressors, and systemic immunosuppressants change skin risk.

    • Nursing assessment

    • Perform head-to-toe inspection with attention to bony prominences, devices, folds, perineum, heels, feet, lines, and areas under oxygen tubing or immobilizers. Assess color in a way appropriate to the patient's skin tone, temperature, moisture, turgor context, blanching, pain, sensation, edema, pulses, wound dimensions, depth, tissue type, drainage, odor after cleansing, edges, undermining, and surrounding skin. Use an approved pressure-risk tool as one part of judgment. Reassess after repositioning, device adjustment, incontinence care, perfusion change, or vasopressor escalation.

    • Nursing actions and why

    • Reposition at an individualized frequency, offload heels, minimize shear during transfers, use pressure-redistributing surfaces, manage moisture, and place protective barriers because prevention removes the mechanical and chemical drivers before tissue dies. Avoid massaging nonblanchable or ischemic areas. Maintain nutrition, hydration, glucose, oxygenation, and perfusion plans. Use ordered dressings that manage moisture without traumatizing new tissue. Measure consistently and photograph only under policy. Remove unnecessary devices and inspect beneath necessary ones. Teach daily foot and skin checks when neuropathy, vascular disease, immobility, or prior injury increases risk.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Escalate rapidly spreading erythema, severe pain out of proportion, crepitus, bullae, dusky change, skin anesthesia, fever, hypotension, or toxic appearance because necrotizing infection can advance quickly. Facial or mucosal blistering, target lesions, or widespread skin detachment after a medication may signal Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis. Circumferential burns can threaten circulation or ventilation. Black eschar with ischemic limb findings, infected pressure injury with systemic deterioration, or a wound exposing tendon, bone, or hardware requires urgent multidisciplinary evaluation.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • The hypodermis supports skin but is not one of its two principal layers. Darker skin may show pressure injury as persistent color, temperature, texture, or pain change rather than obvious redness. A dry wound is not always a healing wound; moisture balance depends on wound type. Albumin is influenced by inflammation and does not independently diagnose nutrition. Do not stage a wound backward as it heals, and do not use pressure-injury stages for every diabetic, arterial, venous, or moisture-associated lesion. Intact skin can cover deep tissue damage. Prevention requires pressure relief, not lotion alone.
