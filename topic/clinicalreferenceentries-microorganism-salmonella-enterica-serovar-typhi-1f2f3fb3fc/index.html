Definition

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi is a clinically important bacterium associated with typhoid fever, enteric fever, bacteremia, and chronic carriage. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi is a Gram-negative bacillus with a facultative anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, motile, and facultative intracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Safe water, sanitation, hand and food hygiene, vaccination for indicated risk, and public-health management of cases and carriers reduce spread. The reviewed route description is fecal oral transmission. The reviewed incubation estimate is 6 to 30 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    After intestinal invasion, survival within phagocytes and systemic dissemination produce enteric fever; the Vi capsule contributes to immune evasion. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi is associated with typhoid fever, enteric fever, bacteremia, and chronic carriage. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Blood culture is most useful early, stool culture can support later diagnosis or carriage assessment, and susceptibility testing guides public-health and clinical decisions. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
