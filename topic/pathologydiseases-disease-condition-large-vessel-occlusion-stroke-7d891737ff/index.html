Risk Factors

  • Atrial fibrillation, ventricular or valvular thrombus, and recent embolic events raise cardioembolic LVO risk.

  • Carotid, vertebral, or intracranial atherosclerosis can create local thrombosis or send a large embolus downstream.

  • Dissection, cancer-associated thrombosis, and other hypercoagulable states matter when age or history does not fit ordinary atherosclerotic risk.

Signs Symptoms

  • Gaze deviation with aphasia or neglect and unilateral weakness is a high-yield anterior LVO pattern.

  • Dense hemiplegia, severe field loss, global aphasia, or combined cortical deficits suggests a proximal vessel even when some symptoms fluctuate.

  • Diplopia, dysphagia, severe ataxia, quadriparesis, or declining consciousness suggests vertebrobasilar LVO.

Diagnostics

  • Use a rapid neurologic examination and validated LVO screen as a trigger, not a verdict, because sensitivity differs by territory.

  • CTA or MRA confirms the occlusion location and vascular anatomy, while NCCT excludes hemorrhage and estimates early infarct burden.

  • CTP or MRI mismatch imaging helps select some extended-window and large-core cases because remaining tissue and core size modify benefit.

Labs

  • Check glucose without delaying vascular imaging because hypoglycemia can imitate LVO signs.

  • Review anticoagulants, platelets, coagulation, renal function, and pregnancy context as required by the treatment pathway.

  • ECG and telemetry begin source evaluation because reopening the vessel does not prevent another cardiac embolus.

Treatments

  • Transfer or route rapidly to EVT-capable care within the local stroke system because procedural delay allows core growth.

  • Give IV thrombolysis promptly when eligible rather than withholding it solely because thrombectomy is planned; the parallel pathway avoids losing a chance for earlier reperfusion.

  • Perform mechanical thrombectomy when expert imaging and clinical criteria show likely benefit because a catheter can remove a clot too large for rapid pharmacologic dissolution.

Nursing Priorities

  • Recognize severe hemiparesis, aphasia, neglect, forced gaze, visual loss, decreased consciousness, or posterior-circulation signs because proximal arterial occlusion threatens a large brain territory and may need thrombectomy.

  • Establish last-known-well, baseline independence, glucose, anticoagulants, and transport destination while activating comprehensive stroke capability because vessel imaging and endovascular access should occur without avoidable interfacility delay.

  • Prepare emergency noncontrast and vascular imaging, intravenous thrombolysis when eligible, and thrombectomy workflow in parallel because one treatment evaluation should not unnecessarily postpone another beneficial reperfusion option.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, airway, oxygenation, blood pressure, rhythm, glucose, vomiting, and aspiration because edema, collateral failure, hemorrhage, and brainstem dysfunction can cause sudden deterioration.

  • Escalate immediately for NIH Stroke Scale increase, falling consciousness, unequal pupils, repeated vomiting, seizure, airway failure, or new hemodynamic instability because expanding core, malignant edema, basilar compromise, and hemorrhage demand emergency action.

Complications

  • Large infarction, malignant edema, herniation, and severe disability occur when a broad territory remains occluded.

  • Re-occlusion, distal embolization, vessel injury, access-site bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage can complicate EVT.

  • Aspiration, DVT, immobility injury, depression, and cognitive-communication impairment can persist after survival.

Contraindications

  • Do not equate low NIHSS with no LVO because disabling visual, language, hand, or posterior deficits may score modestly.

  • Do not delay IV thrombolysis for transfer when the patient is otherwise eligible and protocol supports parallel treatment.

  • Do not promise thrombectomy from one symptom or one clock value because imaging, anatomy, core, disability, and risk must align.

Red Flags

  • Forced gaze with dense hemiparesis, aphasia, neglect, or rapidly rising NIH Stroke Scale

  • Reduced consciousness, quadriparesis, abnormal eye movements, or inability to protect the airway

  • Unequal pupils, posturing, severe headache, repeated vomiting, or seizure

  • New aspiration, severe hypoxemia, unstable blood pressure, dysrhythmia, or treatment-site bleeding

Patient Education

  • Request emergency transport for severe stroke signs because hospitals differ in their ability to perform mechanical thrombectomy quickly.

  • Record the last time the person was definitely normal because advanced imaging may still identify treatment benefit when onset is uncertain.

Nclex Traps

  • Thrombectomy and IV thrombolysis are complementary, not automatically competing, pathways.

  • LVO can exist with low NIHSS, especially early or in posterior circulation.

  • A normal early NCCT does not show that a large artery is open; CTA answers the vessel question.

Name

Large-vessel occlusion stroke

Category

Neurovascular emergency

Definition

Large-vessel occlusion stroke is acute blockage of a major intracranial artery, commonly the intracranial internal carotid, proximal middle cerebral, basilar, or another endovascularly accessible vessel. LVO often produces a severe cortical or brainstem syndrome because a large downstream territory loses flow, but good collaterals can temporarily keep the NIHSS modest. Rapid recognition matters because mechanical thrombectomy can physically remove the clot in appropriately selected patients and may be combined with IV thrombolysis when both are indicated.

Etiology

Most LVOs arise from cardioembolism, artery-to-artery embolism from carotid or vertebral plaque, intracranial atherosclerotic thrombosis, or dissection. Clot composition, length, location, tandem lesions, collateral anatomy, and time influence whether IV lysis opens the artery and whether EVT is technically and clinically beneficial.

Pathology

A proximal occlusion abruptly reduces flow to cortex, deep perforators, or brainstem structures. The initial core forms where collateral delivery is poorest, while surrounding territory remains penumbral if leptomeningeal or communicating arteries provide partial flow. Without recanalization, the core expands and swelling, hemorrhagic transformation, and permanent network loss become more likely.

Pathophysiology

Cortical LVO clues include gaze deviation, aphasia, neglect, dense face-arm weakness, field loss, and profound asymmetry. Basilar LVO can cause diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, coma, or locked-in syndrome. NIHSS favors anterior motor-language deficits and may undercount posterior, visual, gait, or nondominant deficits, so LVO screening cannot depend on a numeric threshold alone. The 2026 guideline expands EVT benefit to some patients with larger cores and strongly recommends EVT for selected basilar occlusion within 24 hours with NIHSS at least 10, but imaging, anatomy, baseline status, and expert protocol remain decisive.
