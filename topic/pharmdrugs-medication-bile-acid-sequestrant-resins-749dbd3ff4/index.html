Used To Treat

LDL-cholesterol reduction; selected products also improve type 2 diabetes control, and the class is commonly used off-label to bind excess colonic bile acids in bile-acid diarrhea.

Description

Bile-acid sequestrants are large, insoluble, nonabsorbed polymers that exchange anions and trap negatively charged bile acids in the intestine. Fecal bile-acid loss forces the liver to convert more cholesterol into bile acids and upregulate LDL receptors, lowering circulating LDL. The same physical binding explains both their usefulness in bile-acid diarrhea and their major limitation: they can bind nutrients and medications indiscriminately.

Mechanism

Interrupting enterohepatic bile-acid recycling depletes the hepatic bile-acid pool. Cholesterol 7-alpha-hydroxylase activity rises, hepatic cholesterol demand increases, and LDL-receptor-mediated clearance rises. Hepatic cholesterol synthesis also compensates, so combination with a statin can be rational. Triglycerides may rise because altered hepatic lipid handling is not selective for LDL pathways.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Screen triglycerides, prior hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, severe constipation, dysphagia and motility disease. These polymers can cause fecal impaction or obstruction, raise triglycerides, reduce fat-soluble vitamin absorption and lower exposure to narrow-therapeutic-index drugs. Product-specific spacing instructions are a core safety requirement.

Adverse Effects

  • Constipation, bloating, nausea, abdominal pain, fecal impaction, dysphagia or bowel/esophageal obstruction

  • Increased triglycerides and possible pancreatitis

  • Deficiency or reduced absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K and numerous oral drugs

Contraindications

  • Product-specific obstruction history or complete biliary obstruction

  • Colesevelam: triglycerides above 500 mg/dL or prior hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis

  • Clarify severe constipation, gastroparesis, major GI surgery, dysphagia and triglycerides above 300 mg/dL

Nursing Essentials

  • Obtain a complete medication and supplement schedule before starting; a binder taken at the wrong time can quietly make another essential drug ineffective.

  • Monitor stool pattern, swallowing, abdominal distention, triglycerides, LDL response and fat-soluble vitamin risk.

Interactions

  • Many drugs should be taken at least 1 hour before or 4 hours after colestipol; colesevelam commonly requires interacting drugs at least 4 hours before.

  • Thyroid hormone, warfarin, phenytoin, cyclosporine, oral contraceptives and selected diabetes/cardiovascular drugs require product-specific spacing and monitoring.

  • Colesevelam can increase metformin extended-release exposure.

Key Labs

  • Fasting lipid panel including triglycerides before and during therapy

  • INR when warfarin is used, TSH/free T4 with thyroid replacement, glucose/A1c with diabetes therapy

  • Vitamin A/D/E/K or coagulation assessment when deficiency risk exists

Nclex Traps

  • A nonabsorbed drug can still cause major interactions by binding another drug in the gut.

  • This class lowers LDL but can raise triglycerides.

  • A patient with severe constipation or obstruction risk may be harmed by a resin even though systemic toxicity is low.
