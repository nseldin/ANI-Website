Definition

Fox sign is an observed skin sign. It is a clue to retroperitoneal hemorrhage. A positive finding means ecchymosis appears over the proximal thigh.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Fox sign means ecchymosis appears over the proximal thigh.

Why It Matters

The observed Fox sign finding can signal retroperitoneal hemorrhage. Retroperitoneal blood tracks along fascial planes beneath the inguinal ligament. Fox sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • retroperitoneal hemorrhage

Assessment Method

Inspect the upper thigh below the inguinal ligament.

Positive Finding

Ecchymosis appears over the proximal thigh.

Mechanism

Retroperitoneal blood tracks along fascial planes beneath the inguinal ligament.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Fox sign finding raises concern for retroperitoneal hemorrhage because retroperitoneal blood tracks along fascial planes beneath the inguinal ligament. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Fox sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Fox sign to focus evaluation of retroperitoneal hemorrhage and the reviewed alternatives: femoral access hematoma, trauma, anticoagulant bruising. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect the upper thigh below the inguinal ligament. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Fox sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

It appears late and bruising has many causes. Therefore, Fox sign cannot confirm or exclude retroperitoneal hemorrhage by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • femoral access hematoma

  • trauma

  • anticoagulant bruising

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as femoral access hematoma, trauma, anticoagulant bruising.

False Negative Situations

Fox sign may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of retroperitoneal hemorrhage.

Modern Status

Historical late sign; urgent imaging is required. Fox sign remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude retroperitoneal hemorrhage. Apply the reviewed limitation for Fox sign: It appears late and bruising has many causes.

Safety Precautions

Observe Fox sign with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect the upper thigh below the inguinal ligament. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Treat unexplained spreading ecchymosis with instability as hemorrhage until evaluated.

Nursing Relevance

Record the exact Fox sign observation, side or distribution, timing, technique, patient response, and change from baseline; the reviewed positive finding is: Ecchymosis appears over the proximal thigh. Continue the focused evaluation by following the reviewed method and its limits: Inspect the upper thigh below the inguinal ligament. Communicate the finding with its suspected association, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, when it is new, worsening, or clinically important.

Documentation Guidance

Document the exact finding, assessment method, side or distribution, timing, patient response, associated symptoms and vital signs, and change from baseline. Record the observed result instead of writing only "positive" or "negative."

Urgent Escalation

For Fox sign, apply this exact authored caution when deciding whom to notify and how quickly: Treat unexplained spreading ecchymosis with instability as hemorrhage until evaluated. Base escalation for Fox sign on the patient's current condition, associated findings, and the cause under evaluation; this card does not assign a universal urgency level.
