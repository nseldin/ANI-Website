Name

QTc

Range

QTc is the QT interval corrected for heart rate and is reported in milliseconds. Reference conventions depend on sex, age, heart rate, correction formula, measurement method, and QRS duration. An AHA/ACCF/HRS ECG standard suggested practical adult prolonged limits of 460 ms or longer in women and longer than 450 ms in men; a separate AHA/ACCF hospital statement described about 480 ms in women and 470 ms in men as 99th-percentile limits and greater than 500 ms as highly abnormal. These are reference and risk markers, not universal treatment thresholds. Verify an automated value, the formula used, heart rate and rhythm, T-wave endpoint, and any wide-QRS or paced context. If the QRS widens or the rhythm is ventricular-paced, part of the longer QT reflects prolonged depolarization rather than repolarization; clinician-directed tracking of the QRS change or JT interval may be needed, using the same method over time.

Why

A prolonged QTc measurement signals delayed ventricular repolarization and is associated with increased susceptibility to torsade de pointes, which can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation and sudden cardiac death; risk rises as QTc lengthens, but no value predicts an individual event with certainty. Important associations include QT-prolonging antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, macrolides or fluoroquinolones, methadone or ondansetron; hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia; bradycardia or pauses; congenital long-QT syndrome; heart disease; and impaired renal or hepatic drug clearance. Assess palpitations, presyncope or syncope, seizure-like events, family sudden death, rhythm and bradycardia, medication and interaction history, and potassium, magnesium, calcium, renal, and hepatic status. Cause-directed evaluation includes clinician validation and repeat 12-lead ECG, comparison with baseline and correction method, laboratory testing, and telemetry when indicated. Escalate immediately for syncope, seizure, ventricular ectopy or torsade, sustained ventricular tachycardia, hemodynamic instability, or rapid deterioration. One QTc value does not diagnose congenital long-QT syndrome or determine medication management by itself.

High Causes

  • QT-prolonging medicines or interacting combinations, including selected antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, antibiotics, antiemetics, and opioids

  • Hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and sometimes hypocalcemia

  • Bradycardia, pauses, or conversion from a rapid rhythm

  • Congenital long-QT syndrome

  • Heart disease, acute illness, and impaired kidney or liver clearance that increases exposure to a QT-prolonging medicine

  • Formula or measurement artifact, especially Bazett overcorrection at faster heart rates or an uncertain T-wave endpoint

Low Causes

  • A short corrected QT is a different abnormal pattern; confirm the measurement and review electrolytes, medicines, and congenital short-QT context rather than assuming that shorter is always safer.

Critical Concerns

  • A QTc above 500 ms is a major torsade risk marker in AHA hospital guidance, especially when it is new, rising from baseline, or combined with bradycardia, ventricular ectopy, low potassium or magnesium, structural heart disease, or more than one QT-prolonging drug.

  • A computer-generated QTc can be wrong. Visually verify the QT endpoint and correction method through the treating clinician, especially with tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, prominent U waves, wide QRS, bundle-branch block, or ventricular pacing.

  • Repeated QTc prolongation plus arrhythmic syncope, family sudden death, or a compatible pathogenic variant warrants long-QT syndrome evaluation; do not label congenital disease from one automated ECG.

Nursing Considerations

  • Review every prescription, over-the-counter product, interaction, recent dose change, renal or hepatic decline, and electrolyte loss before administering a QT-prolonging medicine when monitoring is required.

  • Check the rhythm, heart rate, QRS width, QTc formula and baseline, then trend 12-lead ECG and potassium, magnesium, calcium, and organ function according to the medication label, order, and protocol.

  • For a concerning change, notify the prescriber promptly and follow ordered medication-hold, electrolyte-repletion, telemetry, or emergency plans rather than independently changing therapy from the number alone.

Nclex Traps

  • QTc corrects QT for heart rate; the correction formula matters, and Bazett can overestimate QTc at faster rates.

  • Long QTc is a torsade risk marker, not a guarantee that torsade will occur and not a stand-alone diagnosis of congenital long-QT syndrome.

  • Syncope or seizure-like activity in a patient taking QT-prolonging drugs should trigger rhythm and QTc assessment rather than assuming a neurologic cause.
