Used To Treat

Emergency calcium replacement and selected resuscitation states, including OFF-LABEL adjunctive treatment of calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, severe magnesium toxicity, and membrane stabilization in life-threatening hyperkalemia. It provides more elemental calcium per milliliter than calcium gluconate but carries greater extravasation risk.

Mechanism

Ionized calcium participates in the cardiac action potential, excitation-contraction coupling, vascular smooth-muscle contraction, and neurotransmitter release. Increasing the extracellular calcium gradient raises the probability of calcium influx through unblocked L-type channels, which can temporarily improve cardiac output and vascular tone during calcium-channel blockade. Calcium also restores the difference between resting and threshold potentials in hyperkalemia, making myocytes less likely to depolarize unpredictably even though serum potassium is unchanged. At the neuromuscular junction, calcium opposes magnesium's inhibition of acetylcholine release. These effects can begin quickly but may be transient, explaining why calcium is an adjunct to high-dose insulin, vasopressors, ventilation, and other toxin-specific care rather than definitive detoxification.

Contraindications

  • Do not administer through a known infiltrated line or mix with bicarbonate- or phosphate-containing solutions because insoluble calcium salts can precipitate. Follow line-separation and flushing guidance.

  • Use particular caution with digoxin toxicity because rapid calcium shifts and dysrhythmia are concerns; seek toxicology guidance rather than withholding all calcium reflexively. Neonates have strict ceftriaxone-calcium restrictions under product labeling.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify calcium chloride versus calcium gluconate and compare elemental calcium, not ampule volume, because a volume-for-volume substitution can create a threefold dosing error.

  • Use continuous ECG and blood-pressure monitoring, check IV patency before and during administration, and never ignore burning or swelling because extravasated calcium chloride can cause necrosis and calcinosis.

  • In hyperkalemia, continue insulin/glucose, beta agonist, bicarbonate when indicated, potassium removal, and serial potassium because calcium buys membrane stability but does not remove potassium.

  • In calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, monitor glucose and potassium during high-dose insulin, and activate advanced shock or VA-ECMO consultation when perfusion fails because calcium benefit can be incomplete and short-lived.

Interactions

  • Calcium can precipitate with bicarbonate, phosphate, and ceftriaxone under specified conditions. Digoxin and calcium both affect myocardial calcium handling, so severe poisoning needs expert sequence and monitoring.

Key Labs

  • Serial ionized calcium is preferred for immediate biologic effect; also trend total calcium with albumin, magnesium, potassium, phosphorus, creatinine, glucose, blood gas, lactate, and toxin-specific concentrations where available.

  • Continuous ECG for QRS/QT and dysrhythmia, invasive hemodynamics when indicated, urine output, perfusion examination, and repeated IV-site and distal neurovascular checks.

Nclex Traps

  • Calcium chloride has about three times more elemental calcium per milliliter than 10 percent calcium gluconate and is more damaging if it extravasates.

  • Calcium stabilizes the myocardium in hyperkalemia but does not lower serum potassium.

  • In calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, calcium is an adjunct; high-dose insulin and vasopressors often remain necessary.

Follow line-separation and flushing guidance. Persistent hypotension, bradycardia, conduction block, rising lactate, oliguria, or shock after calcium requires high-dose insulin, vasopressors, airway support, and early ECLS/VA-ECMO evaluation according to the toxin and AHA guidance.

Description

Calcium chloride is a concentrated IV calcium salt that rapidly raises ionized calcium to support contraction and stabilize myocardial excitability during selected emergencies. A typical 10 percent preparation supplies about 27 mg of elemental calcium per milliliter, roughly three times the elemental calcium in the same volume of 10 percent calcium gluconate. In calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, raising extracellular ionized calcium can increase entry through channels that remain functional and improve contractility and vascular tone, but it does not displace all blocker or correct the toxin's metabolic effects. In hyperkalemia it stabilizes the cardiac membrane without lowering potassium, and in magnesium toxicity it functionally opposes magnesium at the heart and neuromuscular junction. Because chloride salt dissociates readily and is caustic to tissue, secure IV delivery and frequent reassessment are central.

Administration Timing

  • Confirm the indication, formulation, concentration, line, ECG, and baseline ionized calcium when time allows. In unstable calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, give protocol-directed calcium while high-dose insulin and vasopressor plans proceed; AHA 2025 considers calcium reasonable, not sufficient monotherapy.

  • Prefer a secure central line for repeated concentrated calcium chloride when feasible. If peripheral administration is necessary during an emergency, use a large reliable vein, watch the site continuously, and stop immediately for pain, swelling, blanching, or resistance.

  • Reassess ECG, blood pressure, perfusion, and ionized calcium after each protocol dose or infusion change. Do not repeat blindly because hypercalcemia can cause vasoconstriction, dysrhythmia, and organ injury.

Adverse Effects

  • Extravasation can cause severe tissue necrosis, slough, and calcinosis. Rapid injection can cause bradycardia, hypotension, syncope, vasodilation, dysrhythmia, or cardiac arrest.

  • Hypercalcemia can cause nausea, confusion, weakness, polyuria, vasoconstriction, acute kidney injury, and dysrhythmia; chloride load can contribute to hyperchloremic acidosis with large exposure.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent hypotension, bradycardia, conduction block, rising lactate, oliguria, or shock after calcium requires high-dose insulin, vasopressors, airway support, and early ECLS/VA-ECMO evaluation according to the toxin and AHA guidance.

  • Any suspected extravasation requires immediate stop, leave access in place for aspiration per protocol, notify pharmacy/provider, elevate and assess the limb, and activate the institution's calcium-extravasation pathway; injury can evolve after the infusion stops.

Evidence Limitations

  • Calcium for calcium-channel-blocker or beta-blocker poisoning is OFF-LABEL and supported mainly by physiologic rationale, observational data, and consensus. AHA 2025 rates calcium reasonable for life-threatening CCB poisoning but only possibly reasonable for beta-blocker poisoning.

  • The exact target ionized calcium and repeat schedule vary by protocol. Greater concentration does not mean calcium chloride is always preferable to gluconate.

Regulatory Status

FDA-labeled calcium chloride products have specified acute calcium indications. Use for calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, beta-blocker poisoning, magnesium toxicity, or hyperkalemic membrane stabilization may be OFF-LABEL and protocol directed.
