Definition

Dysuria means pain, burning, stinging, itching, or other marked discomfort during or immediately around urination. It is a symptom, not a diagnosis of urinary tract infection.

Plain Language

Dysuria means urinating hurts or burns.

Why It Matters

Dysuria can result from a bladder infection, but urethritis or another sexually transmitted infection, vaginitis, a stone, trauma, obstruction, medication or chemical irritation, and cancer can cause the same symptom. Fever, flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy, retention, confusion, low blood pressure, severe pelvic or testicular pain, or visible blood changes the urgency.

Associated Conditions

  • irritation or inflammation of the urethra, bladder, vulva, vagina, or prostate from urinary infection, urethritis or another sexually transmitted infection, vaginitis, stones, trauma, instrumentation, chemical irritation, medication, stricture, or malignancy

Assessment Method

Ask where the discomfort is felt, whether it occurs at the start, throughout, or after voiding, and about frequency, urgency, hematuria, discharge, genital lesions, pelvic or flank pain, fever, pregnancy possibility, sexual exposure, procedures, products, medications, retention, and recurrent symptoms; obtain a correctly collected urinalysis and culture or exposure-site testing when indicated.

Positive Finding

The patient reports pain, burning, stinging, itching, or marked discomfort during or immediately around urination.

Mechanism

Urine passing over inflamed or injured urethral or external tissue and bladder or urethral contractions stimulate sensitized pain receptors; infection is common but stones, trauma, inflammatory disease, chemical exposure, and tumors can produce the same symptom.

Clinical Interpretation

The provoked Dysuria symptom raises concern for irritation or inflammation of the urethra, bladder, vulva, vagina, or prostate from urinary infection, urethritis or another sexually transmitted infection, vaginitis, stones, trauma, instrumentation, chemical irritation, medication, stricture, or malignancy because urine passing over inflamed or injured urethral or external tissue and bladder or urethral contractions stimulate sensitized pain receptors; infection is common but stones, trauma, inflammatory disease, chemical exposure, and tumors can produce the same symptom. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Dysuria does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Dysuria to focus evaluation of irritation or inflammation of the urethra, bladder, vulva, vagina, or prostate from urinary infection, urethritis or another sexually transmitted infection, vaginitis, stones, trauma, instrumentation, chemical irritation, medication, stricture, or malignancy and the reviewed alternatives: vaginitis, urethritis, bladder pain syndrome, genital ulcer disease. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Ask where the discomfort is felt, whether it occurs at the start, throughout, or after voiding, and about frequency, urgency, hematuria, discharge, genital lesions, pelvic or flank pain, fever, pregnancy possibility, sexual exposure, procedures, products, medications, retention, and recurrent symptoms; obtain a correctly collected urinalysis and culture or exposure-site testing when indicated. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Dysuria as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Symptom wording and location vary, external vulvar or vaginal pain can be mistaken for internal urinary burning, and older or immunocompromised patients may have serious infection without dysuria; urinalysis and cultures also require clinical context. Therefore, Dysuria cannot confirm or exclude irritation or inflammation of the urethra, bladder, vulva, vagina, or prostate from urinary infection, urethritis or another sexually transmitted infection, vaginitis, stones, trauma, instrumentation, chemical irritation, medication, stricture, or malignancy by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • vaginitis

False Positive Situations

A false-positive response can reflect another painful or sensory process such as vaginitis, urethritis, bladder pain syndrome, genital ulcer disease, or a trigger that was not applied as defined.

False Negative Situations

Dysuria may not reproduce when the trigger is incomplete, symptoms are intermittent, treatment has reduced the response, or communication is limited.

Modern Status

Current common symptom requiring cause-directed assessment; it does not by itself diagnose a urinary tract infection or identify the anatomic source. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Dysuria, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Ask where the discomfort is felt, whether it occurs at the start, throughout, or after voiding, and about frequency, urgency, hematuria, discharge, genital lesions, pelvic or flank pain, fever, pregnancy possibility, sexual exposure, procedures, products, medications, retention, and recurrent symptoms; obtain a correctly collected urinalysis and culture or exposure-site testing when indicated. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not assume every dysuria episode is a routine bladder infection or use leftover antibiotics; fever, rigors, flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy, urinary retention, hypotension, confusion, visible blood or clots, severe pelvic or testicular pain, rapidly worsening symptoms, or recent instrumentation requires prompt or emergency evaluation for upper infection, obstruction, sepsis, torsion, or injury.
