Plain Language

Alcohol use disorder is a treatable medical condition in which drinking becomes hard to control and continues even though it is causing harm. It can be mild, moderate, or severe; a person does not need to drink every day or have withdrawal to have AUD.

Why It Matters

AUD can lead to injuries, poisoning, suicide risk, liver and pancreatic disease, heart and nerve damage, poor nutrition, pregnancy harm, and dangerous withdrawal. Identifying it changes immediate safety decisions and opens effective treatment with counseling, recovery support, and medication.

Definition

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is the DSM-5-TR diagnosis for a problematic pattern of alcohol use that causes clinically significant impairment or distress. At least 2 of 11 criteria must occur within the same 12-month period; 2 to 3 indicate mild, 4 to 5 moderate, and 6 or more severe AUD. Criteria cover impaired control, social impairment, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, tolerance, and withdrawal. Intoxication, one episode of heavy drinking, tolerance, or withdrawal alone does not automatically establish AUD. ICD-11 uses separate alcohol-related categories such as harmful pattern of use and alcohol dependence, so its labels and requirements should not be treated as exact synonyms for the DSM diagnosis.

Pathology

AUD is not a moral failing and has no single diagnostic lesion or laboratory marker. Repeated alcohol exposure can strengthen cue-driven reward learning, weaken control over use, and recruit stress systems that make craving and negative feelings during abstinence more powerful. The clinical disorder is defined by the pattern of impaired control and harm, not by a particular drink count, appearance, occupation, or laboratory value. Genetic vulnerability, development, trauma, psychiatric illness, social conditions, and alcohol exposure interact differently in each patient.

Pathophysiology

  • Alcohol enhances inhibitory signaling and suppresses excitatory signaling during use. With repeated heavy exposure, the nervous system adapts; sudden reduction can then produce hyperexcitability, autonomic activation, seizures, hallucinations, or delirium.

  • Repeated intoxication and withdrawal can alter reward, stress, memory, and executive-control networks, making alcohol-related cues unusually important and increasing use for relief even after pleasure decreases.

  • Alcohol and its metabolites can directly injure liver, pancreas, heart, brain, peripheral nerves, gastrointestinal tissue, and bone marrow; poor intake and impaired vitamin absorption add malnutrition and thiamine-deficiency risk.

  • Tolerance and physical dependence can occur, but neither is required for AUD and neither describes the full pattern of functional impairment and continued use despite harm.

Etiology

AUD is multifactorial. Inherited vulnerability, earlier onset of drinking, repeated heavy or high-intensity drinking, trauma and chronic stress, depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, impulsivity, family and peer context, easy alcohol access, unstable housing, pain, sleep problems, and other substance use may contribute. These are risk factors rather than proof that one person will develop AUD, and protective relationships, treatment access, and recovery supports can change the course.

Risk Factors

  • Prior AUD, recurrent heavy or high-intensity drinking, early onset of drinking, or repeated intoxication and withdrawal

  • Family history or genetic vulnerability, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress, attention or impulse-control problems, or prior self-harm

  • Childhood adversity, trauma, chronic stress, grief, isolation, unsafe housing, military or occupational exposure, and alcohol-normalizing social environments

  • Chronic pain, insomnia, prescribed sedatives or opioids, tobacco or other substance use, and limited access to nonstigmatizing care

  • Pregnancy, adolescence, older age, liver disease, pancreatitis, seizure history, poor nutrition, or previous severe withdrawal increase the consequences of ongoing use or abrupt cessation

Signs Symptoms

  • Drinking more or longer than intended, repeated unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, or spending substantial time obtaining, using, or recovering from alcohol

  • Missed responsibilities, relationship conflict, reduced activities, hazardous use, or continued drinking despite physical or psychological harm

  • Tolerance or withdrawal; these may be absent, and their absence does not exclude AUD

  • Blackouts, falls, crashes, injuries, sleep disturbance, mood change, sexual or social problems, poor nutrition, tremor, neuropathy, jaundice, abdominal pain, or cognitive change

  • Concealment, shame, repeated emergency visits, medication interactions, or a pattern that conflicts with pregnancy, liver disease, safety-sensitive work, or another medical condition

Diagnostics

  • Screen with a validated instrument such as AUDIT-C or the Single Alcohol Screening Question, then complete a diagnostic interview; a positive screen identifies need for assessment and does not diagnose AUD by itself.

  • Ask about the 11 DSM-5-TR criteria within one 12-month period and record severity, current pattern, last drink, typical and largest amount, speed of drinking, prior treatment, recovery periods, and patient goals.

  • Assess current intoxication and withdrawal separately from the longer-term AUD diagnosis. Identify prior withdrawal seizure or delirium, repeated withdrawals, sedative use, major medical illness, pregnancy, and ability to be safely monitored.

  • Evaluate suicide and self-harm risk, accidental injury, violence exposure, impaired driving, neglect, safeguarding, co-occurring psychiatric disorders, other substances, medications, pain, sleep, housing, nutrition, and support.

  • Use the clinical timeline and collateral information when appropriate to distinguish an independent psychiatric disorder from alcohol-induced symptoms; reassess after intoxication or withdrawal clears without withholding emergency care.

Labs

  • No blood, breath, urine, liver-enzyme, or imaging result diagnoses or grades AUD. A measured alcohol concentration indicates recent exposure but does not reliably predict impairment in a tolerant person or explain every altered mental state.

  • Choose testing from the presentation: bedside glucose, electrolytes including magnesium and phosphorus, renal and liver measures, blood count, coagulation studies, lipase, pregnancy testing, ECG, toxicology, infection testing, trauma imaging, or alcohol concentration may be indicated.

  • AST, ALT, gamma-glutamyl transferase, mean corpuscular volume, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin, and phosphatidylethanol may support an exposure or organ-injury assessment in selected settings, but none is a standalone AUD test and detection windows and performance differ.

  • Before medication, review opioid exposure and anticipated analgesia for naltrexone, renal function for acamprosate, and liver, cardiac, psychiatric, medication, and alcohol-exposure safety for disulfiram; follow the current label and local protocol.

Assessment

First stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, trauma, poisoning, altered consciousness, seizure, severe agitation, withdrawal, and suicide risk. Then use respectful, specific questions about amount, timing, pattern, consequences, prior withdrawal, other sedatives or opioids, pregnancy, nutrition, medical and psychiatric symptoms, medications, housing, supports, and treatment goals. Do not assume that odor of alcohol explains confusion, that a high tolerance means safety, or that a low current alcohol level excludes impending withdrawal.

Differential

  • Alcohol intoxication, alcohol poisoning, alcohol withdrawal, alcohol-induced mood or psychotic disorder, delirium, Wernicke encephalopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, and another substance or medication effect

  • Head injury, stroke, seizure or postictal state, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, sepsis, electrolyte disturbance, renal or hepatic failure, thyroid disease, dementia, and other causes of altered behavior or cognition

  • Major depressive, bipolar, anxiety, trauma-related, psychotic, sleep, pain, and personality disorders that may be independent, alcohol-induced, or co-occurring

  • Hazardous or binge drinking without AUD, culturally patterned use without diagnostic impairment, and prescribed exposure that does not meet the disorder's behavioral criteria

Treatments

  • Treat intoxication, trauma, poisoning, hypoglycemia, thiamine deficiency, and withdrawal as separate acute problems. Benzodiazepines such as diazepam or lorazepam are protocol-selected first-line medicines for clinically significant alcohol withdrawal, not routine long-term AUD treatment; monitor sedation, breathing, falls, and response.

  • Use shared decision making to select abstinence, reduction, and safety goals. Evidence-based behavioral care includes motivational enhancement, cognitive-behavioral approaches, community reinforcement, couples or family work, relapse-prevention skills, and mutual-help or peer support chosen to fit the patient.

  • Offer an AUD medication when appropriate rather than reserving medication for treatment failure. Naltrexone can reduce heavy drinking and craving but requires opioid-free assessment and liver review; acamprosate supports abstinence and requires renal dosing review and is contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency; disulfiram is a selected abstinence-support option only with informed consent, alcohol avoidance, close supervision, and cardiac, liver, psychiatric, and interaction screening.

  • Treat depression, anxiety, trauma, pain, sleep disorders, tobacco use, and other substance disorders concurrently with coordinated care. Withdrawal management alone is not AUD treatment; arrange continuing medication, counseling, primary care, recovery support, and follow-up.

Contraindications

  • Do not advise abrupt unsupervised cessation when physiologic dependence or previous severe withdrawal may be present; withdrawal seizures and delirium can be fatal.

  • Do not give naltrexone during current opioid use or physiologic opioid dependence, when opioid analgesia is expected, or without current label-based liver and opioid-free assessment; precipitated withdrawal and blocked analgesia can cause serious harm.

  • Acamprosate is contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency and requires renal review; it does not treat acute withdrawal.

  • Do not give disulfiram to an intoxicated or uninformed person or when alcohol exposure, severe myocardial disease or coronary occlusion, psychosis, severe hepatic dysfunction, metronidazole, or another current-label contraindication is present.

  • Do not use a withdrawal score when delirium, severe cognitive or communication impairment, or another illness makes symptom reporting unreliable, and do not use a score as the sole level-of-care decision.

Safety Considerations

  • Alcohol withdrawal can begin while alcohol remains measurable. Trend orientation, tremor, sweating, pulse, blood pressure, temperature, hallucinations, agitation, sleep, seizures, medication response, hydration, and breathing rather than relying on one score or level.

  • Alcohol combined with opioids, benzodiazepines, sleep medicines, or other sedatives increases respiratory-depression, aspiration, fall, crash, and overdose risk. Assess all substances and provide overdose education and naloxone when opioid exposure is possible.

  • Give prescribed thiamine promptly when deficiency is possible, especially with malnutrition or neurologic findings; do not delay emergency glucose for hypoglycemia. Escalate confusion, gait ataxia, or abnormal eye movements for possible Wernicke encephalopathy.

  • Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, self-harm, interpersonal violence, impaired driving, firearm or other lethal-means access, unsafe caregiving, and safeguarding needs; intoxication does not make a stated threat irrelevant.

  • After abstinence or reduced use, alcohol tolerance may fall. A return to the previous amount increases poisoning, injury, and interaction risk; plan follow-up and rapid re-entry to care after recurrence.

Nursing Priorities

  • Stabilize immediate medical and behavioral threats, obtain bedside glucose, identify last drink and other substances, and institute seizure, fall, aspiration, suicide, and elopement precautions according to current findings and policy.

  • Use nonstigmatizing language and a private, specific history; document patient report, collateral source, objective findings, withdrawal history, current capacity, treatment goal, and response without labels such as alcoholic or manipulative.

  • Administer protocol-ordered withdrawal medicine, thiamine, fluids, electrolytes, and nutrition while reassessing sedation, airway, respirations, circulation, mental status, gait, intake and output, and laboratory trends.

  • Before AUD medication, reconcile opioids and sedatives, assess pregnancy possibility, renal and hepatic status, interactions, adherence barriers, overdose risk, and the patient's informed preference.

  • Provide a warm handoff to continuing AUD care, medication follow-up, behavioral treatment, recovery supports, primary care, and crisis resources; teach that recurrence is a signal to reassess the plan, not a reason for punishment or discharge from care.

Red Flags

  • Reduced consciousness, slow or irregular breathing, cyanosis, repeated vomiting, aspiration, severe hypoglycemia, serious trauma, chest pain, shock, or suspected mixed overdose

  • Withdrawal seizure, delirium, disorientation, severe agitation, hallucinations with confusion, rapidly worsening autonomic instability, or escalating medication need

  • Confusion with gait ataxia or abnormal eye movements, severe malnutrition, jaundice with confusion, hematemesis or melena, severe abdominal pain, fever, or major electrolyte disturbance

  • Active suicidal intent, recent serious self-harm, violent intent with means, inability to protect a dependent person, unsafe discharge setting, or lack of capacity for an urgent decision

  • Pregnancy with intoxication, withdrawal, trauma, bleeding, severe vomiting, reduced fetal movement, or another obstetric concern

Complications

  • Alcohol poisoning, aspiration, falls, crashes, burns, drowning, violence exposure, suicide, and mixed-substance overdose

  • Withdrawal seizures, alcohol withdrawal delirium, Wernicke encephalopathy, Korsakoff syndrome, neuropathy, cognitive impairment, and sleep disturbance

  • Steatotic liver disease, hepatitis, cirrhosis, pancreatitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, hypertension, stroke, cancer risk, immune dysfunction, anemia, and malnutrition

  • Pregnancy and fetal harm, sexual and reproductive consequences, family disruption, unsafe caregiving, housing and employment loss, legal problems, and treatment interruption

  • Recurrence after treatment, reduced tolerance after abstinence, and worsening depression, anxiety, trauma symptoms, or other substance use

Prognosis

AUD is treatable across the severity spectrum, and many people reduce or resolve harmful drinking over time. Course varies with severity, organ injury, psychiatric and substance comorbidity, housing and social stability, treatment access, and continuing support. Recurrence does not erase prior progress or prove treatment failure; it should prompt reassessment of goals, medication, withdrawal risk, triggers, supports, and level of care. Recovery includes health, function, safety, and quality of life, not only a laboratory result or a single drinking count.

Prevention

  • Screen adults and at-risk adolescents with validated age-appropriate tools and provide brief intervention or diagnostic assessment according to the result; a screen is not a diagnosis.

  • Discuss alcohol-medication interactions, driving and water safety, pregnancy, liver and pancreatic disease, prior withdrawal, and the added danger of combining alcohol with opioids or sedatives.

  • Treat trauma, mood, anxiety, pain, sleep, and social needs early and strengthen protective relationships, safe housing, culturally responsive care, and access to evidence-based treatment.

  • Create a written recurrence and crisis plan with warning signs, coping steps, support contacts, medication follow-up, emergency thresholds, and a route back to care without shame.

Patient Education

  • AUD means alcohol has become difficult to control and is causing harm; it is a medical diagnosis, not a character judgment, and effective treatment can include both counseling and medication.

  • If you have had withdrawal, a seizure, hallucinations, or delirium, do not try to stop suddenly alone. Seek medical guidance; call emergency services for seizure, severe confusion, trouble breathing, repeated vomiting, serious injury, or inability to stay safe.

  • Naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram work differently and are not interchangeable. Tell the prescriber about opioids, liver or kidney disease, pregnancy, heart disease, psychosis, all medicines, and your treatment goal.

  • Withdrawal care is only the first step. Arrange continuing treatment and return early if drinking recurs; a recurrence is information that the plan needs adjustment, not proof that recovery is impossible.

  • Never drive, swim, operate machinery, or care for a dependent person while impaired, and avoid combining alcohol with opioids, benzodiazepines, sleep medicines, or other sedatives.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and attempts to conceive require prompt, nonpunitive obstetric and addiction care; no amount of alcohol is established as safe in pregnancy, and medication and withdrawal plans must be individualized.

  • Adolescents need developmentally appropriate confidential screening within legal limits, family and school context, suicide and safeguarding assessment, and specialty input for medication decisions.

  • Older adults may have greater impairment at lower intake because of body composition, frailty, medication interactions, cognitive change, falls, and liver or kidney disease; do not mistake withdrawal or intoxication for dementia or ordinary aging.

  • People with liver disease, pancreatitis, seizure disorder, malnutrition, cognitive disability, chronic pain, homelessness, incarceration, or co-occurring psychiatric or other substance disorders need coordinated level-of-care and medication planning.

  • Culture, language, military experience, gender, sexual identity, disability, recovery goals, and prior stigma affect disclosure and engagement; adapt communication and supports without changing emergency safety standards.

Nclex Traps

  • A person can have AUD without daily drinking, tolerance, or withdrawal; a positive screen or abnormal liver test does not diagnose AUD by itself.

  • Do not attribute altered mental status to alcohol odor or a measured alcohol level until glucose, trauma, hypoxia, infection, poisoning, withdrawal, and other causes are assessed.

  • Alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening and can begin before the alcohol concentration reaches zero; detoxification alone does not treat AUD.

  • Give emergency glucose for hypoglycemia without waiting for thiamine, while also giving prescribed thiamine promptly when deficiency is possible.

  • Naltrexone requires opioid assessment, acamprosate requires renal assessment, and disulfiram is a selected supervised abstinence-support medicine rather than a punishment for drinking.
