Range

A common venous fasting-plasma reference example after at least 8 hours without food is 70-99 mg/dL (3.9-5.5 mmol/L), but specimen, method, laboratory, pregnancy, age, and clinical setting matter. This is not an inpatient treatment target. ADA 2026 hospital guidance defines hyperglycemia as above 140 mg/dL, generally starts or intensifies treatment for persistent glucose at or above 180 mg/dL confirmed twice within 24 hours, and uses different individualized goals for critically ill and noncritically ill adults. Use the applicable laboratory range and current ordered protocol rather than one universal glucose target.

Why

An abnormal glucose result signals too little or too much immediately available blood sugar and changes neurologic, medication, nutrition, and fluid-safety decisions. Hypoglycemia raises concern for insulin or sulfonylurea effect, missed nutrition, alcohol use, or kidney, liver, adrenal, or severe systemic illness; hyperglycemia can reflect diabetes, acute stress or infection, glucocorticoids, or a DKA or HHS pattern when ketones, acidosis, hyperosmolality, and dehydration fit. Promptly assess symptoms and mental status, airway and swallowing safety, last intake, diabetes medicines and timing, nutrition or feed interruption, infection clues, hydration and urine output, and recent glucose trend; repeat an unexpected bedside result or obtain laboratory confirmation according to protocol without delaying treatment of symptomatic hypoglycemia. Cause-directed evaluation can include repeat blood glucose, A1C, direct beta-hydroxybutyrate, electrolytes and bicarbonate, venous pH, creatinine, and osmolality as ordered for the pattern. Escalate immediately for seizure, altered consciousness, respiratory distress, shock, or suspected DKA or HHS with rapid deterioration. One glucose value does not diagnose diabetes, DKA, HHS, or the cause of dysglycemia; interpret specimen type, meal and medication timing, symptoms, trend, and the full clinical and laboratory pattern.

High Causes

  • Diabetes or stress hyperglycemia

  • DKA or HHS when the complete crisis pattern fits

  • Acute infection, surgery, trauma, or severe illness

  • Glucocorticoids and selected other medicines

  • Enteral feeding or parenteral nutrition

Low Causes

  • Insulin, sulfonylurea, or meglitinide effect

  • Missed meals, interrupted tube feeding, vomiting, or malnutrition

  • Kidney or liver dysfunction

  • Sepsis or other severe illness

  • Adrenal insufficiency

  • Alcohol use

Critical Concerns

  • Hypoglycemia with neuroglycopenic symptoms, seizure, altered consciousness, or need for another person's assistance requires immediate treatment and escalation according to protocol.

  • Marked hyperglycemia with dehydration, ketones or acidosis, hyperosmolality, altered consciousness, shock, or rapid deterioration raises concern for DKA or HHS and requires urgent evaluation.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess symptoms, mental status, airway and swallowing safety, intake, diabetes medicines and timing, feed interruptions, infection clues, hydration, urine output, and the glucose trend; verify an unexpected bedside value according to protocol without delaying treatment of symptomatic hypoglycemia.

  • When a hyperglycemic crisis is possible, trend direct beta-hydroxybutyrate, potassium and other electrolytes, bicarbonate, venous pH, creatinine, osmolality, fluid balance, and trigger evaluation as ordered.

Nclex Traps

  • A fasting reference interval, outpatient diabetes goal, inpatient treatment threshold, and inpatient target are different concepts.

  • Do not diagnose DKA or HHS from glucose alone, and do not begin or advance an insulin infusion without applying the ordered potassium-safety pathway.
