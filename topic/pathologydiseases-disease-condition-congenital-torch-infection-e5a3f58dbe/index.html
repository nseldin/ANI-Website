Pathology

Congenital TORCH infections are vertically transmitted infections that can injure fetal brain, eyes, liver, blood cells, hearing, and growth.

Etiology

Includes toxoplasmosis, other infections, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex, plus related congenital pathogens.

Signs Symptoms

  • growth restriction

  • microcephaly

  • seizures

  • hepatosplenomegaly

  • jaundice

  • petechiae

  • chorioretinitis

  • hearing loss

Diagnostics

  • maternal history and serology

  • newborn PCR/serology by pathogen

  • CBC/LFTs

  • hearing and eye exams

  • neuroimaging when indicated

Treatments

  • pathogen-specific antivirals/antimicrobials when indicated

  • supportive newborn care

  • hearing/vision/developmental follow-up

  • Medication treatment: therapy depends on the organism. Acyclovir is used for neonatal herpes simplex virus, ganciclovir may be used for severe congenital cytomegalovirus, and penicillin G is used for congenital syphilis.

  • Supportive care, hearing/vision follow-up, growth monitoring, and infection-specific precautions are essential.

Nursing Priorities

  • Review maternal fever or rash, exposures, travel, sexual history, vaccination, prenatal testing, ultrasound findings, and timing because fetal effects differ by organism and gestational stage.

  • Assess the newborn for growth restriction, petechiae or purpura, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, microcephaly or hydrocephalus, eye findings, murmur, seizures, feeding difficulty, and hearing response because congenital infection often affects several organs at once.

  • Collect organism-specific maternal, placental, blood, urine, saliva, cerebrospinal-fluid, or lesion testing at the correct time and with contamination precautions because a generic TORCH panel can misclassify infection.

  • Administer prescribed organism-specific antimicrobial or antiviral therapy and monitor blood counts, kidney and liver function, bilirubin, growth, hearing, vision, and development because both infection and treatment can cause delayed toxicity or impairment.

  • Escalate immediately for seizure, apnea, severe jaundice, hypoglycemia, respiratory distress, shock, rapidly rising bilirubin, profound thrombocytopenia, or declining feeding and consciousness because neonatal intensive support is required.

Complications

  • neurodevelopmental delay

  • hearing loss

  • vision loss

  • seizures

  • liver disease

Patient Education

  • Explain that TORCH is a group of infections, not one diagnosis, so testing and treatment must identify the specific organism.

  • Teach the need for long-term hearing, vision, growth, neurologic, and developmental follow-up because some effects appear months or years after birth.

Nclex Traps

  • Petechiae plus hepatosplenomegaly plus hearing/eye findings should trigger congenital infection thinking.

  • Do not lump TORCH infections together. Match treatment and isolation/follow-up to the organism and newborn findings.

Definition

Congenital TORCH infections are vertically transmitted infections that can injure fetal brain, eyes, liver, blood cells, hearing, and growth.

Red Flags

  • Seizure, apnea, abnormal tone, or declining consciousness

  • Severe jaundice, rapidly rising bilirubin, or liver failure

  • Respiratory distress, shock, hypoglycemia, or inability to feed

  • Profound thrombocytopenia, bleeding, or rapidly spreading purpura

    Limitations

    • TORCH is not one disease or one standardized panel.

    • The O category varies and the grouping omits important congenital infections.

    • Shared findings are nonspecific.

    • Testing and treatment are organism and timing specific.

    Nursing Application

    Link maternal history, prenatal screening, newborn findings, specimen timing, isolation, and urgent treatment pathways. Use exact organism-specific cards for transmission, prevention, testing, and treatment. Support families without implying blame.

    Recall Target

    Status

    historical

    Urgent Exceptions

    • Ill neonates with sepsis, vesicles, seizures, respiratory distress, jaundice, bleeding, or altered consciousness require immediate neonatal and infectious-disease evaluation.

    Why

    The grouping prompts consideration of congenital infection when prenatal exposures and multisystem neonatal findings align.
