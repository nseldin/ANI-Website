Plain Language

Suicidal ideation means thoughts about ending one's life, ranging from a wish not to wake up to active thoughts with a plan, intent, or preparations. It is a safety finding, not a mental-disorder diagnosis, and it can change quickly.

Why It Matters

A current plan, intent, access to the method, or recent attempt may require immediate emergency protection. Passive thoughts still require assessment because no score, reassuring appearance, or protective factor can reliably predict the next action.

Definition

Suicidal ideation is thought content involving death by suicide, ranging from passive wishes through active thoughts, plans, intent, and preparations. It is a clinical safety phenomenon rather than a standalone mental-disorder diagnosis and does not independently predict an attempt. A suicide attempt is potentially injurious self-directed behavior with at least some intent to die; nonsuicidal self-injury, self-harm with unclear intent, intrusive ego-dystonic harm obsessions, psychotic commands, and accidental poisoning require separate assessment.

Pathology

Suicidal ideation has no single disease lesion, laboratory marker, or universal progression. Risk changes as psychological pain, hopelessness, mood illness, psychosis, substance exposure, agitation, sleep loss, medical illness, acute stress, access to lethal means, coping, and social support change. Protective factors guide care but cannot erase plan, intent, preparation, or another acute finding, and a person's apparent calm does not establish safety.

Pathophysiology

  • Acute stress, unbearable psychological or physical pain, hopelessness, impaired problem solving, agitation, insomnia, intoxication, psychosis, or mixed mood activation can narrow perceived options and increase urgency.

  • Access to a highly lethal method can shorten the interval between thought and action; collaborative means safety increases time and distance without claiming to predict behavior.

Etiology

Suicidal ideation is multifactorial and may occur with or without a diagnosed mental disorder. Prior suicidal behavior, depression, bipolar illness, psychosis, substance use, trauma, pain, medical illness, loss, violence, isolation, discrimination, legal or financial crisis, and care transitions may interact; none alone determines an outcome.

Risk Factors

  • A prior suicide attempt, recent preparatory behavior, recurrent self-harm, recent psychiatric discharge, or rapid clinical deterioration

  • Depression, bipolar illness, psychosis, substance use, severe agitation, insomnia, pain, serious medical illness, trauma, abuse, or cognitive impairment

  • Recent loss, violence, legal or financial crisis, isolation, access to lethal means, family suicide history, and interruption of care

Signs Symptoms

  • A passive wish to die, thoughts of suicide, an active plan, intent, method research, rehearsal, or collection of medicines or other means

  • Farewell messages, giving away possessions, arranging affairs, escalating substance use, withdrawal, recklessness, agitation, hopelessness, or feeling trapped

  • A sudden apparent calm after deciding on a plan, concealment, inconsistent disclosure, or a recent unexplained injury or poisoning

Diagnostics

  • Suicidal ideation is identified through direct screening and clinical assessment; it is not a standalone diagnosis and no score independently predicts an attempt.

  • A positive screen requires a trained brief suicide-safety or comprehensive assessment that addresses current thoughts, intent, plan, access, preparations, history, acute drivers, protective factors, and immediate needs.

  • Do not use a global low, medium, or high label or one denial of current intent to determine treatment, observation, or discharge.

Labs

  • There is no laboratory test, scan, or biomarker that confirms suicidal ideation or predicts suicide.

  • Use method-, overdose-, injury-, intoxication-, pregnancy-, delirium-, or medical-illness-specific testing when the history or examination supports it.

  • Normal laboratory results do not establish safety and cannot replace assessment of intent, plan, access, preparations, and dynamic clinical change.

Assessment

Ask privately, calmly, and directly about current and recent frequency, duration, controllability, passive versus active thoughts, intent, plan, access, preparations, rehearsal, expected lethality, prior attempts and self-harm, intoxication or withdrawal, psychosis, mania, agitation, sleep, pain, recent events, abuse, supports, reasons for living, dependents, and ability to use safety steps. Seek collateral and review medicine or means access when lawful and relevant, while explaining the limits of confidentiality.

Differential

  • Passive death wish, active suicidal ideation, preparatory behavior, suicide attempt, or recent aborted or interrupted attempt

  • Self-harm or nonsuicidal self-injury without intent to die, recognizing that intent can be mixed, uncertain, or change within an episode

  • Intrusive harm obsession, command hallucination, delusional belief, delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, cognitive impairment, or accidental poisoning

Treatments

  • Stabilize medical injury or poisoning first or concurrently. For imminent danger, do not leave the person alone; use the least restrictive safe observation and environment and obtain emergency mental-health evaluation.

  • Complete a collaborative safety plan, lethal-means counseling, warm handoff, timely follow-up, and treatment of the underlying psychiatric, substance, pain, sleep, trauma-related, or medical condition.

  • No generic medicine treats suicidal ideation as a standalone diagnosis. FDA labeling does not establish esketamine as preventing suicide or reducing suicidal ideation or behavior, and it does not replace hospitalization when warranted.

Contraindications

  • Do not use a no-suicide contract, promise secrecy, rely on a low or reassuring score, or discharge based only on denial of current intent.

  • Do not substitute medication, a hotline number, a protective factor, or family presence for indicated emergency evaluation and a safe disposition.

  • Do not use coercive or punitive communication when collaborative, trauma-informed, least-restrictive care can maintain safety.

Safety Considerations

  • Reassess after clinical change, intoxication resolution, medicine initiation or dose change, new stress, transfer, leave, or discharge because risk is dynamic.

  • Document the person's own words, plan and means findings, preparations, prior behavior, acute drivers, protective factors, consultation, observation, means-safety actions, and follow-up.

  • Explain privacy and its limits, include the person in decisions whenever possible, and use jurisdiction, facility, age, and setting-specific emergency procedures.

Nursing Priorities

  • Maintain immediate safety, communicate calmly and directly, remove accessible hazards according to policy, and treat injury, poisoning, intoxication, withdrawal, or delirium.

  • Initiate the approved emergency pathway for plan, intent, preparations, accessible means, inability to maintain safety, or another acute red flag.

  • Complete or support a collaborative safety plan, lethal-means discussion, warm handoff, and repeat assessment at transitions; document objective findings and actions.

Red Flags

  • Current plan plus intent, accessible means, preparations, rehearsal, recent attempt, or rapid escalation

  • Command hallucinations, severe mixed or manic agitation, intoxication, withdrawal, delirium, or inability or unwillingness to maintain safety

  • Unsafe home, abuse, coercion, concealed injury or overdose, unavailable support, or loss of contact during an acute transition

Complications

  • Suicide attempt or death, poisoning, trauma, anoxic injury, disability, and harm to dependents or responders

  • Recurrent crisis, interrupted medical or psychiatric care, substance-related harm, and worsening underlying illness

  • Disengagement after emergency, inpatient, military, correctional, or other high-risk care transitions

Prognosis

Suicidal ideation is dynamic and treatable, but no assessment can guarantee an outcome. Prognosis changes with the underlying illness, prior behavior, acute drivers, access to means, substance use, support, follow-up, and engagement. Reassessment and treatment focus on current needs and modifiable risks rather than deterministic labels.

Prevention

  • Use collaborative lethal-means safety, a practical written safety plan, evidence-based treatment of underlying illness, and timely follow-up after transitions.

  • Address sleep, substance use, pain, violence, abuse, isolation, interrupted medicines, and barriers to care that can intensify risk.

  • Teach warning signs and crisis contacts to the patient and chosen supports and confirm a warm rather than passive referral whenever possible.

Patient Education

  • Asking about suicide does not implant the idea; disclose worsening thoughts, planning, preparations, or access to means as early as possible.

  • Use the written safety plan: recognize warning signs, try internal coping steps, contact supportive people and professionals, and make the environment safer.

  • In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support; call 911 or go to an emergency department for immediate danger. Use appropriate local services outside the United States.

Special Populations

  • The NIMH ASQ is validated for medical patients age 8 and older; a positive screen requires follow-up assessment, while suspected risk under age 8 requires full evaluation rather than an unvalidated screen.

  • Children and adolescents need caregiver and safeguarding assessment; pregnancy and postpartum illness require urgent review of psychosis, mood, safety, infant care, and treatment.

  • Older adults, veterans and firearm owners, people with cognitive or developmental disability, and people needing language or cultural adaptation require tailored questions, means assessment, and support.

Nclex Traps

  • Direct suicide questions are appropriate; a positive screen is not a diagnosis, prediction, or disposition.

  • Protective factors and apparent calm do not negate plan, intent, accessible means, preparations, or a recent attempt.

  • Observation levels are clinical and policy decisions, not universal numeric cutoffs, and esketamine does not replace indicated hospitalization.
