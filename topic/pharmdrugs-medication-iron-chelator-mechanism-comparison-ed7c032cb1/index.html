Used To Treat

Chronic transfusional iron overload and selected non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia iron overload depending on product. Chelation removes excess storage iron over time; it does not treat ordinary iron-deficiency anemia and can become dangerous when continued after iron burden is low.

Description

Repeated transfusion delivers iron that the body cannot actively excrete. Once transferrin becomes saturated, non-transferrin-bound iron drives free-radical injury in the liver, heart, endocrine organs, and marrow. Chelators surround ferric iron so it can be eliminated. Deferasirox is tridentate: two molecules coordinate one Fe3+ ion and the complex leaves mainly in bile and feces. Deferiprone is bidentate: three molecules coordinate one Fe3+ ion and the complex is excreted mainly in urine. These structural differences explain dosing, elimination, and monitoring. Overchelation is not extra protection; it removes needed metal and increases organ toxicity.

Mechanism

Ferric iron has six coordination sites. Each deferasirox molecule supplies three donor sites, so two drug molecules form a stable 2:1 complex with one Fe3+ ion that is excreted largely through the hepatobiliary route. Each deferiprone molecule supplies two donor sites, so three molecules form a 3:1 complex that is eliminated mainly in urine. Chelation lowers labile plasma iron and gradually reduces tissue stores, but benefit depends on net iron balance across transfusion input and drug removal. Deferasirox can injure renal tubules, liver, and GI mucosa; deferiprone can suppress neutrophils through an incompletely understood idiosyncratic mechanism. Ferritin trends are useful but inflammation distorts them, so liver iron concentration and organ assessment may be needed.

Boxed Warning

Deferasirox carries boxed warnings for renal failure, hepatic failure, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Deferiprone carries a boxed warning for agranulocytosis and neutropenia requiring scheduled ANC monitoring and immediate interruption for infection symptoms or neutropenia. Chelation must be reduced or interrupted as iron burden falls because overchelation can become life-threatening.

Adverse Effects

  • Acute kidney injury, Fanconi syndrome, hepatic failure, GI ulceration or bleeding, rash, auditory or ocular toxicity with deferasirox

  • Agranulocytosis/neutropenia, liver-enzyme elevation, zinc deficiency, nausea, and arthralgia with deferiprone

  • Overchelation with worsening renal, hepatic, marrow, growth, or micronutrient toxicity

Contraindications

  • Do not use chelation for iron-deficiency anemia or without evidence of pathologic iron burden.

  • Deferasirox has renal, platelet, performance-status, malignancy, and hepatic restrictions that require the current label.

  • Deferiprone requires reliable ANC access and immediate response to fever, sore throat, or infection.

Nursing Essentials

  • Track transfusion iron input, ferritin trend, liver iron concentration when indicated, organ function, and the product-specific stop or reduction threshold.

  • Teach that fever during deferiprone is a same-day blood-count problem and that dehydration during deferasirox can turn a stable regimen into acute kidney and liver injury.

Interactions

  • Deferasirox interacts with nephrotoxic, ulcerogenic, anticoagulant, CYP, OATP, and BCRP substrates; aluminum antacids are avoided.

  • Deferiprone plus another neutropenia-causing drug raises marrow risk; mineral loss can require zinc monitoring.

Key Labs

  • Ferritin trend, liver iron concentration when indicated, CBC/ANC, creatinine/eGFR, urinalysis/protein, electrolytes, AST/ALT/bilirubin, and zinc

  • Cardiac, hepatic, endocrine, auditory, ocular, growth, and transfusion-burden assessment

Nclex Traps

  • Chelators remove excess iron; they are not treatments for low iron.

  • Ferritin alone can rise with inflammation, and a falling ferritin can signal overchelation rather than uncomplicated success.
