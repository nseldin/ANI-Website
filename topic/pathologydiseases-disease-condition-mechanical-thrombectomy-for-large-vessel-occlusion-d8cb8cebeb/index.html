Risk Factors

  • Large core and poor collaterals reduce recoverable tissue, although current evidence supports benefit in some larger-core patients.

  • Tortuous vessels, tandem lesions, dissection, severe atherosclerosis, bleeding risk, or difficult access can increase procedural complexity.

  • Delay from recognition, transfer, imaging, or anesthesia allows core expansion and lowers potential benefit.

Signs Symptoms

  • Anterior LVO often causes gaze deviation, aphasia or neglect, field loss, and dense unilateral weakness.

  • Basilar LVO can cause diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, reduced consciousness, or locked-in syndrome.

  • A low NIHSS can still conceal disabling or unstable LVO, so vascular imaging follows the syndrome rather than score alone.

Diagnostics

  • NCCT excludes hemorrhage and estimates early infarct burden; CTA confirms the occlusion and access anatomy.

  • CTP or MRI diffusion/perfusion assesses core and mismatch when required for late, unknown-onset, or large-core selection.

  • Document LKW, baseline function, disabling deficit, NIHSS items, BP, glucose, anticoagulants, airway, and transfer timeline because procedure choice is clinical plus imaging.

Labs

  • Glucose excludes mimic, while CBC, platelets, coagulation, renal function, blood type, and medication history support procedural and bleeding safety.

  • Do not let nonessential laboratory delay consume the treatment window when the emergency pathway allows parallel processing.

  • Postprocedure hemoglobin and renal or coagulation testing is guided by bleeding, contrast, medication, and access-site context.

Treatments

  • Activate the endovascular team and transfer pathway immediately after LVO recognition because workflow time changes brain outcome.

  • Give eligible IV thrombolysis without waiting for EVT because pharmacologic and mechanical reperfusion can be complementary.

  • Use aspiration, stent-retriever, angioplasty, stenting, anesthesia, and rescue strategies according to anatomy and specialist judgment rather than a single universal device plan.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm the stroke and endovascular handoff includes last-known-well, baseline function, NIH Stroke Scale, vessel and perfusion imaging, thrombolytic status, anticoagulants, allergies, renal information, and family contact because rapid decisions require a complete risk-and-benefit picture.

  • Prepare prescribed access, laboratory work, blood pressure control, airway strategy, and transfer without delaying the procedure for nonessential tasks because reperfusion benefit declines as salvageable tissue becomes infarcted.

  • Keep the treated limb straight as ordered and assess puncture site, distal pulse, color, temperature, capillary refill, pain, and swelling because femoral or radial access complications can cause occult hemorrhage or limb ischemia.

  • Monitor NIH Stroke Scale trend, consciousness, pupils, blood pressure within the post-reperfusion target, oxygenation, rhythm, glucose, headache, nausea, and urine because hemorrhage, edema, reocclusion, reperfusion injury, and contrast-related complications need early recognition.

  • Escalate for NIH Stroke Scale increase, severe headache, vomiting, acute hypertension, reduced consciousness, expanding access hematoma, absent distal pulse, hypotension, flank pain, or airway compromise because intracranial hemorrhage and access-site bleeding are time-critical emergencies.

Complications

  • Intracranial hemorrhage, vessel perforation or dissection, distal embolization, vasospasm, re-occlusion, and failed reperfusion can worsen neurologic injury.

  • Groin or radial hematoma, retroperitoneal bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, limb ischemia, contrast reaction, and kidney injury are procedure-related risks.

  • Cerebral edema and no-reflow can progress even after technically successful reperfusion because opened artery does not guarantee viable microcirculation.

Contraindications

  • Do not redirect a thrombectomy query to mechanical heart-valve complications; the procedure is cerebral endovascular clot retrieval.

  • Do not promise EVT based on time alone because vessel, imaging, disability, baseline, and procedural risk must align.

  • Do not withhold otherwise eligible IVT simply because EVT is planned.

Red Flags

  • New neurologic deficit, NIH Stroke Scale increase, severe headache, vomiting, or seizure

  • Reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, posturing, aspiration, or inability to protect the airway

  • Expanding access-site hematoma, uncontrolled bleeding, hypotension, or new flank or back pain

  • Absent distal pulse, cool pale limb, severe access-limb pain, or progressive swelling

Patient Education

  • Keep the access limb in the instructed position and call before getting up because movement can reopen the artery and cause serious bleeding.

  • Report new weakness, speech change, headache, nausea, groin swelling, numbness, or limb coolness immediately because these can signal reocclusion or procedural complications.

Nclex Traps

  • EVT is not open craniotomy and is not a heart-valve procedure.

  • A 24-hour pathway is selected, not automatic, eligibility.

  • Successful reperfusion does not end neurologic or access-site monitoring.

Name

Mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion

Category

Endovascular stroke procedure

Definition

Mechanical thrombectomy is catheter-based removal of an intracranial large-vessel clot using aspiration, a stent retriever, or combined technique. It restores flow directly and is standard care for appropriately selected LVO stroke. The procedure is not open-brain surgery: arterial access, commonly from the groin or wrist, is advanced under imaging to the occlusion. Eligibility integrates disabling deficit, vessel and anatomy, infarct core, collateral or mismatch imaging when required, baseline function, bleeding and procedural risk, time, and expert judgment.

Etiology

Thrombectomy treats an accessible arterial occlusion caused by embolus or thrombosis; it does not treat every ischemic stroke, diffuse small-vessel disease, primary hemorrhage, or tissue already irreversibly infarcted. Tandem stenosis, dissection, intracranial atherosclerosis, or unusual anatomy can complicate access and recanalization.

Pathology

A large clot in the ICA, proximal MCA, basilar artery, or another selected vessel blocks flow to a broad territory. A microcatheter crosses or approaches the thrombus, then aspiration or device capture removes it. Restoring large-artery patency can rescue collateral-supported penumbra, but microvascular no-reflow, distal emboli, or established core may limit recovery.

Pathophysiology

Benefit depends on achieving meaningful reperfusion before threatened tissue becomes irreversible. Standard early anterior-circulation pathways are strongest for proximal ICA/M1-type LVO, while imaging selects many patients in later windows. The 2026 guideline supports EVT for selected anterior LVO from 0 to 6 hours and selected patients from 6 to 24 hours, expands benefit to some larger-core patients, and strongly recommends EVT for selected basilar occlusion within 24 hours when NIHSS is at least 10. These population recommendations do not make every person within 24 hours eligible; vessel, imaging, disability, baseline, anatomy, and risk still decide.
