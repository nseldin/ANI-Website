Class

Long-acting progestin contraceptive delivered by a radiopaque subdermal implant

Used To Treat

Prevention of pregnancy for up to five years with the current U.S. NEXPLANON label; etonogestrel is also the active progestin released from some combination vaginal-ring products, which are separate formulations.

Boxed Warning

Current 2026 NEXPLANON labeling carries a boxed warning for complications from improper insertion and removal. Deep, intramuscular, intrafascial, intravascular, or misplaced insertion can cause pain, paresthesia, bleeding, hematoma, scarring, nerve or vascular injury, migration to pulmonary vessels, difficult or impossible removal, and need for surgery. Proper training is required, and NEXPLANON is available under a REMS. Palpate both ends immediately after insertion and have the patient palpate it. If it is not palpable, use backup nonhormonal contraception until presence is confirmed and localize it before any removal attempt.

Mechanism

Etonogestrel diffuses from an ethylene-vinyl acetate polymer and binds nuclear progesterone receptors. Sustained systemic concentrations slow hypothalamic GnRH pulse frequency and blunt pituitary LH, preventing the midcycle LH surge and ovulation. Cervical mucus becomes scant and viscous, reducing sperm penetration, and endometrial glands become atrophic. Release is highest early and gradually declines, yet remains sufficient through the labeled five-year interval in eligible users. CYP3A metabolism means enzyme inducers can lower exposure enough to permit follicular activity or pregnancy. Barium sulfate makes NEXPLANON radiopaque, so X-ray or CT can localize a nonpalpable rod; high-frequency ultrasound or MRI can also be used. The insertion plane over triceps avoids the neurovascular sulcus.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify current trained-clinician and REMS requirements, insertion site, aseptic technique, and immediate palpability. Document arm, exact location, insertion date and removal deadline at five years.

  • Teach expected bleeding variability, how to palpate the rod, and urgent symptoms: chest pain, dyspnea, cough/hemoptysis, focal arm neurologic symptoms, severe lower abdominal pain, pregnancy symptoms, jaundice, or thrombosis.

Contraindications

  • Known or suspected pregnancy, current or past thrombosis/thromboembolic disorder, active liver disease or liver tumor, unexplained uterine bleeding, breast cancer or another progestin-sensitive cancer, and component allergy.

  • Do not attempt blind removal of a nonpalpable implant. Confirm location and refer to a clinician experienced in deep removal near nerves or vessels.

Interactions

  • CYP3A inducers such as rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, topiramate at inducing exposure, some antiretrovirals, and St. John's wort can lower effectiveness and increase breakthrough bleeding.

  • Strong CYP3A inhibitors can raise etonogestrel exposure; lamotrigine and other hormone-sensitive regimens require product-specific review, especially when a combined estrogen ring rather than implant is involved.

Key Labs

  • Pregnancy assessment before insertion; no routine serum etonogestrel level is needed, though specialized blood testing can help confirm presence when imaging fails

  • Implant palpability and site, bleeding pattern, blood pressure, mood, medication inducers, liver or thrombotic symptoms, and pregnancy/ectopic warning signs

Nclex Traps

  • The current U.S. NEXPLANON duration is up to five years; three years is outdated labeling.

  • A nonpalpable rod is not proof of absence. Prevent pregnancy with backup, localize by imaging, and never explore blindly near the arm's neurovascular structures.

Description

Etonogestrel is the active desogestrel metabolite and a potent progesterone-receptor agonist that suppresses the LH surge, prevents ovulation, thickens cervical mucus, and thins endometrium during continuous implant exposure. NEXPLANON places 68 mg inside a flexible radiopaque rod under the skin of the upper arm. The current U.S. label extends contraceptive use through five years, not the older three-year teaching. The device and drug must be understood together: a rod inserted too deeply can injure nerves or vessels, become nonpalpable, or migrate into pulmonary vasculature. Because the implant is removable, fertility usually returns quickly after removal, unlike the prolonged pharmacokinetic tail of DMPA.

Adverse Effects

  • Unpredictable bleeding or amenorrhea, headache, acne, breast pain, abdominal pain, weight change, mood symptoms, vaginitis, and insertion-site discomfort

  • Deep insertion, neurovascular injury, intravascular or pulmonary migration, thrombosis, ectopic pregnancy if failure occurs, liver injury, severe depression, ovarian cyst, anaphylaxis, or difficult surgical removal
