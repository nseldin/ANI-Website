Definition

Erythema is a visible skin-color change caused by increased blood flow in superficial skin vessels. It may look red, pink, dusky, or simply different from the patient's usual skin tone. It describes a finding, not a disease.

Plain Language

Erythema means an area of skin looks redder or otherwise changed from baseline because more blood is flowing near the surface.

Why It Matters

Erythema can help identify inflammation, pressure injury, allergy, infection, or medication reaction, but it does not establish the cause. Rapid spread with fever or severe pain, bullae, numbness, dusky color, or systemic illness can indicate a dangerous skin or soft-tissue infection and needs urgent assessment.

Associated Conditions

  • increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from local vasodilation or inflammation caused by pressure, heat, friction, infection, allergy, injury, medication reaction, or inflammatory disease

Assessment Method

Inspect in neutral light and compare with the patient's baseline, then document location, border, shape, distribution, warmth, tenderness, swelling, firmness, blanching when gentle pressure is appropriate, skin integrity, pain or itch, rate of spread, exposures, medications, fever, and systemic findings; use warmth, texture, edema, and symptoms as well as color when visual change is subtle.

Positive Finding

An area is visibly red, pink, dusky, or otherwise changed from baseline because of increased superficial blood flow; it may be warm, tender, swollen, or blanching or nonblanching depending on the cause.

Mechanism

Inflammatory or vascular mediators dilate superficial vessels and increase local blood volume; persistent pressure, vascular injury, or severe inflammation can alter blanching and surrounding tissue findings.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Erythema finding raises concern for increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from local vasodilation or inflammation caused by pressure, heat, friction, infection, allergy, injury, medication reaction, or inflammatory disease because inflammatory or vascular mediators dilate superficial vessels and increase local blood volume; persistent pressure, vascular injury, or severe inflammation can alter blanching and surrounding tissue findings. Use Erythema to focus evaluation of increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from local vasodilation or inflammation caused by pressure, heat, friction, infection, allergy, injury, medication reaction, or inflammatory disease and the reviewed alternatives: Purpura, Ecchymosis, Cyanosis, Urticaria. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Erythema as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Color perception varies with skin pigmentation, lighting, temperature, and observer; blanching is not cause-specific, serious deep infection can look mild early, and erythema alone does not prove infection, allergy, or pressure injury. Therefore, Erythema cannot confirm or exclude increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from local vasodilation or inflammation caused by pressure, heat, friction, infection, allergy, injury, medication reaction, or inflammatory disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

  • Purpura

  • Ecchymosis

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as Purpura, Ecchymosis, Cyanosis, Urticaria.

False Negative Situations

Erythema may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of increased superficial cutaneous blood flow from local vasodilation or inflammation caused by pressure, heat, friction, infection, allergy, injury, medication reaction, or inflammatory disease.
