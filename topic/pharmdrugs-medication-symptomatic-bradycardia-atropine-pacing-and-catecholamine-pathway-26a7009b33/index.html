Used To Treat

Adult bradycardia producing hypotension, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure while reversible causes and the exact conduction problem are assessed.

Description

A slow heart rate is treated when it causes inadequate perfusion, not because one number is below normal. Atropine blocks cardiac muscarinic M2 signaling and removes parasympathetic braking at the SA and AV nodes. That works best when excessive vagal tone or nodal tissue is the problem. It may be ineffective when conduction failure is below the AV node because the diseased His-Purkinje system is not being held slow by vagal input. If perfusion remains poor, transcutaneous pacing directly depolarizes myocardium, while epinephrine or dopamine increases beta-1 cAMP signaling to support rate and contractility. Oxygenation, ischemia, medications, hyperkalemia, hypothermia, and other causes must be corrected because the algorithm is a bridge, not the diagnosis.

Mechanism

M2 receptors couple to Gi, reduce adenylyl cyclase, open potassium currents, and slow pacemaker depolarization and AV conduction. Atropine competitively blocks those receptors, allowing intrinsic sympathetic and pacemaker activity to dominate. Transcutaneous pacing delivers electrical current across the chest to capture myocardium independently of the blocked native pathway; electrical spikes without a palpable pulse or pressure rise are not mechanical capture. Epinephrine and dopamine increase beta-1 receptor signaling, cAMP, calcium entry, chronotropy, and inotropy, but they also increase myocardial oxygen demand and dysrhythmia risk. High-grade infranodal block, transplanted-heart physiology, and rapidly deteriorating perfusion therefore favor early pacing and expert support rather than repeated faith in atropine.

Boxed Warning

High-alert safety warning: treat bradycardia when it produces hypotension, acute altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure; the rate alone is not the indication. The 2025 AHA adult algorithm uses atropine 1 mg IV, repeated every 3 to 5 minutes to a 3 mg maximum. If it is ineffective, begin transcutaneous pacing and/or dopamine 5 to 20 mcg/kg/min or epinephrine 2 to 10 mcg/min while seeking expert and transvenous-pacing support. Atropine may fail in high-grade infranodal block because vagal blockade cannot repair diseased His-Purkinje conduction, so do not delay pacing while perfusion worsens. Assess oxygenation, ischemia, potassium, temperature, and medication or toxin causes in parallel. During transcutaneous pacing, confirm both electrical and mechanical capture; provide analgesia or sedation only as hemodynamics permit.

Adverse Effects

  • Atropine can cause tachycardia, dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, delirium, and reduced sweating; pacing is painful in conscious patients and can cause skeletal-muscle contraction.

  • Catecholamines can cause tachyarrhythmia, ischemia, hypertension, extravasation injury, and increased myocardial oxygen consumption.

Contraindications

  • In immediately life-threatening symptomatic bradycardia, relative anticholinergic cautions do not outweigh perfusion rescue, but the rhythm and cause still determine whether atropine is likely to work.

  • Do not delay pacing in severe high-grade block or worsening instability while waiting for an atropine response that may never occur.

Nursing Essentials

  • Assess airway, breathing, oxygenation, pulse, pressure, mental status, chest discomfort, heart failure, rhythm strip, 12-lead ECG, IV access, and reversible causes in parallel.

  • During pacing, confirm both electrical capture and a corresponding pulse or arterial waveform; provide analgesia or sedation when hemodynamics permit without sacrificing perfusion.

Interactions

  • Beta blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, digoxin, antiarrhythmics, sedatives, and cholinergic exposures can contribute to bradycardia and alter response.

  • Epinephrine or dopamine with other sympathomimetics raises tachyarrhythmia and ischemia risk, but emergency benefit may still dominate under continuous monitoring.

Key Labs

  • Continuous ECG, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, perfusion, glucose, potassium, magnesium, calcium, temperature, drug levels or toxicology context when relevant, and ischemia evaluation.

  • Document the rhythm and hemodynamic response after each intervention rather than charting heart rate alone.

Nclex Traps

  • Atropine treats symptomatic bradycardia with a pulse; it is not used for asystole or pulseless electrical activity.

  • Pacing spikes are not enough. Mechanical capture requires a pulse, pressure rise, or perfusing waveform after each captured complex.
