Risk Factors

  • Atherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and prior TIA raise posterior as well as anterior stroke risk.

  • Vertebral dissection risk rises after neck trauma or connective-tissue vulnerability because an intimal tear can narrow the lumen or generate emboli.

  • Basilar thrombosis can evolve after transient diplopia, dysarthria, vertigo, or weakness, so fluctuating posterior symptoms require urgent evaluation.

Signs Symptoms

  • Sudden persistent vertigo with inability to stand or walk, direction-changing nystagmus, severe truncal ataxia, or focal neurologic findings suggests a central process.

  • Diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, hoarseness, facial sensory change, limb weakness, and crossed findings localize toward brainstem pathways.

  • Decreased consciousness, quadriparesis, abnormal pupils, or preserved awareness with near-total paralysis suggests severe basilar involvement.

Diagnostics

  • Perform a full eye-movement, cranial-nerve, gait, limb-coordination, motor, sensory, speech, swallowing, and consciousness exam because FAST and NIHSS can miss posterior deficits.

  • NCCT excludes hemorrhage but is insensitive for early posterior-fossa ischemia; CTA head-neck evaluates vertebral and basilar patency, while MRI DWI better detects small brainstem or cerebellar infarction.

  • Urgently involve stroke and endovascular teams when basilar occlusion is possible because deterioration can be abrupt and EVT selection is time-sensitive.

Labs

  • Bedside glucose excludes a mimic, while acute reperfusion labs and anticoagulant history frame bleeding safety.

  • ECG and telemetry search for cardioembolism; vascular imaging searches for atherosclerosis or dissection because prevention depends on mechanism.

  • Toxic, metabolic, and infectious testing is selected when the exam is nonfocal, but it should not delay vascular imaging for a concerning focal syndrome.

Treatments

  • Evaluate eligible disabling posterior-circulation stroke for IV thrombolysis because a low NIHSS may still conceal severe disability.

  • Activate EVT evaluation for basilar or other treatable LVO because reopening the artery can prevent catastrophic brainstem infarction.

  • Monitor cerebellar infarcts for edema and hydrocephalus because decompression or CSF diversion can become lifesaving even after the initial vessel decision.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess sudden vertigo with inability to walk, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, nystagmus, visual-field loss, crossed findings, limb ataxia, weakness, and consciousness because posterior strokes are often missed by face-arm-speech screening.

  • Establish last-known-well, baseline function, glucose, anticoagulants, headache and neck trauma, then activate emergency brain and vascular imaging because basilar or vertebral occlusion can deteriorate catastrophically despite a modest NIH Stroke Scale score.

  • Maintain nothing-by-mouth status, protect the airway, use aspiration and fall precautions, and prepare reperfusion transfer because bulbar dysfunction and severe imbalance create immediate risks while posterior tissue remains salvageable.

  • Monitor consciousness, pupils, eye movements, speech, swallowing, respiratory pattern, limb strength, coordination, blood pressure, rhythm, and oxygenation because brainstem edema or basilar progression can abruptly impair ventilation and airway reflexes.

  • Escalate immediately for declining consciousness, new quadriparesis, abnormal pupils or eye movements, inability to handle secretions, apnea, recurrent vomiting, NIH Stroke Scale increase, or severe occipital headache because basilar occlusion, hemorrhage, and brainstem compression require emergency intervention.

Complications

  • Aspiration and respiratory failure can occur when medullary or pontine pathways fail.

  • Cerebellar edema can compress the brainstem or obstruct CSF, causing hydrocephalus and herniation.

  • Basilar occlusion can cause locked-in syndrome, coma, extensive disability, or death.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose benign vertigo from dizziness alone when the patient cannot walk, has focal findings, or has new severe headache or neck pain.

  • Do not rely on a negative early CT to exclude posterior ischemia.

  • Do not call a low NIHSS nondisabling without asking about gait, diplopia, swallowing, vision, and hand function.

Red Flags

  • Sudden diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, severe ataxia, or inability to stand unaided

  • Declining consciousness, quadriparesis, locked-in features, or abnormal respiratory pattern

  • Inability to handle secretions, aspiration, apnea, cyanosis, or severe oxygen desaturation

  • Severe occipital headache, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, seizure, or rapid neurologic decline

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for sudden severe imbalance, double vision, slurred speech, or swallowing difficulty because posterior stroke may lack facial droop.

  • Do not walk unassisted, eat, drink, or drive during abrupt posterior symptoms because falls and aspiration can occur before diagnosis.

Nclex Traps

  • Posterior stroke is not synonymous with simple dizziness.

  • A low NIHSS can underestimate posterior circulation disability.

  • Cerebellar infarction can become a neurosurgical emergency when swelling compresses the brainstem.

Vertebrobasilar and posterior circulation stroke

Category

Neurovascular emergency

Definition

Vertebrobasilar and posterior circulation stroke is arterial ischemia caused by an occlusion or critically reduced blood flow in vertebral, basilar, cerebellar, or posterior cerebral arteries. The resulting injury can affect the brainstem, cerebellum, occipital lobes, thalami, and connected pathways, causing vertigo, severe imbalance, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, nystagmus, crossed face-body findings, visual loss, weakness, or reduced consciousness. These strokes are high risk because compact brainstem anatomy places cranial-nerve, long motor-sensory, respiratory, and arousal pathways close together, yet common stroke scales can underweight many of these deficits.

Etiology

Mechanisms include vertebral or basilar atherosclerotic thrombosis, cardiac or artery-to-artery embolism, vertebral dissection, small perforator disease, and less common inflammatory or hypercoagulable vasculopathy. Neck trauma or sudden neck pain can accompany dissection, but absence of pain does not exclude it.

Pathology

The vertebral arteries join to form the basilar artery, which supplies the pons through perforators and gives rise to cerebellar and posterior cerebral branches. Occlusion location determines whether the dominant injury is medullary, pontine, midbrain, cerebellar, thalamic, or occipital. A basilar occlusion can disconnect the cortex from motor output while sparing consciousness, producing locked-in syndrome, or can damage arousal pathways and cause coma.

Pathophysiology

Central vertigo arises when ischemia disrupts vestibular nuclei or cerebellar connections. Crossed findings occur because cranial-nerve structures affect the ipsilateral face while long motor or sensory tracts affect the contralateral body. Cerebellar swelling can compress the fourth ventricle and brainstem, causing hydrocephalus or sudden deterioration. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline strongly supports EVT for selected basilar-artery occlusion presenting within 24 hours when NIHSS is at least 10, but individual eligibility still depends on imaging, timing, disability, anatomy, bleeding risk, and local protocol.
