Definition

Perthes test is a bedside venous maneuver. It is a clue to deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities. A positive finding means varicosities empty when deep veins are patent; pain and persistent distention raise concern for obstruction.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Perthes test means varicosities empty when deep veins are patent; pain and persistent distention raise concern for obstruction.

Why It Matters

A positive Perthes test response can signal deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities. Calf-muscle pumping diverts blood into deep veins when they are open. Perthes test cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities

Assessment Method

Apply a superficial tourniquet and have an appropriate patient walk briefly while observing veins and pain.

Positive Finding

Varicosities empty when deep veins are patent; pain and persistent distention raise concern for obstruction.

Mechanism

Calf-muscle pumping diverts blood into deep veins when they are open.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Perthes test response raises concern for deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities because calf-muscle pumping diverts blood into deep veins when they are open. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Perthes test does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Perthes test to focus evaluation of deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities and the reviewed alternatives: arterial claudication, muscle pain, poor tourniquet placement. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Apply a superficial tourniquet and have an appropriate patient walk briefly while observing veins and pain. Evidence Limitations

Poor accuracy and exercise risk make it unsuitable for suspected acute thrombosis. Therefore, Perthes test cannot confirm or exclude deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • arterial claudication

  • muscle pain

  • poor tourniquet placement

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from arterial claudication, muscle pain, poor tourniquet placement, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Perthes test may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Obsolete and potentially unsafe; duplex ultrasound is preferred. Perthes test remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude deep venous patency in a limb with varicosities. Apply the reviewed limitation for Perthes test: Poor accuracy and exercise risk make it unsuitable for suspected acute thrombosis.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Perthes test, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Apply a superficial tourniquet and have an appropriate patient walk briefly while observing veins and pain. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not perform when acute DVT, ischemia, ulceration, or fall risk is possible.
