Used To Treat

Immediate treatment of glucose below 70 mg/dL in a conscious person who can safely swallow and is not NPO. A common adult/inpatient protocol gives 15 g of fast-acting glucose, rechecks in 15 minutes, and repeats while glucose remains below 70 mg/dL or is not trending upward. Individual protocols may use less carbohydrate in young children or some automated-insulin-delivery situations. Unsafe swallowing, seizure, or unconsciousness requires glucagon or IV dextrose rather than oral treatment.

Description

Oral glucose is D-glucose supplied as tablets, gel, liquid, or another measured fast-acting form to correct hypoglycemia in a person who can swallow. It is already a monosaccharide, so intestinal absorption does not wait for starch or sucrose digestion. That makes glucose preferable to high-fat sweets, whose fat slows gastric delivery, and preferable to sucrose when acarbose is blocking alpha-glucosidase cleavage. The 15-15 rule is a reassessment loop, not permission to keep stacking sugar without checking: treat, wait about 15 minutes, recheck, repeat if still low, then address the cause and recurrence risk.

Mechanism

Luminal D-glucose enters small-intestinal enterocytes mainly through the apical sodium-glucose cotransporter SGLT1, powered by the sodium gradient maintained by sodium-potassium ATPase. Glucose then exits across the basolateral GLUT2 transporter into portal blood, directly increasing circulating substrate for the brain and other tissues. No enzymatic carbohydrate cleavage is required, which explains why pure glucose still works during alpha-glucosidase inhibition with acarbose. The glucose rise treats neuroglycopenia but does not remove excess insulin, a sulfonylurea, missed nutrition, alcohol effect, renal drug accumulation, or another cause; those processes can outlast the absorbed glucose and produce another low.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Oral glucose has no boxed warning, but route selection is a safety decision. Do not place tablets, liquid, food, or gel into the mouth of an unconscious, seizing, severely confused, vomiting, NPO, or swallowing-impaired patient because choking and aspiration can be fatal. Use glucagon or IV dextrose per protocol, protect the airway, and evaluate persistent or recurrent hypoglycemia rather than repeatedly masking it with oral sugar.

Adverse Effects

  • Transient hyperglycemia, nausea, abdominal discomfort, or overtreatment followed by glucose variability

  • Choking or aspiration when oral treatment is given despite impaired consciousness or swallowing

  • Recurrent hypoglycemia when insulin, a secretagogue, alcohol, exercise, poor intake, renal impairment, or endocrine disease continues to act

Contraindications

  • Unconsciousness, active seizure, inability to follow commands or swallow safely, severe vomiting, or major aspiration risk

  • NPO status or another clinical restriction that requires IV or protocol-directed rescue

  • Do not rely on oral glucose alone when hypoglycemia persists, recurs, or accompanies critical illness, long-acting insulin, or sulfonylurea toxicity

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm glucose and immediately assess airway, mental status, swallow safety, IV access, NPO status, and the likely cause. If oral treatment is safe, give the protocol-specified measured fast glucose rather than an unmeasured snack.

  • Recheck in about 15 minutes and repeat according to protocol until glucose is at least 70 mg/dL or clearly rising. When stable and a meal is not imminent, add longer-lasting carbohydrate/protein as appropriate, reconcile insulin or secretagogues, and continue observation when recurrence risk remains.

Interactions

  • Acarbose delays sucrose and starch breakdown, so use D-glucose/dextrose rather than table sugar for rescue.

  • Insulin, sulfonylureas, and meglitinides can continue lowering glucose after oral treatment; dose/meal mismatch or renal accumulation must be corrected.

  • Beta blockers may hide tremor and tachycardia, while alcohol can impair hepatic glucose output; neither makes a normal-looking pulse proof that the low is resolved.

Key Labs

  • Immediate capillary or plasma glucose and repeat about 15 minutes after treatment; use a fingerstick when symptoms and lagging CGM data disagree

  • Serial glucose until recurrence risk is acceptably low

  • Cause-directed renal/hepatic function, medication timing, nutrition, alcohol/exercise history, cortisol or toxicology testing when clinically indicated

Nclex Traps

  • The standard rescue amount is commonly 15 grams, not 15 milligrams.

  • Oral glucose is for a patient who can swallow safely; an unconscious patient needs glucagon or IV dextrose.

  • Chocolate is not ideal first rescue because fat slows absorption, and sucrose is unreliable during acarbose therapy because it still requires enzymatic cleavage.

  • A normal recheck does not end the problem when a long-acting glucose-lowering drug can produce another low.
