Definition

Clostridium perfringens is a clinically important bacterium associated with foodborne gastroenteritis, clostridial myonecrosis, traumatic wound infection, and sepsis with hemolysis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

  • Content

    Clostridium perfringens is a Gram-positive bacillus with a obligate anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, spore-forming, nonmotile, and extracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Rapid tissue growth and alpha toxin can destroy cell membranes and muscle, while enterotoxin formed in the intestine causes a shorter self-limited foodborne syndrome. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

  • Content

    Clostridium perfringens is associated with foodborne gastroenteritis, clostridial myonecrosis, traumatic wound infection, and sepsis with hemolysis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.
