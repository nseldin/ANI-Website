Definition

Antiphospholipid syndrome is an acquired autoimmune thrombophilia in which persistent antiphospholipid antibodies are associated with objectively confirmed thrombosis, defined pregnancy morbidity, or both. A positive antibody test alone is not the syndrome, and the prolonged clotting time sometimes caused by lupus anticoagulant does not mean the patient is protected from clotting.

Pathology

Thrombi may affect veins, arteries, or small vessels in nearly any organ. Placental pathology can show thrombosis, infarction, and impaired remodeling. Catastrophic APS produces rapid small-vessel thrombosis in multiple organs, often with inflammation and tissue ischemia.

Pathophysiology

  • Pathogenic antibodies recognize phospholipid-binding proteins such as beta-2 glycoprotein I and alter endothelial cells, platelets, monocytes, coagulation, fibrinolysis, complement, and trophoblast function. These changes increase clot formation and weaken normal clot-limiting pathways, which creates a persistent prothrombotic state.

  • A second stress such as infection, surgery, immobility, pregnancy, or estrogen exposure may shift this primed system into clinical thrombosis. In pregnancy, complement activation and placental dysfunction can impair implantation and blood flow even without a large visible clot.

Etiology

Primary APS occurs without another systemic autoimmune disease. Secondary APS most often accompanies systemic lupus erythematosus but may occur with other autoimmune disease. Antiphospholipid antibodies may appear transiently during infection or after medications; persistence and clinical context are essential before assigning the syndrome.

Risk Factors

  • Lupus anticoagulant, high antibody titers, or positivity across all three standard antibody groups

  • Prior thrombosis or obstetric APS

  • Systemic lupus erythematosus

  • Smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, estrogen exposure, surgery, immobility, infection, pregnancy, and postpartum physiology

Signs Symptoms

  • Deep-vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, myocardial or limb ischemia

  • Recurrent fetal loss, fetal death, early severe placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, or growth restriction

  • Livedo reticularis or racemosa, skin ulcers, thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, valve disease, kidney microangiopathy, and neurologic manifestations

  • Catastrophic APS with rapid multiorgan dysfunction involving kidneys, lungs, brain, heart, skin, or adrenal glands

Diagnostics

  • Document the clinical event objectively with imaging, pathology, or obstetric records and test lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-I antibodies using standardized methods.

  • Confirm persistence on repeat testing at least twelve weeks later. Classification criteria organize research cohorts and support reasoning, but clinicians must still exclude stronger causes and should not turn one low-level positive result into a lifelong diagnosis.

  • Interpret lupus anticoagulant testing with the laboratory and hematology team because heparin, direct oral anticoagulants, warfarin, inflammation, and acute thrombosis can distort results.

  • Evaluate for systemic lupus erythematosus and conventional arterial or venous risk factors rather than attributing every event to antibodies.

Labs

  • Lupus anticoagulant is a functional phospholipid-dependent coagulation finding; it may prolong aPTT while conferring thrombosis risk in vivo.

  • Anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-I antibodies are measured by immunoassay and interpreted by isotype, level, persistence, and clinical context.

  • CBC may show thrombocytopenia or hemolysis; creatinine, urinalysis, liver tests, troponin, and hemolysis studies are selected by the affected organs.

  • A mixing study that fails to correct a prolonged phospholipid-dependent clotting time can support an inhibitor pattern but does not independently prove APS.

Assessment

Assess current and prior thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, antibody history, lupus features, smoking, estrogen exposure, recent infection, surgery, immobility, cancer risk, and the exact anticoagulant regimen. Examine for venous swelling, pulmonary compromise, focal neurologic deficit, digital or skin ischemia, bleeding, hypertension, and simultaneous organ dysfunction. In pregnancy, integrate maternal findings with placental and fetal surveillance rather than treating a laboratory antibody as the whole diagnosis.

Differential

Differentiate APS from inherited thrombophilia, provoked venous thromboembolism, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, complement-mediated microangiopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation, sepsis, malignancy, embolic heart disease, vasculitis, and cholesterol embolization. Obstetric evaluation must also consider preeclampsia, placental disorders, genetic fetal loss, and anatomic pregnancy complications.

Treatments

  • Thrombotic APS usually requires long-term vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation, with intensity individualized to venous versus arterial events, recurrence, and bleeding risk.

  • Obstetric APS is commonly managed with low-dose aspirin plus heparin during pregnancy and coordinated postpartum thromboprophylaxis; the exact plan depends on prior thrombosis and pregnancy history.

  • Control smoking, hypertension, lipids, diabetes, immobility, and estrogen exposure because antibody risk and conventional vascular risk are additive.

  • Catastrophic APS requires emergency multidisciplinary treatment of the trigger plus therapeutic anticoagulation and intensive immunomodulation, commonly corticosteroids with plasma exchange or immunoglobulin; organ support must not wait for every laboratory result.

  • Patients with antibodies but no APS event receive risk-based prevention rather than automatic full anticoagulation.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose APS from one positive antibody panel or from a prolonged aPTT alone.

  • Direct oral anticoagulants, particularly rivaroxaban, should not replace vitamin K antagonists in high-risk triple-positive APS and are generally avoided for arterial APS unless a specialist identifies an exceptional rationale.

  • Warfarin is teratogenic and is generally replaced by a pregnancy-compatible anticoagulant under specialist guidance.

  • Do not stop anticoagulation abruptly for a procedure or pregnancy without a documented bridging or transition plan.

Safety Considerations

  • Balance thrombosis and bleeding at every transition; thrombocytopenia in APS does not automatically eliminate clot risk.

  • Verify drug, dose, last administration, renal function, INR where applicable, and procedure plan before invasive care.

  • Use the laboratory's guidance when anticoagulants interfere with lupus-anticoagulant testing; an uninterpretable result should not be called negative.

  • New multiorgan decline can be catastrophic APS, sepsis, DIC, TTP, or another emergency and needs parallel evaluation.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess for unilateral swelling, chest pain, dyspnea, hypoxemia, focal neurologic change, abdominal pain, skin ischemia, and pregnancy warning signs to detect thrombosis or placental complications before organ injury progresses.

  • Monitor anticoagulant adherence, INR when ordered, CBC, bleeding, renal function, interactions, and peri-procedure instructions because both under-anticoagulation and excessive anticoagulation can cause serious harm.

  • In pregnancy, coordinate maternal and fetal surveillance, injection teaching, preeclampsia education, and a written delivery and postpartum anticoagulation plan.

  • Use rapid escalation for simultaneous kidney, lung, neurologic, skin, or hemodynamic deterioration because catastrophic APS can thrombose multiple organs over hours to days.

  • Teach smoking cessation, movement during travel, hydration, and early communication before estrogen therapy, surgery, pregnancy, or prolonged immobility.

Red Flags

  • Sudden focal neurologic deficit, chest pain, severe dyspnea, hemoptysis, syncope, or a cold painful limb

  • Pregnancy with severe headache, vision change, hypertension symptoms, bleeding, reduced fetal movement, or preterm labor concerns

  • Rapid failure of multiple organs, diffuse skin ischemia, shock, or acute kidney injury suggesting catastrophic APS

  • Major bleeding, intracranial symptoms, or hemodynamic change during anticoagulation

Complications

  • Recurrent venous or arterial thrombosis, stroke, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension

  • Pregnancy loss, placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, and preterm birth

  • Chronic kidney, valve, skin, cognitive, or neurologic injury

  • Catastrophic APS and anticoagulant-related major bleeding

Prognosis

Recurrence risk depends on the antibody profile, event type, coexisting autoimmune disease, vascular risks, and anticoagulant quality. Reliable long-term therapy substantially reduces thrombosis but does not erase risk. Catastrophic APS remains life-threatening and benefits from immediate recognition and combined treatment.

Prevention

Maintain the prescribed anticoagulant and monitoring plan after thrombotic APS. Reduce smoking, estrogen, immobility, and uncontrolled cardiovascular risks. Use individualized prophylaxis around surgery, hospitalization, pregnancy, postpartum, and prolonged travel. Treat infections promptly and maintain coordinated lupus control when APS is secondary to SLE.

Patient Education

  • A lupus anticoagulant can prolong a lab clotting test while increasing real-world clot risk; the name is misleading.

  • Carry the anticoagulant name and indication and tell every clinician before a procedure or new medicine.

  • Never double or stop anticoagulant doses without instructions; seek help for major bleeding or clot symptoms.

  • Plan pregnancy before conception so teratogenic medicine can be changed safely and early obstetric treatment can begin.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require obstetric, rheumatology, and hematology coordination because thrombosis, placental disease, and medication risks change over time.

  • Children with persistent antibodies and thrombosis need pediatric expertise; adult classification and anticoagulation assumptions are not automatically transferable.

  • Older adults and patients with kidney disease, thrombocytopenia, falls, or interacting medicines need intensified bleeding and dosing review.

Nclex Traps

  • Antiphospholipid antibodies without a qualifying clinical event are not the same as APS.

  • A prolonged aPTT does not mean the patient will bleed; lupus anticoagulant is associated with thrombosis.

  • Pregnancy treatment is not warfarin continuation; a specialist-directed aspirin and heparin plan is typical.

  • Rapid multiorgan thrombosis is catastrophic APS and requires emergency combined therapy, not anticoagulation follow-up alone.
