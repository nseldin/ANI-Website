Class Pathway

  • Broad PBP binding and beta-lactamase stability

  • Agent-specific nonfermenter spectrum

  • Renal exposure, CNS toxicity, and valproate depletion

Used To Treat

Severe susceptible polymicrobial, ESBL-producing, healthcare-associated, CNS, or resistant Gram-negative infections when a carbapenem's exact spectrum and site fit the organism.

Description

Carbapenems are broad PBP-binding beta-lactams stable to many ESBL and AmpC enzymes, but they are not identical and are not automatically active against carbapenemase producers. Imipenem is paired with cilastatin because renal dehydropeptidase I would otherwise destroy imipenem and generate a nephrotoxic metabolite; imipenem has the strongest seizure concern and is not the routine meningitis carbapenem. Meropenem is commonly used for susceptible Pseudomonas and bacterial meningitis because it combines broad Gram-negative activity with lower epileptogenic potential. Ertapenem's long half-life enables once-daily dosing but it lacks dependable Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Enterococcus activity. Doripenem is antipseudomonal, but its U.S. label warns against use for ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia after excess mortality and lower cure in a trial.

Mechanism

Carbapenems rapidly enter many Gram-negative organisms, bind multiple PBPs, and resist hydrolysis by many older serine beta-lactamases. KPC and other carbapenemases can still hydrolyze them; porin loss plus AmpC or ESBL overproduction can also raise MICs. Imipenem is uniquely vulnerable to human renal dehydropeptidase I, so cilastatin blocks that enzyme without antibacterial activity. Renal failure prolongs exposure for all major agents and raises CNS toxicity. Carbapenems also sharply lower valproate concentrations, probably through inhibition of valproate-glucuronide hydrolysis and altered recycling, so breakthrough seizures can occur even while a carbapenem itself lowers seizure threshold.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Avoid casual carbapenem escalation. These drugs can cause anaphylaxis, CDI, severe neurotoxicity when accumulated, and a rapid clinically important valproate interaction; broad exposure also selects carbapenem-resistant organisms.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, diarrhea, rash, phlebitis, liver-test abnormalities, cytopenias, CDI, hypersensitivity, confusion, myoclonus, and seizures can occur.

  • Imipenem has a prominent seizure signal, especially with CNS disease, excessive dose, or renal impairment; meropenem and ertapenem can also cause neurotoxicity when exposure is high.

Contraindications

  • Do not choose ertapenem when Pseudomonas or Acinetobacter requires coverage because its convenient once-daily kinetics do not create activity against those organisms.

  • Avoid a carbapenem with valproate when a workable alternative exists because concentration can fall rapidly and remain low after discontinuation; dose escalation of valproate is often ineffective.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before administration, verify organism/MIC, source, prior carbapenemase result, CNS history, renal trajectory, valproate use, and exact product. Recalculate during sepsis, dialysis transitions, or renal recovery because yesterday's dose can become today's underexposure or toxicity.

  • Treat new aphasia, hallucination, tremor, myoclonus, or reduced responsiveness as a possible drug concentration problem and escalate promptly because nonconvulsive seizures may be subtle.

Interactions

  • Carbapenems markedly reduce valproic acid/divalproex exposure and can precipitate seizures; use a noninteracting antimicrobial or alternative antiseizure strategy with specialist input.

  • Probenecid can increase selected carbapenem exposure and is generally not intentionally combined; other neurotoxic or nephrotoxic drugs increase clinical vulnerability.

Key Labs

  • Culture/MIC and carbapenemase testing, creatinine/eGFR, urine output, CBC, liver tests, mental status, seizure assessment, and valproate concentration when exposure has occurred.

  • A susceptible carbapenem MIC must be paired with the labeled or guideline-supported dose and infusion for the site; class name alone does not prove adequate exposure.

Nclex Traps

  • Ertapenem is the carbapenem with the major Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter gap.

  • Cilastatin protects imipenem from a human renal enzyme; it is not a beta-lactamase inhibitor and does not broaden bacterial spectrum.

Source Note

Current U.S. labels for imipenem/cilastatin, meropenem, ertapenem, doripenem, Recarbrio, and Vabomere.

Population Risks

  • Type

    pediatric

    Label

    Pediatric caution

    Note

    Pediatric indication, meningitis role, formulation, dose, interval, renal maturation, and maximum dose differ by agent and age. Use the exact label or pediatric infectious-disease protocol because beta-lactams are not safely scaled from an adult regimen by intuition.

  • Type

    geriatric

    Label

    Older adult caution

    Note

    Older adults can have sharply reduced renal clearance despite an unremarkable serum creatinine. Estimate kidney function, reassess it during acute illness, and connect new confusion, myoclonus, or seizure to possible beta-lactam accumulation rather than assuming delirium is unrelated.

  • Type

    pregnancy

    Label

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Many beta-lactams have substantial pregnancy and lactation experience, but infection severity, allergy history, maternal organ function, placental exposure, neonatal bilirubin handling, and the exact agent still matter. Treat serious infection promptly with an evidence-supported regimen rather than applying one class-wide pregnancy rule.
