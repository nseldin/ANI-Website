Definition

Lethargy is an abnormally drowsy state in which the person is slower and less attentive but still awakens to voice or light touch and can respond meaningfully.

Plain Language

The person is unusually sleepy and slow, but they wake fairly easily; this is less severe than stupor or coma.

Why It Matters

Lethargy can result from sedating medication, low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, low blood glucose, infection, metabolic illness, poisoning, or brain injury. Worsening wakefulness, weak breathing, poor airway protection, or a focal deficit requires urgent escalation.

Associated Conditions

  • mildly reduced wakefulness and attention from sleep deprivation, medication or substance effect, metabolic or endocrine disturbance, infection, hypoxia, hypercapnia, neurologic injury, or another systemic illness

Assessment Method

Call the patient's name, use light touch when appropriate, and document the least stimulus that produces eye opening, speech, command following, and sustained attention; compare with baseline and assess airway, breathing, oxygenation, bedside glucose, vital signs, pupils, and motor symmetry.

Positive Finding

The patient appears abnormally drowsy or slow but awakens with voice or light stimulation, responds meaningfully, and may drift back to sleep when stimulation stops.

Mechanism

Reduced activity in the ascending arousal system, cerebral hemispheres, or their metabolic support lowers wakefulness without producing the profound unresponsiveness of stupor or coma.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Lethargy finding raises concern for mildly reduced wakefulness and attention from sleep deprivation, medication or substance effect, metabolic or endocrine disturbance, infection, hypoxia, hypercapnia, neurologic injury, or another systemic illness because reduced activity in the ascending arousal system, cerebral hemispheres, or their metabolic support lowers wakefulness without producing the profound unresponsiveness of stupor or coma. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Lethargy does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Lethargy to focus evaluation of mildly reduced wakefulness and attention from sleep deprivation, medication or substance effect, metabolic or endocrine disturbance, infection, hypoxia, hypercapnia, neurologic injury, or another systemic illness and the reviewed alternatives: normal sleep, fatigue, depression, obtundation, stupor. Evidence Limitations

Normal sleep, fatigue, sedating medication, baseline developmental or neurologic differences, hearing loss, and timing of the examination can mimic lethargy; the word alone does not quantify consciousness. Therefore, Lethargy cannot confirm or exclude mildly reduced wakefulness and attention from sleep deprivation, medication or substance effect, metabolic or endocrine disturbance, infection, hypoxia, hypercapnia, neurologic injury, or another systemic illness by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as normal sleep, fatigue, depression, obtundation, stupor.

False Negative Situations

Lethargy may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of mildly reduced wakefulness and attention from sleep deprivation, medication or substance effect, metabolic or endocrine disturbance, infection, hypoxia, hypercapnia, neurologic injury, or another systemic illness.
