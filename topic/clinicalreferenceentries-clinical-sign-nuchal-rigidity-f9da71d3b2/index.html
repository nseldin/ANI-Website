Definition

Nuchal rigidity is involuntary painful resistance when the examiner gently tries to flex the neck forward. It is a sign of meningeal irritation, not simply any complaint of a stiff neck.

Plain Language

The neck resists bending forward because the tissues surrounding the brain and spinal cord may be irritated.

Why It Matters

Nuchal rigidity can occur with meningitis or another serious intracranial process. Its absence does not rule out meningitis, so fever, severe headache, altered mental status, seizure, or a nonblanching rash still requires emergency assessment.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

First exclude trauma or another reason not to move the cervical spine; with the supine head supported, gently assess passive neck flexion without force and document involuntary resistance, pain, range, fever, headache, photophobia, rash, mental status, focal deficits, seizure, age, immune status, and recent infection or procedure.

Positive Finding

Passive neck flexion meets involuntary resistance or painful spasm that prevents the chin from approaching the chest.

Mechanism

Flexion stretches irritated meninges and cervical nerve-root coverings, provoking protective muscle contraction and pain.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Nuchal rigidity finding raises concern for meningeal irritation from meningitis, subarachnoid bleeding, or another inflammatory or infectious process affecting the meninges because flexion stretches irritated meninges and cervical nerve-root coverings, provoking protective muscle contraction and pain. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Nuchal rigidity does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Evidence Limitations

Cervical arthritis, muscle strain, dystonia, trauma, parkinsonian rigidity, guarding, reduced consciousness, age, and prior medication can alter the examination; neither presence nor absence identifies the cause. Therefore, Nuchal rigidity cannot confirm or exclude meningeal irritation from meningitis, subarachnoid bleeding, or another inflammatory or infectious process affecting the meninges by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as cervical muscle strain, cervical arthritis, dystonia, voluntary guarding.

False Negative Situations

Nuchal rigidity may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of meningeal irritation from meningitis, subarachnoid bleeding, or another inflammatory or infectious process affecting the meninges.

Modern Status

Current classic meningeal-irritation sign with limited sensitivity; its absence does not exclude meningitis, especially early in illness or in infants, older adults, or immunocompromised patients. When performed as described, Nuchal rigidity can adjust concern for meningeal irritation from meningitis, subarachnoid bleeding, or another inflammatory or infectious process affecting the meninges, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis.
