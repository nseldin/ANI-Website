Used To Treat

Bleeding and perioperative risk in von Willebrand disease. Mild responsive type 1 disease may be treated with desmopressin; severe deficiency, an unresponsive phenotype, many type 2 variants, type 3 disease, major bleeding, or major procedures generally require VWF-containing therapy under a hematology plan.

Description

Von Willebrand factor performs two jobs: it anchors platelets to injured vessel walls and carries factor VIII in circulation. That is why VWD can produce mucosal or menstrual bleeding from weak platelet adhesion and a secondary reduction in factor VIII. Desmopressin does not manufacture VWF; it releases the patient's stored VWF and factor VIII, so a prior response trial and subtype matter. VWF concentrate supplies functional protein when stores are inadequate or abnormal. Antifibrinolytics are especially useful on fibrinolytic mucosal surfaces because they protect the clot after it forms. Therapy is selected by subtype, baseline activity, bleed severity, prior response, and procedure target.

Mechanism

At vascular injury, VWF binds exposed collagen and changes conformation so platelet glycoprotein Ib can tether to it under shear. VWF also binds factor VIII and protects it from proteolytic clearance. Desmopressin activates endothelial V2 receptors, raises cAMP, and releases VWF and factor VIII from Weibel-Palade bodies; repeated closely spaced doses lose effect as stores deplete and add water retention. Plasma-derived VWF/FVIII complexes or recombinant VWF directly restore adhesive multimers; recombinant VWF may need initial factor VIII when baseline FVIII is low because endogenous VIII requires time to accumulate. Tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid blocks plasminogen docking on fibrin, which is useful in the mouth, nose, uterus, and urinary tract only when the indication and thrombosis risk are appropriate.

Boxed Warning

Desmopressin can cause life-threatening hyponatremia and may worsen thrombosis risk in type 2B VWD. VWF products can cause hypersensitivity, inhibitor formation, and thrombosis when VWF/FVIII levels become excessive. Treatment must follow the diagnosed subtype and measured response rather than the broad label of a bleeding disorder.

Adverse Effects

  • Hyponatremia, headache, flushing, blood-pressure change, or tachyphylaxis with desmopressin

  • Hypersensitivity, thrombosis, or inhibitor formation with VWF-containing products

  • Persistent bleeding when the phenotype is unresponsive or the procedure target is underdosed

Contraindications

  • Do not rely on desmopressin for type 3 VWD or an unresponsive severe phenotype.

  • Avoid desmopressin in hyponatremia, substantial renal impairment, polydipsia, or other labeled high-risk states.

  • Type 2B disease requires specialist caution because releasing abnormal VWF can increase platelet aggregation and thrombocytopenia.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify VWD type, prior desmopressin challenge, sodium/fluid plan, VWF activity, factor VIII, and the procedure-specific target.

  • Assess mucosal, menstrual, postoperative, GI, joint, and deep-tissue bleeding; the same laboratory diagnosis can produce different bedside risk.

Interactions

  • SSRIs, antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulants, and NSAIDs can worsen mucosal bleeding.

  • Other hyponatremia-promoting drugs and excess hypotonic fluid magnify desmopressin water retention.

Key Labs

  • VWF antigen and activity, factor VIII activity, CBC/platelets, subtype testing, and desmopressin response

  • Serum sodium and fluid balance when desmopressin is used; peak/trough activity for replacement around major procedures

Nclex Traps

  • VWD is not only a platelet disorder because VWF also stabilizes factor VIII.

  • A normal platelet count does not exclude defective platelet adhesion, and desmopressin does not work when releasable functional stores are absent.
