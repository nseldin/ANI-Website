Definition

Human immunodeficiency virus type 1, abbreviated HIV-1, is an enveloped retrovirus that infects key immune cells, establishes lifelong infection, and without effective treatment can progressively weaken cell-mediated immunity. HIV-1 is the predominant cause of HIV infection worldwide. The virus, HIV infection, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome are related but not identical: AIDS is the advanced clinical stage defined by severe immune suppression or specific opportunistic conditions.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    HIV-1 belongs to the lentivirus group of retroviruses and is distinct from HIV-2. The unqualified term HIV can refer to either type and is therefore kept as a search term rather than a strict identity alias for HIV-1 alone. Viral subtypes and resistance patterns do not create new species cards, but they can change epidemiology, test interpretation, and selection of an effective antiretroviral regimen.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    HIV-1 has two copies of positive-sense RNA that are reverse transcribed into DNA. A host-derived lipid envelope carries viral proteins used for attachment and entry, while a conical capsid encloses the genome and enzymes. Reverse transcriptase lacks high-fidelity proofreading, so replication generates variation rapidly. Integration of viral DNA into host-cell chromosomes creates a durable reservoir that current therapy can suppress but not eradicate.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    People with HIV-1 are the reservoir. Transmission requires infectious blood, semen, vaginal or rectal fluids, or breast milk to reach susceptible mucosa, damaged tissue, or the bloodstream; sexual, injection-equipment, occupational, and perinatal exposures are clinically important. HIV is not spread by ordinary touching, shared dishes, toilets, or casual social contact. Viral suppression with treatment prevents sexual transmission, linking individual care directly to prevention.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Viral envelope proteins bind CD4 and a chemokine coreceptor, permitting fusion and entry into susceptible T lymphocytes and other cells. Reverse-transcribed DNA integrates into the host genome, and ongoing replication plus immune activation gradually depletes and impairs CD4 T cells. As cell-mediated immunity falls, latent pathogens reactivate and organisms ordinarily controlled by healthy immunity can cause severe disease. This mechanism explains why CD4 count predicts particular opportunistic risks while viral load measures active replication.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Acute infection may cause a nonspecific febrile illness or no recognized symptoms, followed by a long clinically quiet phase despite continued viral activity. Untreated progression can cause weight loss, recurrent infection, neurologic disease, malignancy, and AIDS-defining conditions. Because symptoms cannot identify or exclude HIV, testing is essential. Severe dyspnea, focal neurologic deficits, altered mental status, shock, or a suspected opportunistic infection requires urgent evaluation.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Recommended diagnostic algorithms combine antigen-antibody screening with a supplemental differentiation assay and nucleic-acid testing when acute infection or discordant results are possible. The window period means a recent exposure can precede test positivity. After diagnosis, HIV RNA viral load measures treatment response, CD4 count estimates immune function, and resistance testing helps construct an active regimen. A screening result should never be presented as a final diagnosis without its required confirmation pathway.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Standard Precautions and sharps safety protect healthcare workers without stigmatizing patients or adding unnecessary isolation. Prevention includes testing, condoms and other barrier strategies, not sharing injection equipment, preexposure or postexposure prophylaxis when indicated, and prompt treatment. Combination antiretroviral therapy should begin under current guidance and be taken consistently; incomplete or functionally weak therapy permits replication and resistance. Undetectable equals untransmittable applies to sexual transmission when durable viral suppression is maintained.
