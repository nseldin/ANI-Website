Definition

Pneumoconiosis develops when inhaled occupational mineral dust accumulates in lung and triggers chronic inflammation, nodules, and fibrosis according to the specific dust. The characteristic pattern is progressive exertional dyspnea, cough, restrictive or mixed physiology, and exposure-linked imaging abnormalities. Confirm or stage it with detailed lifetime occupational and hobby exposure history; distinguish it from idiopathic fibrosis, sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, chronic infection, and smoking-related disease by exposure, imaging distribution, and testing. The priority is to stop further exposure and strengthen respiratory protection because progressive respiratory failure can complicate Pneumoconiosis. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea, accessory-muscle use, abnormal breath sounds, cough/sputum, chest pain, hypoxemia, cyanosis, fatigue, or confusion.

  • Silent chest, stridor, rising CO2 signs, or exhaustion is more dangerous than a noisy but moving airway.

Diagnostics

  • detailed lifetime occupational and hobby exposure history

  • high-resolution chest CT and pulmonary function with diffusion capacity

  • oxygen, exercise, tuberculosis, autoimmune, and malignancy evaluation according to dust type

  • Distinguish Pneumoconiosis from idiopathic fibrosis, sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, chronic infection, and smoking-related disease by exposure, imaging distribution, and testing.

Labs

  • PaO2 or SpO2 may fall with gas-exchange failure

  • PaCO2 may rise with ventilatory failure

  • WBC may rise with infection

  • lactate may rise with severe hypoxia or sepsis

Treatments

  • stop further exposure and strengthen respiratory protection

  • use vaccination, smoking cessation, oxygen, rehabilitation, and cause-specific symptom care

  • monitor for tuberculosis, autoimmune, cancer, or progressive fibrosis risks specific to the dust

Nursing Priorities

  • Obtain a lifetime job and hobby history with dust type, intensity, duration, protective controls, coworkers, smoking, and symptom timeline because mineral-dust disease may appear years after exposure and the specific dust changes associated risks.

  • Trend cough, sputum, dyspnea, resting and exertional oxygen saturation, breath sounds, pulmonary function, imaging, weight, exercise tolerance, and right-heart findings because progressive fibrosis reduces gas exchange and may cause pulmonary hypertension.

  • Coordinate occupational-medicine referral, exposure elimination, workplace reporting or surveillance, and correctly fitted respiratory protection because treatment cannot remove established scar but stopping inhalation can limit further injury and protect coworkers.

  • Support vaccination, smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation, prescribed oxygen, airway-clearance care when secretions are present, and nutrition because preventing infection and deconditioning preserves limited respiratory reserve.

  • Escalate immediately for severe or rapidly worsening dyspnea, new hypoxemia, hemoptysis, fever with respiratory decline, chest pain or syncope, tuberculosis symptoms, or rapidly increasing edema because acute infection, malignancy, embolism, or right-heart failure may be superimposed.

Complications

  • progressive respiratory failure can complicate Pneumoconiosis.

  • pulmonary hypertension can complicate Pneumoconiosis.

  • tuberculosis can complicate Pneumoconiosis.

  • autoimmune disease can complicate Pneumoconiosis.

  • lung cancer depending on exposure

Red Flags

  • Severe dyspnea, new resting hypoxemia, cyanosis, or respiratory fatigue

  • Hemoptysis, new focal chest pain, unexplained weight loss, or suspicious imaging change

  • Fever, night sweats, productive cough, or known tuberculosis exposure

  • Syncope, jugular venous distention, rapidly increasing edema, or oliguria

Patient Education

  • Teach that symptoms can progress after exposure ends because retained dust continues to drive inflammation, so scheduled lung and occupational follow-up remains important.

  • Explain that respirators supplement engineering dust controls rather than replace them, and a workplace evaluation may prevent the same irreversible disease in others.

Nclex Traps

  • Pneumoconiosis requires an exposure history; imaging alone is not enough

  • Removing exposure prevents more injury but does not erase established fibrosis
