Contraindications

  • Established anuria, severe hypovolemia, severe pulmonary congestion or pulmonary edema, active intracranial bleeding except during craniotomy, and mannitol hypersensitivity are current label contraindications.

  • A solution with crystals must not be infused. Inspect the container, warm to redissolve according to product instructions, and use the required in-line filter because mannitol crystallizes at lower temperatures.

Nursing Essentials

  • Before infusion, document neurologic or ocular target, airway, pressure, cardiac and lung findings, urine output, creatinine/eGFR, sodium, potassium, measured or calculated osmolality, recent osmotherapy, and reliable IV access.

  • Use a dedicated filtered line; do not administer through the same set as blood. Reassess neurologic findings, pupils, pressure target, lungs, oxygenation, hemodynamics, urine response, sodium, osmolality, and kidney function. Stop and escalate pulmonary edema, anuria, progressive osmolality, or neurologic worsening.

Interactions

  • Other nephrotoxins increase acute kidney injury risk. Other diuretics can intensify later volume and electrolyte loss, and sodium-volume change can alter lithium handling.

  • Do not run blood products through the same administration set because product labeling warns of incompatibility or pseudoagglutination concerns. Follow line and filter policy exactly.

Key Labs

  • Urine output, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, sodium, potassium, measured serum osmolality, calculated osmolality and osmolar gap under protocol, pressure, oxygenation, lung examination, fluid balance, and neurologic or ocular endpoint.

  • No single universal osmolality number replaces clinical judgment. Interpret trend, dose timing, renal clearance, osmolar gap, volume state, and response under the local neurocritical-care protocol.

Nclex Traps

  • Mannitol can worsen pulmonary edema despite ultimately increasing urine because its first major effect draws intracellular water into the vascular compartment.

  • Its effect depends on an osmotic gradient and renal filtration. It is not a conventional sodium-transporter blocker and is not appropriate for anuric patients.

Class

Intravenous osmotic diuretic and hyperosmolar agent

Used To Treat

Reduction of elevated intracranial pressure and cerebral edema and reduction of elevated intraocular pressure under current U.S. injection labeling. It is not routine therapy for systemic edema because its initial intravascular expansion can worsen pulmonary congestion.

Mechanism

After IV administration, mannitol remains largely extracellular and increases measured serum osmolality. Across an intact blood-brain or blood-ocular barrier, water moves from tissue toward the hypertonic plasma compartment, reducing intracranial or intraocular volume. Plasma expansion can transiently improve blood rheology but increases preload. At the glomerulus, mannitol is filtered and not meaningfully reabsorbed; increased tubular osmolality reduces water reabsorption in the proximal tubule and descending limb and increases urine flow. As water loss exceeds electrolyte loss, sodium can rise. Excess exposure, renal vasoconstriction, tubular-cell vacuolization described as osmotic nephrosis, and volume depletion can cause acute kidney injury. If mannitol crosses a damaged barrier or accumulates after repeated dosing, tissue osmolality rises and water can move back into the brain when plasma concentration falls. This is why response, renal clearance, osmolality, osmolar gap, hemodynamics, and neurologic findings must be followed together.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Mannitol can cause fatal fluid and electrolyte shifts, pulmonary edema, heart-failure decompensation, acute kidney injury, and neurologic deterioration. Evaluate renal, cardiac, pulmonary, electrolyte, and osmolar status before therapy. Established anuria, severe hypovolemia, severe pulmonary congestion or edema, and active intracranial bleeding except during craniotomy are contraindications under current labeling.

Class Pathway

  • Extracellular osmotic gradient

  • Water shift from brain or eye into plasma

  • Filtered nonreabsorbed osmole producing osmotic diuresis

Description

Mannitol is an IV six-carbon sugar alcohol that acts as an extracellular osmole, raising plasma and tubular osmolality to draw water out of brain or eye tissue and then hold filtered water inside the nephron for excretion. This action lowers brain water or vitreous volume and produces osmotic diuresis. The sequence explains both benefit and danger: water first moves into the circulation, expanding plasma volume and potentially worsening pulmonary edema or heart failure; later renal water loss can cause hypovolemia, hypernatremia, hypotension, and kidney injury. Sodium may initially fall from dilution and later rise as water is excreted. When renal clearance is poor or the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, mannitol can accumulate and weaken or reverse the osmotic gradient, contributing to rebound pressure or edema.

Adverse Effects

  • Initial intravascular expansion can cause hypertension, pulmonary edema, or heart-failure worsening; later diuresis can cause dehydration, hypotension, hypernatremia, hypokalemia, or other electrolyte shifts.

  • Acute kidney injury, osmotic nephrosis, rebound intracranial pressure, headache, nausea, confusion, seizure, infusion reaction, and tissue injury after extravasation can occur.
