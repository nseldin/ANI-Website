Definition

Salicylate toxicity develops when salicylate stimulates respiratory drive and uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, producing early respiratory alkalosis followed by anion-gap acidosis, heat, glucose stress, and cellular toxicity. The characteristic pattern is tinnitus, vomiting, diaphoresis, tachypnea, confusion, fever, mixed alkalosis-acidosis, and a concentration that must be trended with symptoms and timing. Confirm or stage it with serial salicylate concentrations until clearly falling; distinguish it from sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, toxic alcohol exposure, and primary respiratory alkalosis by exposure, level trend, acid-base pattern, and osmolar context. The priority is to give activated charcoal when appropriate and airway-safe because cerebral and pulmonary edema can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • serial salicylate concentrations until clearly falling

  • blood gas, electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, lactate, and electrocardiogram

  • exposure timing, formulation, coingestant, and chronic-versus-acute assessment

  • Distinguish Salicylate toxicity from sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, toxic alcohol exposure, and primary respiratory alkalosis by exposure, level trend, acid-base pattern, and osmolar context.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • give activated charcoal when appropriate and airway-safe

  • alkalinize serum and urine with bicarbonate while correcting potassium and glucose

  • perform hemodialysis for severe neurologic, pulmonary, renal, acid-base, or concentration criteria

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for confusion, seizure, pulmonary edema, severe acidemia, rising level despite therapy, renal failure, or inability to alkalinize because urgent hemodialysis may be required.

  • Contact the poison center or medical toxicologist and obtain serial salicylate levels with acid-base tests because a single early concentration may not show peak or tissue toxicity.

  • Administer ordered intravenous sodium bicarbonate while monitoring serum potassium, blood glucose, urine pH, and blood pH because alkalinization limits salicylate entry into the brain and increases renal elimination.

  • Assess respiratory rate, temperature, tinnitus, vomiting, mental status, pulmonary findings, glucose, and fluid balance to detect cerebral or pulmonary edema and metabolic exhaustion.

  • Trend the disease-specific findings of tinnitus, vomiting, diaphoresis, tachypnea, confusion, fever, mixed alkalosis-acidosis, and a concentration that must be trended with symptoms and timing and escalate immediately for cerebral and pulmonary edema because salicylate stimulates respiratory drive and uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, producing early respiratory alkalosis followed by anion-gap acidosis, heat, glucose stress, and cellular toxicity.

Complications

  • cerebral and pulmonary edema

  • seizure can complicate Salicylate toxicity.

  • coma can complicate Salicylate toxicity.

  • death can complicate Salicylate toxicity.

  • hypoglycorrhachia despite normal serum glucose

  • sudden deterioration after acidemia increases tissue salicylate entry

Red Flags

  • Confusion, seizure, or declining consciousness

  • Pulmonary edema or increasing oxygen requirement

  • Severe acidemia or rising salicylate concentration despite therapy

  • Renal failure or failure to achieve prescribed alkalinization

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to check all pain, cold, and combination products for salicylates because unintentional duplication can cause toxicity.

  • Explain that rapid breathing, ringing in the ears, repeated vomiting, fever, or confusion after exposure requires poison-center or emergency guidance.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not intubate casually; apnea and acidemia can abruptly raise brain salicylate

  • A falling concentration does not reassure if pH and clinical status are worsening
