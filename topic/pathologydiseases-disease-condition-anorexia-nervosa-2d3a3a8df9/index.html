Plain Language

Anorexia nervosa is an eating disorder in which a person persistently restricts food and remains intensely afraid of weight gain or acts in ways that prevent weight restoration, leading to a significantly low weight for that person's age, development, and health. It is not ordinary appetite loss, and severe illness can be hidden by denial, adaptation, or an apparently calm appearance.

Why It Matters

Restriction can slow the heart, lower blood pressure and temperature, disturb electrolytes, weaken bones and muscles, suppress hormones, impair growth and thinking, and become fatal. Treatment also must manage refeeding risk and suicide risk, not simply tell the patient to eat.

Definition

Anorexia nervosa is defined by persistent restriction causing significantly low body weight in context, intense fear of gaining weight or persistent behavior that interferes with weight gain, and disturbed experience of weight or shape, undue influence of weight or shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of low weight. DSM-5-TR identifies restricting and binge-eating/purging subtypes. Atypical anorexia nervosa belongs under OSFED because weight is not significantly low despite the other anorexia pattern and substantial loss.

Pathology

The illness produces adaptive starvation changes rather than one diagnostic tissue lesion. Findings can include loss of fat and muscle, cardiac atrophy and reduced output, bradycardia, orthostatic intolerance, slowed gastrointestinal motility, marrow suppression, endocrine suppression, low bone density, and brain or cognitive changes. Edema may reflect fluid shifts and does not prove adequate nutrition.

Pathophysiology

  • Energy deficiency lowers metabolic rate and alters autonomic, endocrine, gastrointestinal, immune, skeletal, and reproductive function. Malnutrition can intensify rigidity, anxiety, food preoccupation, depressed mood, and impaired insight, which then sustain restriction.

  • Vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, water loading, and driven exercise add dehydration, acid-base, electrolyte, renal, and arrhythmia risk. The binge-eating/purging subtype is still anorexia nervosa when the low-weight anorexia syndrome is present.

  • When nutrition increases after substantial undernutrition, insulin-driven cellular uptake and renewed metabolism can expose phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, thiamine, glucose, sodium, and fluid problems; risk depends on the complete clinical context and trajectory.

Etiology

Anorexia nervosa is multifactorial. Genetic vulnerability, temperament, threat and reward learning, perfectionism or rigidity, puberty, mood or anxiety symptoms, social and sport pressures, trauma, weight stigma, and dieting can interact, but none alone proves cause. Families do not cause anorexia nervosa; involved caregivers can be an important part of treatment.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of an eating disorder, anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, or other psychiatric illness

  • Adolescence or young adulthood, puberty, recent dieting or rapid weight loss, weight-focused sport or occupation, and repeated weight stigma

  • Type 1 diabetes with insulin restriction, gastrointestinal or endocrine illness, pregnancy or postpartum change, food insecurity, and medications that reduce appetite can complicate recognition

  • Any sex, gender, race, ethnicity, and starting body size can be affected; prior higher weight does not protect against severe restriction or medical instability

Signs Symptoms

  • Persistent restriction, skipped meals, narrowing food variety, ritualized eating, calorie or weight preoccupation, avoidance of eating with others, driven exercise, or repeated body checking

  • Fear of weight gain, behavior that prevents restoration, distorted or overvalued weight and shape, or limited recognition of medical seriousness

  • Weight or growth-curve decline, fatigue, cold intolerance, dizziness, fainting, weakness, constipation, early fullness, abdominal discomfort, dry skin, hair change, or reduced concentration

  • Bradycardia, hypotension or orthostatic change, hypothermia, poor perfusion, edema, menstrual or other endocrine suppression, delayed puberty, stress injuries, or low bone density

  • Binge eating, vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, water loading, substance use, depression, anxiety, self-harm, or suicidal thinking may coexist

Diagnostics

  • Establish intake, restriction, weight and growth trajectory, rate and amount of change, fear and behavior around restoration, body-image meaning, bingeing, purging, exercise, fluid manipulation, medicines, supplements, diabetes behavior, duration, and impairment. Obtain collateral history when appropriate because the patient may minimize symptoms without intending deception.

  • Assess temperature, resting and orthostatic pulse and blood pressure, hydration and perfusion, cardiac and neurologic status, muscle strength, abdominal and oral findings, endocrine and menstrual history, growth and puberty, pregnancy possibility, suicide risk, substance use, abuse, and safeguarding.

  • Apply current DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 requirements in context. A low BMI alone is not anorexia nervosa; conversely, a person with an anorexia pattern and dangerous loss at an average or high weight may have atypical anorexia under OSFED and require the same medical-risk assessment.

  • Choose outpatient, day, medical inpatient, or psychiatric crisis care from physiology, trajectory, behavior, mental state, supports, response to outpatient care, and local criteria. Never use one absolute weight or BMI threshold as the sole admission decision.

Labs

  • Common risk-directed studies include complete blood count, glucose, renal and liver measures, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, urinalysis, pregnancy testing, and ECG. Add endocrine, nutrient, bone, or other testing when history and duration support it.

  • Check and trend phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, glucose, fluid balance, cardiac and neurologic findings during nutritional restoration according to refeeding risk and protocol; a normal prefeeding value does not eliminate later shifts.

  • Normal laboratory results do not exclude severe restriction, bradycardia, poor perfusion, low bone density, or need for medical stabilization.

Assessment

Prioritize current medical stability and suicide risk before debating diagnosis or motivation. Compare current weight and vital signs with the person's prior growth or weight trajectory, but do not let weight obscure syncope, chest symptoms, hypothermia, orthostasis, dehydration, weakness, ECG risk, acute refusal, uncontrolled purging, altered mental status, pregnancy, or rapid decline. Determine decision-making capacity for the specific treatment only when relevant and under applicable law.

Differential

Differentiate anorexia nervosa from OSFED atypical anorexia, ARFID, bulimia nervosa, major depression with appetite loss, obsessive-compulsive disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, psychosis, substance use, autism-related feeding needs, and medical causes such as celiac or inflammatory bowel disease, endocrine disease, infection, malignancy, malabsorption, swallowing disorder, medication effect, and food insecurity. Medical disease and anorexia nervosa can coexist.

Treatments

  • Stabilize acute cardiovascular, metabolic, hydration, temperature, glucose, electrolyte, and organ problems. Use individualized nutritional rehabilitation and weight-restoration goals with eating-disorder, medical, dietetic, and nursing expertise; use a protocol that prevents both ongoing underfeeding and refeeding syndrome.

  • Adults should receive an eating-disorder-focused psychotherapy such as CBT-ED, MANTRA, or specialist supportive clinical management according to guideline, preference, access, and formulation. Adolescents and emerging adults with an involved caregiver should be offered eating-disorder-focused family-based treatment when appropriate.

  • Medication is not sole treatment. A specialist may consider adjunctive olanzapine in selected patients to assist weight gain after discussing limited benefit and metabolic, movement, and sedation risks; it does not replace nutrition or psychotherapy. Treat confirmed comorbid illness without assuming starvation-related symptoms will respond normally before nutrition improves.

  • Plan relapse prevention around regular eating, early behavior and vital-sign changes, body-image and anxiety work, school or work return, activity progression, bone and reproductive health, and rapid re-entry to care.

Contraindications

  • Bupropion is contraindicated in a patient with a current or prior diagnosis of anorexia nervosa because seizure risk is higher.

  • Do not use medication, psychotherapy, vitamins, oral contraceptives, or exercise alone as a substitute for adequate nutrition and medical stabilization.

  • Do not prescribe unsupervised rapid caloric escalation to a patient at meaningful refeeding risk; follow the responsible protocol and monitoring plan.

  • Avoid medications that worsen hypotension, bradycardia, electrolyte problems, sedation, seizure risk, or QT risk unless the indication, alternatives, and monitoring have been reviewed.

Safety Considerations

  • A patient may be medically unstable without reporting distress, and a higher-weight patient may be unstable after rapid loss. Appearance and insight are not safety tests.

  • During refeeding, monitor symptoms, vital signs, fluid status, edema, glucose, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, and thiamine needs according to protocol; new dyspnea, weakness, confusion, edema, or arrhythmia requires prompt evaluation.

  • Ask directly about suicide, self-harm, medication or insulin manipulation, vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, water loading, substances, abuse, and access to lethal means.

  • Exercise and bathroom access may need temporary individualized limits for physiologic safety or interruption of compensatory behavior; explain the rationale and use the least restrictive effective plan.

Nursing Priorities

  • Measure and trend prescribed weight, growth, temperature, resting and orthostatic vital signs, hydration, intake and output, edema, cardiac and neurologic findings, bowel function, activity, purging behavior, ECG, and laboratory results using consistent technique and trauma-informed explanation.

  • Administer meals, supplements, thiamine, electrolytes, fluids, and medications exactly as ordered; observe for refeeding complications, covert purging, water loading, excessive activity, or deterioration without shaming or assuming intent.

  • Use calm, matter-of-fact boundaries. Validate fear and distress while keeping the agreed nutrition and safety plan consistent across staff.

  • Coordinate eating-disorder, medical, dietetic, pharmacy, therapy, family, pediatric or obstetric, school, and discharge care; confirm follow-up and an emergency plan before transition.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, hypothermia, severe weakness, reduced consciousness, or ECG abnormality

  • Severe dehydration or electrolyte disturbance, acute food or fluid refusal, rapidly declining trajectory, uncontrolled vomiting or purging, severe abdominal symptoms, or emerging organ failure

  • New edema, dyspnea, muscle weakness, delirium, or arrhythmia after nutrition increases, suggesting possible refeeding complications

  • Suicidal intent, recent serious self-harm, dangerous insulin or medication manipulation, inability to maintain safety, abuse, or a dependent person at risk

  • Pregnancy with dehydration, poor intake, syncope, repeated purging, bleeding, reduced fetal movement, or other obstetric concern

Complications

  • Bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmia, cardiac dysfunction, syncope, hypothermia, dehydration, electrolyte and acid-base disorders, kidney injury, and sudden death

  • Refeeding syndrome, hypoglycemia, edema, thiamine deficiency, delirium, seizure, respiratory or cardiac failure during unsafe nutritional restoration

  • Gastroparesis-like symptoms, constipation, liver-test abnormalities, marrow suppression, muscle loss, endocrine suppression, infertility, low bone density, fractures, delayed puberty, and impaired growth

  • Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, substance use, self-harm, suicide, cognitive slowing, isolation, disability, and relapse

Prognosis

Many patients recover, but course and timing vary and relapse can occur. Earlier specialist care, restoration of adequate nutrition, family or social support, diagnosis-matched psychotherapy, and continued medical monitoring improve outcomes. Weight restoration is necessary when weight is low but does not alone establish cognitive, emotional, behavioral, endocrine, skeletal, or functional recovery.

Prevention

  • Respond early to restriction, rapid loss, growth deviation, compulsive exercise, food rituals, or withdrawal rather than waiting for visibly low weight.

  • Use body-neutral counseling, safe sport and school practices, and careful review before praising weight loss, especially in youth or a person with prior eating-disorder symptoms.

  • Create a relapse plan with the person's earliest eating, exercise, mood, vital-sign, and social warning signs and direct access back to care.

Patient Education

  • Anorexia nervosa is a brain-and-body illness, not vanity or a choice. Malnutrition can make fear, rigidity, low mood, stomach discomfort, and concentration worse.

  • A normal blood test or a calm appearance does not prove your heart or nutrition is safe. Tell the team about fainting, chest symptoms, vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, water loading, exercise, insulin or medicine changes, and the true intake.

  • Nutrition may need to increase under a structured protocol because both continued underfeeding and refeeding complications are dangerous. Do not copy another patient's feeding plan.

  • Seek emergency help for fainting, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, inability to drink, suicidal intent, or new swelling, breathing difficulty, or weakness after feeding increases.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need growth-curve, height, puberty, school, bone, and brain-development assessment. Family-based treatment uses caregivers as recovery supports and does not assign blame.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require coordinated obstetric and eating-disorder care for nutrition, hydration, fetal growth, vomiting or purging, medication, mood, and suicide risk. Fertility can return before cycles appear regular.

  • Men, athletes, older adults, people in larger bodies, and gender-diverse patients are often missed. Muscularity concerns, performance pressures, or prior higher weight do not reduce medical risk.

  • Type 1 diabetes requires urgent review of insulin omission or restriction, glucose and ketone risk, and coordinated diabetes-eating-disorder care; never simplify insulin for weight-control purposes.

Nclex Traps

  • Anorexia nervosa is not the same as the symptom anorexia, which means reduced appetite.

  • Do not use one BMI to diagnose anorexia nervosa, exclude atypical anorexia, or determine admission by itself.

  • Bradycardia can reflect dangerous energy deficiency; do not assume it is athletic conditioning without the full context.

  • Normal electrolytes do not exclude serious restriction or cardiac risk, and prefeeding normal phosphorus does not eliminate later refeeding risk.

  • Medication alone is not treatment; bupropion is contraindicated with current or prior anorexia nervosa.
