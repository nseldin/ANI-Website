Risk Factors

  • Active bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, severe uncontrolled hypertension, recent intracranial or intraspinal surgery or trauma, bleeding diathesis, and high-risk intracranial lesions raise or establish bleeding risk according to the specific drug label and protocol.

  • Anticoagulant exposure, abnormal hemostasis, recent procedures, pregnancy, severe organ dysfunction, or difficult noncompressible punctures require detailed protocol review because bleeding consequences vary.

  • Large established infarct, severe hyperglycemia, uncontrolled pressure, and delayed treatment can reduce benefit or increase hemorrhagic risk depending on imaging and pathway.

Signs Symptoms

  • A sudden disabling focal deficit with hemorrhage excluded is the clinical target, whether the NIHSS is high or low.

  • Improvement before treatment requires expert reassessment because residual disability and re-occlusion risk may persist.

  • After treatment, new severe headache, vomiting, acute hypertension, neurologic decline, angioedema, or systemic bleeding is an emergency.

Diagnostics

  • Record exact LKW, discovery time, baseline function, disabling deficit, NIHSS items, glucose, weight, BP, anticoagulants, procedures, bleeding history, and brain-imaging result.

  • Use NCCT or appropriate MRI to exclude hemorrhage; do not delay otherwise eligible standard-window IVT for advanced imaging.

  • Use DWI-FLAIR or perfusion mismatch for selected unknown-onset or 4.5-to-9-hour pathways because the imaging estimates favorable tissue timing.

  • For guideline-based IVT, lower BP below 185/110 mm Hg before treatment and maintain it below 180/105 mm Hg for at least 24 hours afterward because higher pressure increases hemorrhage risk; follow the current protocol rather than extrapolating these thresholds to every stroke situation.

Labs

  • Check glucose immediately and correct below 60 mg/dL because hypoglycemia is both a mimic and a brain threat.

  • Platelets, INR/aPTT, renal function, and other testing follow exposure and protocol; treatment may proceed before coagulation results in selected patients without recent anticoagulant or heparin use under the label and local pathway.

  • Frequent BP and neurologic assessment are physiologic monitoring, not optional paperwork, because hypertension and early decline can signal hemorrhage risk.

Treatments

  • Administer the protocol-selected alteplase or tenecteplase as rapidly as safely possible for an eligible disabling deficit because delay reduces salvageable tissue.

  • For an eligible early-arriving minor nondisabling noncardioembolic ischemic stroke, use the protocol's short-course DAPT pathway rather than IV thrombolysis because the 2026 guideline finds no thrombolytic benefit for that defined group; first exclude hemorrhage and review antiplatelet contraindications and bleeding risk.

  • Continue parallel EVT evaluation for LVO because IVT should not automatically delay or replace thrombectomy.

  • If serious bleeding or suspected intracranial hemorrhage occurs, stop the relevant infusion when applicable and activate the stroke hemorrhage/reversal pathway because deterioration can be rapid.

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify disabling deficit, last-known-well, glucose, emergency imaging result, blood pressure, anticoagulants, platelets, recent surgery or bleeding, and protocol contraindications because thrombolysis benefit depends on rapid selection while hemorrhage risk remains individualized.

  • Record baseline neurologic findings, weight, vital signs, intravenous access, and all treatment milestones before administering the prescribed thrombolytic because precise dosing and time documentation support safe reperfusion care.

  • Monitor neurologic examination and blood pressure at protocol intervals, airway, oxygenation, headache, nausea, bleeding, and puncture sites because intracranial hemorrhage, angioedema, and systemic bleeding may emerge during or after treatment.

  • Avoid unnecessary invasive procedures, intramuscular injections, and antithrombotic medicines until the post-treatment pathway permits them because accelerated fibrin breakdown makes fresh tissue trauma and overlapping anticoagulation hazardous.

  • Stop the infusion when applicable and escalate immediately for NIH Stroke Scale increase, severe headache, vomiting, acute hypertension, tongue swelling, stridor, hypotension, or major bleeding because symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and orolingual angioedema require emergency imaging and treatment.

Complications

  • Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage is the feared complication because bleeding into injured brain can rapidly expand.

  • Orolingual angioedema, systemic bleeding, access-site bleeding, and rare hypersensitivity can threaten airway or circulation.

  • Failure to recanalize or early re-occlusion can leave the deficit unchanged or worse despite correct treatment.

Contraindications

  • Never give IV thrombolysis for active intracranial hemorrhage.

  • Do not apply a memorized exclusion list without the current drug label and institutional pathway because recommendations and patient context change.

  • Do not use NIHSS alone to define disability or delay standard-window treatment for unnecessary advanced imaging.

Red Flags

  • NIH Stroke Scale increase, new deficit, severe headache, vomiting, or acute hypertension

  • Tongue or lip swelling, stridor, drooling, wheeze, or inability to handle secretions

  • Hemodynamic instability, rapidly falling hemoglobin, hematemesis, melena, or uncontrolled bleeding

  • Reduced consciousness, unequal pupils, seizure, posturing, or acute respiratory compromise

Patient Education

  • Report any new headache, weakness, nausea, mouth swelling, or bleeding immediately because these may be early treatment complications.

  • Do not take aspirin, anticoagulants, or unapproved medicines after thrombolysis because the stroke team must first confirm post-treatment bleeding safety.

Nclex Traps

  • The 2025 TNKase U.S. label states treatment within 3 hours, while the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline endorses alteplase or tenecteplase for eligible patients through 4.5 hours; follow the current protocol rather than blending sources silently.

  • A low NIHSS can still be disabling.

  • Do not delay IVT for thrombectomy transfer or advanced imaging when the standard pathway already establishes eligibility.

Name

Intravenous thrombolysis for acute ischemic stroke

Category

Acute stroke reperfusion pathway

Definition

Intravenous thrombolysis is systemic fibrinolytic treatment intended to dissolve an occluding thrombus and restore cerebral blood flow before threatened tissue becomes infarcted. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline endorses either alteplase or tenecteplase for eligible patients with disabling acute ischemic stroke in the 4.5-hour treatment window and emphasizes rapid treatment regardless of NIHSS score. Selected unknown-onset or 4.5-to-9-hour patients may be treated using advanced mismatch imaging. These are guideline pathways, not a self-screening checklist.

Etiology

IVT treats fibrin-rich arterial occlusion; it does not treat primary intracranial hemorrhage, nonvascular mimic, or established tissue death. Benefit depends on a treatable ischemic mechanism and remaining viable tissue, while harm rises when hemostasis is impaired or fragile injured vessels bleed.

Pathology

Alteplase and tenecteplase convert plasminogen to plasmin, which cleaves fibrin and can reopen an artery. Fibrinolysis cannot distinguish only the pathologic clot from every protective hemostatic plug, so intracranial and systemic bleeding are the central hazards. Reperfusion itself can expose injured microvasculature and produce hemorrhagic transformation.

Pathophysiology

Earlier treatment rescues more penumbra because the infarct core expands with time. A low NIHSS does not establish nondisability; isolated aphasia, hemianopia, severe gait failure, or dominant-hand weakness can be disabling. Conversely, for eligible early-arriving minor nondisabling ischemic stroke, the 2026 guideline prefers short-course dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) rather than IV thrombolysis because trials have not shown thrombolytic benefit in that population. DAPT selection, agents, duration, and start time still require confirmed nonhemorrhagic ischemic stroke, the current protocol, and review of bleeding risk, allergy, current antithrombotics, planned procedures, and other contraindications; this recommendation must not be transferred to a low-NIHSS but disabling deficit or to a mechanism requiring a different prevention strategy. The 2026 guideline regimens are alteplase 0.9 mg/kg up to 90 mg or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg up to 25 mg for eligible disabling AIS through 4.5 hours. The February 2025 U.S. TNKase label indicates adult AIS, uses a weight-banded 0.25 mg/kg regimen with a 25 mg maximum as a single IV bolus over 5 seconds, and states treatment as soon as possible within 3 hours. The professional guideline therefore reaches beyond the FDA-labeled stroke window; this difference is why current institutional protocol and stroke expertise are essential.
