Plain Language

Self-harm is intentional self-poisoning or injury regardless of the person's stated purpose. Some acts include intent to die and others do not, so every episode needs both physical assessment and direct suicide assessment.

Why It Matters

Serious bleeding, poisoning, strangulation injury, head injury, infection, or organ damage may be hidden, and prior self-harm is associated with repetition and suicide. A calm appearance or a nonsuicidal label does not make the episode medically safe.

Definition

Self-harm is intentional self-poisoning or self-injury irrespective of apparent purpose. It is a behavior and clinical presentation rather than a standalone mental-disorder diagnosis. Nonsuicidal self-injury is narrower because death is not intended; a suicide attempt includes at least some intent to die. Intent may be mixed, unclear, or change during an episode. Repetitive stereotyped self-injury associated with some neurodevelopmental conditions requires its own functional and medical assessment and is outside the scope of NICE NG225.

Pathology

Self-harm does not have one disease lesion, biomarker, or motive. An act may temporarily regulate overwhelming emotion, interrupt dissociation, express distress, punish the self, respond to trauma, or enact suicidal intent. Function differs between people and between episodes in the same person, so method, wound appearance, calm behavior, or a previous nonsuicidal label cannot determine current intent or medical severity without direct assessment.

Pathophysiology

  • Injury or poisoning produces method-specific physical harm such as bleeding, infection, tissue, nerve or tendon injury, arrhythmia, seizure, organ failure, aspiration, airway injury, or anoxic brain damage.

  • Short-term relief after an act can negatively reinforce repetition, while pain, shame, concealment, relationship disruption, and fragmented care may increase future vulnerability.

Etiology

Self-harm is multifactorial. Emotional dysregulation, depression, bipolar illness, borderline personality disorder, psychosis, eating or substance-use disorders, trauma, abuse, bullying, conflict, loss, chronic pain, dissociation, isolation, intoxication, and access to medicines or other means may interact. No single diagnosis or motive applies to every act.

Risk Factors

  • Prior self-harm, prior suicide attempt, increasing frequency or lethality, recent discharge, or interrupted follow-up

  • Depression, bipolar illness, borderline personality disorder, psychosis, eating disorder, substance use, trauma, dissociation, pain, or emotional dysregulation

  • Abuse, bullying, conflict, loss, isolation, intoxication, impulsivity, unsafe housing, and access to medicines, weapons, ligatures, or other means

Signs Symptoms

  • Cuts, burns, bites, blunt injury, ligature or strangulation injury, intentionally ingested medicines or chemicals, or another intentional injury

  • Unexplained scars or wounds, concealment, repeated emergency visits, stockpiling medicines, or worsening frequency, severity, or lethality

  • Bleeding, pain, altered consciousness, vomiting, arrhythmia, respiratory symptoms, infection, neurovascular impairment, or a stated or uncertain wish to die

Diagnostics

  • Begin with airway, breathing, circulation, hemorrhage, consciousness, poison exposure, strangulation, head injury, neurovascular or tendon damage, pain, pregnancy, infection, and other immediate physical needs.

  • Conduct physical and psychosocial assessment concurrently when possible and document method, amount, timing, site, expected lethality, stated purpose, intent, planning, rescue likelihood, regret, and current suicide risk.

  • Do not use a risk score to predict suicide or repetition, assign a global low, medium, or high category, or determine treatment or discharge.

Labs

  • There is no universal self-harm laboratory panel or biomarker of intent.

  • Use method- and timing-specific toxicology concentrations, ECG, glucose, electrolytes, renal or hepatic studies, blood gas, pregnancy testing, imaging, wound evaluation, and poison-center guidance as indicated.

  • A normal early result may not exclude evolving poisoning or delayed organ injury; repeat testing and observation follow the exposure, timing, formulation, symptoms, and protocol.

Assessment

After immediate stabilization, ask privately and nonjudgmentally about method, amount, timing, site, expected lethality, intent to die, planning, rescue likelihood, regret, current suicidal thoughts, plan and means, prior acts, triggers and function, substances, abuse and safeguarding, supports, access to medicines, and ability to use a safety plan. Assess pain and physical injury independently of apparent intent and obtain collateral when clinically and legally appropriate.

Differential

  • Suicide attempt, preparatory behavior, self-harm with uncertain or mixed intent, and nonsuicidal self-injury

  • Accidental injury or poisoning, repetitive stereotyped self-injury, body-focused repetitive behavior, and developmentally or neurologically mediated behavior

  • Psychosis, delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, factitious injury, abuse, assault, trafficking, or coercion

Treatments

  • Stabilize poisoning and injury without waiting for psychiatric assessment; provide wound, hemorrhage, antidote, toxicology, surgical, strangulation, neurologic, infection, tetanus, and pain care as indicated.

  • Complete a collaborative psychosocial assessment, care plan, safety plan, lethal-means discussion, and follow-up. Offer adults a structured, person-centered CBT-informed intervention tailored to self-harm.

  • Consider dialectical behaviour therapy adapted for adolescents with frequent self-harm and significant emotional dysregulation. Do not offer a medicine specifically to reduce self-harm; treat confirmed comorbid disorders separately and consider overdose toxicity and available quantity.

Contraindications

  • Do not use punitive, aversive, shaming, dismissive, or withholding approaches and do not delay analgesia, antidote, wound care, or other medical treatment.

  • Do not use risk scales to predict suicide or repetition or to decide treatment, admission, referral, or discharge.

  • Do not provide procedural safer-self-harm instructions or prescribe unnecessarily toxic or excessive quantities without treatment and medicines-access review.

Safety Considerations

  • Reassess wound or poison progression, consciousness, pain, infection, neurovascular function, suicide intent, escalation, medication access, safeguarding, engagement, and follow-up attendance.

  • Prescribing after self-poisoning should consider toxicity, pharmacokinetics, interactions, the person's access to medicines, and limited quantities where clinically appropriate.

  • Maintain privacy and dignity while explaining confidentiality limits, documenting objective findings and information sources, and following local safeguarding and emergency policy.

Nursing Priorities

  • Stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, hemorrhage, consciousness, and method-specific injury; contact poison or toxicology services when indicated.

  • Use private nonjudgmental communication, treat pain and wounds, ask directly about suicide, and assess abuse, exploitation, intoxication, psychosis, and ability to maintain safety.

  • Coordinate psychosocial assessment, safety planning, medicines and means review, safeguarding, warm handoff, wound instructions, and follow-up.

Red Flags

  • Major bleeding, airway or strangulation injury, overdose or unknown ingestion, reduced consciousness, seizure, arrhythmia, severe pain, head injury, or neurovascular compromise

  • Active suicide plan or intent, recent attempt, escalating frequency or lethality, psychosis, severe agitation, intoxication, or withdrawal

  • Unsafe home, abuse, trafficking, coercion, unavailable support, concealed injury, or inability or unwillingness to maintain safety

Complications

  • Hemorrhage, infection, scarring, nerve or tendon injury, organ failure, aspiration, airway injury, anoxic brain injury, disability, and chronic pain

  • Recurrent self-harm, suicide attempt or death, substance-related harm, interrupted treatment, and repeated emergency care

  • Shame, concealment, family conflict, school or work loss, exploitation, and worsening underlying psychiatric or medical illness

Prognosis

Repetition risk is dynamic and cannot be reduced to a score. Recovery is possible and is supported by treating physical harm, understanding the act's function and intent, treating underlying disorders, collaborative safety planning, reducing access to lethal means, caring follow-up, and accessible crisis care. One episode or one stated motive does not determine the future course.

Prevention

  • Develop a practical safety plan that identifies warning signs, coping steps, supportive people, professional contacts, and ways to make the environment safer.

  • Treat underlying mental, substance, pain, trauma-related, developmental, and medical needs and address abuse, bullying, isolation, and barriers to care.

  • Provide timely follow-up and warm transitions after emergency or inpatient care and review access to medicines and other lethal means collaboratively.

Patient Education

  • Seek medical care after poisoning, strangulation, deep injury, persistent bleeding, altered consciousness, chest symptoms, severe pain, or infection signs even when death was not intended.

  • Use the written safety plan and disclose escalating urges, changing methods, suicidal thoughts, or access to means early; treatment is not punishment.

  • In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support and call 911 or go to an emergency department for immediate danger; use appropriate local services elsewhere.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require developmental, family, school, abuse, exploitation, and safeguarding assessment and a youth-appropriate psychological pathway.

  • Autistic people and people with intellectual disability need distinction from repetitive stereotyped self-injury plus communication, sensory, caregiver, and pain-assessment adaptations.

  • Pregnancy, older age, frailty, polypharmacy, custody, trafficking, disability, language, and culture change physical risk, disclosure, support, and treatment planning.

Nclex Traps

  • Treat physical harm before or alongside psychosocial care; stated lack of suicidal intent does not remove the need for direct suicide assessment.

  • No risk score should determine prediction or discharge, and calm behavior does not prove medical or psychiatric safety.

  • There is no medication specifically indicated to stop self-harm; treat the confirmed underlying disorder and consider overdose toxicity and access.
