Range

Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) is a pattern-based test that separates albumin and alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, and gamma globulin fractions. The laboratory may report each fraction and describe or quantify a narrow monoclonal band or M-protein (often called an M-spike), a broad polyclonal pattern, or no suspicious band. Methods, fraction intervals, reporting limits, and specimen effects differ, so use the performing laboratory's interpretation rather than one universal numeric range. Follow the ordered preparation instructions; fasting is not universally required, and medicines should not be stopped unless the clinician directs it.

Why

An abnormal SPEP result signals a changed serum-protein distribution and may be associated with a monoclonal gammopathy, meaning protein made by one clone of plasma or lymphoid cells, or with a polyclonal response from inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, or liver disease; low fractions can reflect impaired liver synthesis, kidney or gastrointestinal protein loss, malnutrition, or immune deficiency. Promptly assess fatigue, pallor, fever or recurrent infection, bleeding, bone or back pain and fracture, weakness or confusion, thirst or dehydration, reduced urine output or edema, neuropathy, weight loss, enlarged nodes or spleen, liver disease, inflammatory symptoms, total protein and albumin, CBC, calcium, creatinine/eGFR, and urine protein. Cause-directed evaluation of a suspicious monoclonal pattern includes serum immunofixation, serum free kappa and lambda light chains with their ratio, quantitative immunoglobulins, and urine electrophoresis or immunofixation when indicated; hematology may add imaging and bone-marrow evaluation after integrating symptoms and organ findings. Escalate urgently for acute kidney injury, symptomatic hypercalcemia, spinal-cord-compression symptoms, severe anemia or bleeding, hyperviscosity-type neurologic or visual symptoms, sepsis, or another unstable organ complication; an M-spike without acute illness needs timely clinician follow-up, not an SPEP-only emergency response. An M-spike does not diagnose multiple myeloma and may reflect monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), an often asymptomatic precursor condition, or another disorder; a normal SPEP does not exclude light-chain or low-level plasma-cell disease, and normal total protein does not exclude a monoclonal protein. Myeloma diagnosis requires the full clinical, laboratory, imaging, and often bone-marrow criteria.

High Causes

  • Monoclonal M-protein from MGUS, multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, AL amyloidosis, or another plasma-cell or lymphoid disorder

  • Broad polyclonal gamma elevation from chronic infection, autoimmune or inflammatory disease, or liver disease

  • Alpha-fraction increases during an acute-phase inflammatory response

  • Relative concentration of albumin or other fractions with dehydration

Low Causes

  • Low albumin from impaired liver synthesis, inflammation, malnutrition or malabsorption, nephrotic kidney loss, protein-losing enteropathy, or burns

  • Low gamma globulins from immune deficiency, immunosuppressive therapy, or protein loss

  • A small or free-light-chain monoclonal process below SPEP detection

Critical Concerns

  • Acute kidney injury, symptomatic hypercalcemia, new weakness or bowel/bladder dysfunction suggesting spinal-cord compression, severe anemia or bleeding, hyperviscosity-type neurologic or visual symptoms, sepsis, or rapid deterioration requires urgent evaluation independent of the SPEP number.

  • A suspicious band needs confirmation and typing with serum immunofixation and serum free light chains; selected contexts also require urine immunofixation.

  • Do not diagnose multiple myeloma from an M-spike, and do not use a normal SPEP or normal total protein to exclude light-chain or low-level plasma-cell disease.

Nursing Considerations

  • Assess bone pain, fracture or cord-compression symptoms, fatigue and anemia, infection, bleeding, calcium-related symptoms, kidney function and urine output, edema or protein loss, neuropathy, liver and inflammatory disease, weight trend, and relevant family or treatment history.

  • Review CBC, calcium, creatinine/eGFR, total protein, albumin, urine protein, immunofixation, serum free light chains and ratio, and quantitative immunoglobulins; prepare for hematology-directed imaging or bone-marrow evaluation when indicated.

  • Compare serial M-protein results using the same method when possible and communicate the laboratory's interpretive comment rather than labeling the pattern as cancer.

Nclex Traps

  • An M-spike identifies a monoclonal-protein pattern, not a multiple-myeloma diagnosis; MGUS and other disorders are possible.

  • A normal SPEP does not exclude light-chain myeloma, AL amyloidosis, or another low-level monoclonal process.

  • Polyclonal elevation commonly reflects inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, or liver disease; low protein fractions may reflect liver synthesis, nutrition, immune deficiency, or protein loss.
