Definition

Human adenovirus is a clinically important virus associated with pharyngitis, pneumonia, conjunctivitis, and gastroenteritis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

Envelope status helps explain environmental stability and susceptibility to routine controls, while genome type shapes replication and diagnostic targets.     Structure and traits

  • Content

    Hand hygiene, environmental cleaning, respiratory and contact controls, and safe swimming-water practices reduce spread; vaccine use is limited to specific populations. The reviewed route description is contact transmission. Reviewed intervals differ across respiratory, ocular, and gastrointestinal adenovirus syndromes. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Adenoviruses infect epithelial cells at respiratory, ocular, and gastrointestinal sites; tissue injury and inflammation vary by viral type and host. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Human adenovirus is associated with pharyngitis, pneumonia, conjunctivitis, and gastroenteritis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Nucleic-acid or antigen testing can support diagnosis, but results require clinical context because shedding may continue after acute illness. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
