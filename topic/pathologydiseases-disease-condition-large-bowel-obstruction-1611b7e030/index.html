Definition

Large bowel obstruction develops when a tumor, volvulus, stricture, fecal impaction, or other lesion blocks colonic transit, progressively distending bowel and threatening perfusion. The characteristic pattern is abdominal distention, cramping, obstipation, vomiting later in the course, and dilated colon with a transition point. Confirm or stage it with CT abdomen and pelvis to locate cause, ischemia, perforation, and cecal size; distinguish it from ileus, toxic megacolon, acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, and small-bowel obstruction by transition point, inflammation, and dilation pattern. The priority is to resuscitate, correct electrolytes, rest and decompress the bowel because cecal ischemia can complicate Large bowel obstruction. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Abdominal pain pattern, distention, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea or constipation, stool/emesis blood, jaundice, dysphagia, poor intake, dehydration, or guarding are the useful GI clues.

  • Escalate rigid abdomen, peritonitis signs, GI bleeding, shock, severe dehydration, fever with jaundice, or worsening mental status.

Diagnostics

  • CT abdomen and pelvis to locate cause, ischemia, perforation, and cecal size

  • CBC, electrolytes, lactate, renal function, and serial examination

  • endoscopic evaluation or contrast study only when stable and therapeutically useful

  • Distinguish Large bowel obstruction from ileus, toxic megacolon, acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, and small-bowel obstruction by transition point, inflammation, and dilation pattern.

Labs

  • hemoglobin may fall with bleeding

  • AST/ALT and bilirubin may rise with hepatic/biliary injury

  • lipase rises in pancreatitis

  • albumin and INR reflect liver synthetic function

Treatments

  • resuscitate, correct electrolytes, rest and decompress the bowel

  • obtain early surgical consultation

  • use endoscopic detorsion or stenting only for selected causes without perforation or ischemia

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for peritoneal signs, fever, tachycardia, rapidly increasing distention, rising lactate, shock, or free air because perforation or strangulation requires emergency intervention.

  • Keep the patient nothing by mouth and establish intravenous access because progressive colonic distention can cause dehydration, ischemia, and perforation.

  • Assess abdominal distention, pain, stool and flatus passage, vomiting, rectal bleeding, and cancer or volvulus history because the cause determines decompression or surgery.

  • Avoid routine laxatives, enemas, or bowel preparation until obstruction and perforation are excluded because added volume or pressure can worsen dilation.

  • Monitor abdominal girth, tenderness, bowel sounds, urine output, electrolytes, white blood cell count, lactate, and imaging progression to detect ischemia.

Complications

  • cecal ischemia can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

  • perforation can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

  • fecal peritonitis can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

  • sepsis can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

  • aspiration can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

  • kidney injury can complicate Large bowel obstruction.

Red Flags

  • Peritoneal signs or free intraperitoneal air

  • Rapidly increasing abdominal distention

  • Fever, tachycardia, rising lactate, or shock

  • Severe continuous pain or new rectal bleeding

Patient Education

  • Teach the patient to seek care for progressive distention, vomiting, severe pain, or inability to pass both stool and gas.

  • Explain why laxatives or bowel preparations should not be self-started when a mechanical obstruction is possible.

Nclex Traps

  • Passing liquid stool does not exclude partial obstruction

  • Do not give stimulant laxatives when mechanical obstruction or perforation is suspected
