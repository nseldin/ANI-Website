Definition

Miosis means pupillary constriction: one or both pupils are smaller than expected for the lighting and the person's baseline.

Plain Language

Miosis means an unusually small or constricted pupil.

Why It Matters

Miosis can be a normal response to light, but it may also accompany opioid exposure, cholinergic poisoning, Horner syndrome, eye medicines, or a pontine brain injury. Breathing and consciousness, not pupil size alone, determine opioid urgency, and absence of pinpoint pupils does not exclude overdose.

Associated Conditions

  • normal bright-light or near response, aging, opioid or clonidine effect, cholinergic or organophosphate toxicity, Horner syndrome, pontine injury, inflammation, or miotic eye medication

Assessment Method

Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document symmetry and light and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines and exposures, and assess eyelids, eye movements, vision, mental status, respiratory rate and ventilation, secretions, sweating, bowel activity, weakness, and the neurologic examination.

Positive Finding

One or both pupils are smaller than expected for the lighting and baseline and may fail to dilate normally in darkness; pathologic miosis may be unilateral or bilateral.

Mechanism

Excess parasympathetic activation of the iris sphincter, loss of sympathetic input to the iris dilator, direct drug action, or damage to central sympathetic pathways shifts the pupil toward constriction.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Miosis finding raises concern for normal bright-light or near response, aging, opioid or clonidine effect, cholinergic or organophosphate toxicity, Horner syndrome, pontine injury, inflammation, or miotic eye medication because excess parasympathetic activation of the iris sphincter, loss of sympathetic input to the iris dilator, direct drug action, or damage to central sympathetic pathways shifts the pupil toward constriction. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Miosis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Miosis to focus evaluation of normal bright-light or near response, aging, opioid or clonidine effect, cholinergic or organophosphate toxicity, Horner syndrome, pontine injury, inflammation, or miotic eye medication and the reviewed alternatives: normal light response, Horner syndrome, opioid effect, cholinergic toxicity, miotic eye drops, pontine lesion. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document symmetry and light and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines and exposures, and assess eyelids, eye movements, vision, mental status, respiratory rate and ventilation, secretions, sweating, bowel activity, weakness, and the neurologic examination. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Miosis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

There is no single abnormal diameter for every age and lighting condition; normal near focus, bright light, older age, dark irises, prior surgery, and eye drops affect appearance, while hypoxia or mixed ingestion can remove the expected miosis and absence does not exclude opioid toxicity. Therefore, Miosis cannot confirm or exclude normal bright-light or near response, aging, opioid or clonidine effect, cholinergic or organophosphate toxicity, Horner syndrome, pontine injury, inflammation, or miotic eye medication by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • normal light response

  • Horner syndrome

  • opioid effect

  • cholinergic toxicity

  • miotic eye drops

  • pontine lesion

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as normal light response, Horner syndrome, opioid effect, cholinergic toxicity, miotic eye drops, pontine lesion.

False Negative Situations

Miosis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of normal bright-light or near response, aging, opioid or clonidine effect, cholinergic or organophosphate toxicity, Horner syndrome, pontine injury, inflammation, or miotic eye medication.

Modern Status

Current descriptive finding, not proof of one toxidrome or brain lesion; breathing, consciousness, secretions, weakness, symmetry, reactivity, and associated neurologic signs determine urgency. Safety Precautions

Observe Miosis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Measure both pupils in bright and dim light while the patient fixates at distance, document symmetry and light and near responses, review eye drops, patches, medicines and exposures, and assess eyelids, eye movements, vision, mental status, respiratory rate and ventilation, secretions, sweating, bowel activity, weakness, and the neurologic examination. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not wait for pinpoint pupils before treating respiratory depression; support ventilation and give cause-directed opioid reversal when indicated, protect staff and decontaminate suspected organophosphate exposure, and urgently evaluate sudden unilateral miosis with ptosis or neck pain, new neurologic deficit, coma, or rapidly worsening weakness and secretions.
