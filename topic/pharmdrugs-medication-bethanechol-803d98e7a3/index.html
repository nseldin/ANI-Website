Contraindications

  • Mechanical bladder-neck, urethral or GI obstruction; questionable wall integrity; recent risky anastomosis

  • Asthma, active peptic ulcer, pronounced bradycardia/hypotension, coronary disease or marked vagotonia

  • Hyperthyroidism, epilepsy, parkinsonism, peritonitis and acute inflammatory GI lesions under labeling

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm a distended hypotonic bladder with an open outlet; acute painful retention often needs catheterization before medication.

  • Give on an empty stomach, commonly 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals, because food increases nausea/vomiting.

Interactions

  • Atropine and other antimuscarinics oppose bethanechol.

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors and other cholinergics intensify toxicity.

  • Ganglion blockers can produce a critical blood-pressure fall when combined.

Key Labs

  • Bladder scan/post-void residual and urine output

  • Blood pressure, pulse and respiratory assessment

  • Renal function and obstruction/hydronephrosis evaluation

Nclex Traps

  • Bethanechol contracts detrusor; it does not treat BPH obstruction.

  • Diarrhea, wheeze and bradycardia are one coherent muscarinic toxidrome.

  • Food timing is for tolerability, not because the drug treats a GI infection.

Used To Treat

Selected acute postoperative or postpartum and neurogenic urinary retention when the bladder is hypotonic and no mechanical obstruction is present.

Mechanism

Activation of M3 receptors couples Gq to phospholipase C, IP3 formation, calcium release and smooth-muscle contraction in the bladder detrusor and bowel. Detrusor contraction raises intravesical pressure and promotes voiding only when the outlet is open; the same muscarinic signaling can constrict airway smooth muscle and cause bronchospasm. M2 cardiac and glandular effects contribute bradycardia, hypotension, salivation and sweating. Because bethanechol is not rapidly hydrolyzed by acetylcholinesterase, effects outlast acetylcholine. It has negligible useful nicotinic action and does not relax a prostate, urethral stricture or stone.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Exclude mechanical urinary or GI obstruction and protect recently operated or structurally weak bladder/bowel. Cholinergic toxicity can cause bronchospasm, bradycardia, hypotension, diarrhea, vomiting, sweating and salivation. Atropine antagonizes severe muscarinic toxicity.

Adverse Effects

  • Abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and fecal urgency

  • Sweating, salivation, flushing, headache and miosis

  • Bradycardia, hypotension and syncope

  • Bronchospasm, wheezing and urinary urgency

Required Monitoring

  • Voiding response, post-void residual when ordered, abdominal distention/pain, blood pressure, heart rate, lung sounds, bowel effects, and cholinergic-excess symptoms

Patient Education

  • Take exactly as prescribed and do not self-treat urinary retention without ruling out obstruction.

  • Report wheezing, trouble breathing, fainting, very slow heartbeat, severe cramps, diarrhea, or heavy sweating/salivation.

  • Avoid assuming more is better. Too much muscarinic stimulation can become dangerous.

Description

Bethanechol is a direct muscarinic agonist that contracts the bladder detrusor to promote urination in selected nonobstructive urinary retention. It also increases GI motility by reproducing parasympathetic acetylcholine signaling; its beta-methyl group favors muscarinic over nicotinic activity, and its carbamate resists acetylcholinesterase. It is appropriate only when the outlet is open: forcing a bladder to contract against an obstruction can raise pressure, reflux urine, injure the wall and delay needed decompression.
