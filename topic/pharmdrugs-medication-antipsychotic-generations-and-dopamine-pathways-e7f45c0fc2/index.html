Used To Treat

Schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis, selected bipolar episodes, severe agitation, and product-specific adjunctive or pediatric indications. Generation describes pharmacology and adverse-effect tendencies; it does not rank clinical quality or guarantee tolerability.

Description

Antipsychotics reduce psychosis mainly by lowering excessive mesolimbic dopamine signaling, but dopamine is also required in motor, endocrine, reward, and frontal-cortical circuits. First-generation drugs are defined by strong dopamine D2 antagonism. Most second-generation drugs combine D2 antagonism with serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism, while aripiprazole-family agents partially stimulate D2 receptors. The useful and harmful effects therefore depend on which pathway is being changed, how tightly and how long D2 is occupied, and what additional histamine, muscarinic, adrenergic, and serotonin receptors the drug affects.

Mechanism

Mesolimbic D2 blockade reduces positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions. The same blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway removes dopamine's restraint on acetylcholine and can cause dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, and tardive dyskinesia. Tuberoinfundibular D2 blockade removes dopamine's inhibition of prolactin release, causing hyperprolactinemia. Excessive D2 blockade in mesocortical circuits may worsen motivation or cognition in some patients. 5-HT2A antagonism can disinhibit dopamine release in striatal and tuberoinfundibular pathways, which helps explain the generally lower EPS/prolactin burden of many second-generation drugs. Partial D2 agonists reduce signaling where dopamine is high but preserve some signaling where it is low; this lowers prolactin risk but often produces akathisia.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING: Elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotic drugs have increased mortality, commonly from cardiovascular or infectious causes; these agents is not approved for dementia-related psychosis. All classes can cause neuroleptic malignant syndrome, tardive dyskinesia, orthostasis, falls, metabolic or cardiac toxicity, and temperature dysregulation. Drug-specific risk is more useful than the word atypical.

Adverse Effects

  • D2 pathway effects: acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia and hyperprolactinemia

  • H1/M1/alpha-1 effects: sedation, weight gain, anticholinergic toxicity and orthostatic hypotension

  • Metabolic syndrome, QT prolongation, seizures, neutropenia or myocarditis depending on the agent

  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability and altered mental status

Contraindications

  • Agent-specific hypersensitivity and major cardiac, hematologic, metabolic or neurologic contraindications

  • Do not use generation labels as a substitute for QT, ANC, metabolic, pregnancy or interaction assessment

  • Avoid uncritical antipsychotic use for dementia behaviors because mortality and cerebrovascular risks are increased

Nursing Essentials

  • Establish target symptoms and baseline AIMS movement exam, weight/waist, blood pressure, glucose/A1c, lipids, ECG risk and prolactin symptoms before treatment.

  • Name the pathway when symptoms appear: neck spasm is acute dystonia; inner restlessness is akathisia; shuffling rigidity is parkinsonism; late choreiform movements suggest tardive dyskinesia.

Interactions

  • Dopamine agonists oppose antipsychotic effects; multiple dopamine blockers raise EPS and NMS risk.

  • Sedatives, antihypertensives, anticholinergics and QT-prolonging drugs amplify receptor-based toxicity.

  • CYP1A2, CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 interactions vary substantially by agent and smoking status.

Key Labs

  • Weight/BMI/waist, blood pressure, fasting glucose or A1c and fasting lipids

  • AIMS and focused EPS assessment

  • ECG, electrolytes, prolactin, CBC/ANC or drug levels when agent and symptoms require

Nclex Traps

  • Atypical does not mean free of EPS, tardive dyskinesia, NMS or dementia mortality.

  • Treating akathisia as anxiety and increasing the antipsychotic can worsen the problem.

  • Anticholinergics may help acute dystonia or parkinsonism but do not treat tardive dyskinesia and can worsen cognition.
