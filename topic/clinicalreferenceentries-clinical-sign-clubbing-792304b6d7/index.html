Definition

Clubbing is an observed change in which the ends of fingers or toes become enlarged and rounded while the nails curve more than usual. It is a physical sign, not a disease.

Plain Language

The fingertips become bulb-shaped and the nails wrap more sharply over them.

Why It Matters

New clubbing can be an early clue to serious lung, heart, infectious, bowel, or liver disease, including lung cancer, cystic fibrosis, and bronchiectasis. The finding does not identify the cause by itself, so the patient's symptoms and cause-directed evaluation remain essential.

Associated Conditions

  • acquired pulmonary, cardiovascular, infectious, gastrointestinal, hepatic, and neoplastic disorders, or less often a benign familial pattern

Assessment Method

Inspect fingers and toes from the side and above, palpate nail-bed firmness, compare both sides and prior baseline, assess the nail-fold profile angle and phalangeal depth, and oppose matching dorsal distal fingers for the Schamroth window; ask about onset, family history, dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, fever, chest pain, cyanosis, weight loss, bowel symptoms, and joint or bone pain.

Positive Finding

The distal digits are bulbously enlarged with increased nail curvature, a spongy nail bed, loss of the usual nail-fold angle or a profile angle greater than 180 degrees, and often loss of the normal diamond-shaped Schamroth window.

Mechanism

The mechanism remains incompletely established; a leading model proposes that megakaryocytes or platelets bypass normal pulmonary filtering, lodge distally, and release vascular and connective-tissue growth mediators such as VEGF and PDGF.

Evidence Limitations

Mild clubbing is subjective, measurements and the Schamroth window are imperfect, normal variants and nail disease can mimic it, and absence of clubbing does not exclude serious cardiopulmonary disease. Therefore, Clubbing cannot confirm or exclude acquired pulmonary, cardiovascular, infectious, gastrointestinal, hepatic, and neoplastic disorders, or less often a benign familial pattern by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • normal increased nail curvature

  • pseudoclubbing from acro-osteolysis

  • onychomycosis

  • acromegaly

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as normal increased nail curvature, pseudoclubbing from acro-osteolysis, onychomycosis, acromegaly.

False Negative Situations

Clubbing may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of acquired pulmonary, cardiovascular, infectious, gastrointestinal, hepatic, and neoplastic disorders, or less often a benign familial pattern.

Modern Status

Current physical sign that warrants evaluation when newly acquired; it is an association, not a diagnosis, and familial or idiopathic clubbing can occur.
