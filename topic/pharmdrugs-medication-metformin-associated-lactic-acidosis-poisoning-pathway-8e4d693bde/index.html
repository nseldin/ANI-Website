Used To Treat

Acute overdose or therapeutic accumulation with severe lactic acidosis, shock, kidney failure, hypothermia, altered consciousness, or multiorgan dysfunction in a patient exposed to metformin.

Description

Metformin is a biguanide glucose-lowering drug whose accumulation inhibits mitochondrial respiratory-chain complex I, promoting lactate production and potentially causing profound acidosis and shock. Sepsis, shock, hypoxia, seizure, liver failure, mesenteric ischemia, and other illnesses can raise lactate and also reduce kidney clearance, causing metformin accumulation. Both processes often reinforce each other. In a large acute overdose, metformin itself can drive profound acidosis even before kidney failure. Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, tachypnea, hypothermia, hypotension, confusion, and rising lactate can progress to refractory vasoplegic shock and cardiac arrest. Metformin concentrations are rarely available fast enough to determine initial treatment. Rescue stops the drug, treats every reversible cause of shock and hypoxia, supports ventilation and circulation, follows pH and lactate closely, and starts extracorporeal treatment early when EXTRIP severe criteria are met. Hemodialysis removes metformin and lactate and provides bicarbonate-buffered correction, but recurrence can occur after an initial session because tissue metformin redistributes.

Mechanism

Metformin lowers hepatic glucose output and alters mitochondrial energy metabolism, including inhibition of respiratory-chain complex I. In excessive exposure or impaired clearance, reduced oxidative phosphorylation shifts pyruvate toward lactate, while hepatic lactate utilization and gluconeogenesis cannot keep pace. Acidemia then weakens myocardial contractility, reduces catecholamine responsiveness, promotes vasodilation, and worsens organ perfusion, which produces still more lactate. Kidney failure both permits metformin accumulation and limits acid removal. Supportive treatment restores oxygen delivery and treats other lactate sources, while intermittent hemodialysis removes the small, minimally protein-bound drug, clears lactate, and supplies bicarbonate. Because metformin has a large apparent tissue distribution, post-dialysis redistribution explains why serial lactate and pH monitoring and repeat treatment may be needed.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for rapid deep breathing, confusion, fainting, severe weakness, hypothermia, shock, or a substantial overdose. Stop metformin, check glucose, blood gas, lactate, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, ECG, and evaluate urgently for sepsis, hypoxia, ischemia, seizure, and co-ingestants.

  • Support airway and ventilation without erasing compensatory minute ventilation during a poorly planned intubation; resuscitate perfusion, use vasopressors for persistent shock, and treat the underlying illness. Bicarbonate may be a bridge in selected profound acidemia but adds sodium and carbon dioxide and cannot remove metformin.

  • Contact nephrology and toxicology early. EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment for lactate above 20 mmol/L, pH at or below 7.0, or failure of standard support, and suggests it at somewhat less extreme values; shock, kidney impairment, liver failure, or decreased consciousness lower the treatment threshold.

Nursing Essentials

  • Clarify acute ingestion versus therapeutic use, formulation, amount, last dose, kidney trend, dehydration, vomiting or diarrhea, infection symptoms, contrast or procedure history, alcohol, liver disease, heart failure, and every glucose-lowering co-medication.

  • Trend mental status, core temperature, respiratory rate and minute ventilation, end-tidal carbon dioxide when available, blood pressure, perfusion, ECG, glucose, urine output, lactate, pH, bicarbonate, potassium, and ionized calcium. Document trajectory rather than isolated values.

  • Coordinate dialysis access and timed pre-, intra-, and post-treatment samples. Continue frequent pH and lactate checks after apparent correction because redistribution or an untreated shock source can produce rebound.

Key Labs

  • Serial arterial or venous blood gas, lactate, electrolytes, bicarbonate, anion gap, glucose, creatinine and urea, liver studies, CBC and cultures when infection is possible, ketones, salicylate and acetaminophen levels in unknown overdose, pregnancy testing, and ECG.

  • A metformin concentration can confirm exposure but often returns too late and correlates imperfectly with severity. Falling lactate after dialysis is reassuring only when pH, perfusion, temperature, kidney function, and the underlying cause improve together.

Adverse Effects

  • Severe MALA can cause profound acidemia, vasoplegic and cardiogenic shock, hypothermia, dysrhythmia, respiratory fatigue, kidney and liver injury, altered consciousness, pancreatitis, cardiac arrest, and death.

  • Bicarbonate can cause hypernatremia, volume overload, reduced ionized calcium, and excess carbon dioxide; intubation can precipitate arrest if compensatory ventilation is lost; dialysis can cause hypotension, access complications, and electrolyte shifts.

Contraindications

  • Do not label every lactate elevation in a metformin user as isolated drug toxicity; missing sepsis, ischemia, hypoxia, or shock leaves the lactate source untreated. Do not wait for a metformin level before treating severe physiology.

  • Do not rely on bicarbonate alone when EXTRIP criteria indicate extracorporeal treatment, and do not abruptly reduce compensatory ventilation during airway management without matching the patient's required minute ventilation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Severe or worsening acidemia, lactate approaching EXTRIP thresholds, shock, kidney or liver failure, hypothermia, decreased consciousness, or failure of standard support requires ICU, nephrology, toxicology, and immediate hemodialysis-capable care. Repeat treatment may be needed until lactate is below 3 mmol/L and pH above 7.35 with sustained clinical improvement.

Interactions

  • Kidney injury, dehydration, sepsis, hypoxia, heart failure, liver failure, alcohol excess, and some nephrotoxic drugs increase accumulation or lactate risk. Contrast exposure is a context for kidney reassessment, not proof of causation.

  • Insulin or sulfonylurea co-exposure can cause hypoglycemia, while metformin alone usually does not; check glucose serially because recurrent hypoglycemia points to a co-exposure or another process that requires separate treatment.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Kidney disease

    Note

    Reduced elimination is a central risk for therapeutic accumulation and lowers the threshold for early extracorporeal treatment.

  • Population

    Older or acutely ill adults

    Note

    Sepsis, dehydration, heart failure, and polypharmacy can convert stable therapy into combined illness- and drug-associated acidosis.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal shock and acidemia threaten both patients; stabilize and dialyze when indicated while coordinating obstetric assessment.

Evidence Limitations

  • EXTRIP thresholds are structured recommendations based largely on low-quality evidence and cannot separate metformin-induced from metformin-associated lactic acidosis. Treatment decisions combine physiology, trajectory, comorbid illness, and dialysis feasibility.

Nclex Traps

  • A patient taking metformin can have sepsis or ischemia as an additional lactate source.

  • Bicarbonate changes pH temporarily but does not remove metformin from the body.

  • After dialysis, tissue redistribution can cause rebound, so lactate and pH monitoring must continue.

Regulatory Status

There is no specific antidote. Bicarbonate and extracorporeal treatment are severity- and protocol-directed; EXTRIP provides lactate, pH, support-failure, and comorbidity criteria for dialysis.

Boxed Warning

METFORMIN-LACTIC-ACIDOSIS WARNING: call 911 for rapid deep breathing, confusion, hypothermia, fainting, or shock. Treat sepsis, hypoxia, ischemia, and other lactate sources while stopping metformin; preserve compensatory ventilation and arrange early hemodialysis when EXTRIP pH, lactate, shock, or organ-failure criteria are met.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
