Definition

Mitral regurgitation develops when systolic backflow from the left ventricle into the left atrium results from leaflet, chordal, papillary-muscle, annular, or ventricular disease. The characteristic pattern is a holosystolic apical murmur radiating toward the axilla, dyspnea, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary edema, or an acute soft murmur with shock. Confirm or stage it with transthoracic echocardiography for mechanism, severity, chamber response, and pulmonary pressure; distinguish it from tricuspid regurgitation, ventricular septal defect, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and flow murmurs by location, maneuvers, and echocardiography. The priority is to stabilize acute pulmonary edema and afterload when appropriate because acute pulmonary edema can complicate Mitral regurgitation. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, hypotension, weak/absent pulses, cool skin, altered mentation, or low urine output depending on the perfusion problem.

  • Worsening trend matters: new ECG change, rising troponin/BNP, escalating oxygen need, or falling blood pressure changes priority.

Diagnostics

  • transthoracic echocardiography for mechanism, severity, chamber response, and pulmonary pressure

  • transesophageal echo for unclear anatomy or procedural planning

  • serial symptoms, ventricular size and function, rhythm, and exercise response

  • Distinguish Mitral regurgitation from tricuspid regurgitation, ventricular septal defect, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and flow murmurs by location, maneuvers, and echocardiography.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • stabilize acute pulmonary edema and afterload when appropriate

  • repair or replace severe primary disease at the correct symptom and ventricular threshold

  • treat ventricular heart failure and consider transcatheter therapy for selected secondary disease

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for acute severe dyspnea, new pulmonary edema, hypotension, syncope, rapid atrial fibrillation, new chest pain, or abrupt murmur change after infarction or infection because acute severe regurgitation may require urgent intervention.

  • Assess exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, fatigue, palpitations, edema, murmur change, and activity tolerance because increasing regurgitant volume raises left-atrial pressure and can progress before resting symptoms seem dramatic.

  • Trend heart rate, rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation, lung sounds, daily weight, edema, and urine output because atrial fibrillation, pulmonary congestion, and falling forward output are common routes to decompensation.

  • Administer prescribed diuretics and afterload or rhythm therapy while monitoring renal function, potassium, pressure, and symptoms because relieving congestion helps breathing but excessive volume removal can reduce perfusion.

  • Coordinate scheduled echocardiography and valve-team follow-up because ventricular size and function, pulmonary pressure, and valve anatomy determine when repair is safer than waiting for irreversible dysfunction.

Complications

  • acute pulmonary edema can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

  • cardiogenic shock can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

  • atrial fibrillation can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

  • stroke can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

  • pulmonary hypertension can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

  • progressive ventricular failure can complicate Mitral regurgitation.

Red Flags

  • Sudden pulmonary edema or rapidly increasing oxygen need

  • Hypotension, cool skin, confusion, or falling urine output

  • New rapid atrial fibrillation, syncope, or sustained ventricular rhythm

  • Abrupt murmur or hemodynamic change after infarction, trauma, or infection

Patient Education

  • Teach daily weight and symptom tracking because a rising weight, new orthopnea, or declining walking tolerance may reveal congestion before severe distress develops.

  • Explain why scheduled echocardiograms matter even when symptoms are mild: the ventricle can compensate for leakage until structural damage is harder to reverse.

Nclex Traps

  • Acute severe regurgitation may have a short or quiet murmur because pressures equalize rapidly

  • A normal ejection fraction can conceal impaired forward output
