Used To Treat

Postmeal glucose excursions in selected patients with type 2 diabetes; pramlintide is also used with mealtime insulin in selected type 1 diabetes. The therapies act at different points: carbohydrate breakdown, gastric delivery/glucagon, endogenous insulin release, or direct insulin replacement.

Description

Acarbose is an intestinal alpha-glucosidase enzyme inhibitor that delays conversion of complex carbohydrate and sucrose into absorbable monosaccharides; it must meet the meal at the first bite. Pramlintide is an injected amylin analog given before major meals with mealtime insulin; it slows gastric emptying, suppresses meal-related glucagon, and increases satiety. Repaglinide briefly stimulates endogenous insulin before a meal. Lispro supplies rapid exogenous insulin. Knowing the location of action explains why each has different timing, hypoglycemia, and GI rules.

Mechanism

Brush-border alpha-glucosidases cleave oligosaccharides and disaccharides to glucose; acarbose competitively inhibits these enzymes and pancreatic alpha-amylase, spreading absorption later. Unabsorbed carbohydrate reaches colonic bacteria, producing gas and diarrhea. Amylin is normally cosecreted with insulin from beta cells; pramlintide activates amylin receptors in hindbrain and vagal circuits, slowing gastric emptying, suppressing postmeal glucagon, and signaling satiety. Repaglinide closes beta-cell SUR1/KATP channels, while lispro activates insulin receptors after rapid absorption. Acarbose alone does not force insulin, but pramlintide plus insulin can create severe early postmeal hypoglycemia if insulin is not reduced.

Boxed Warning

Pramlintide BOXED WARNING: severe hypoglycemia can occur within about 3 hours when combined with mealtime insulin, especially in type 1 diabetes; careful selection, education, glucose monitoring, and initial mealtime-insulin reduction are required. Acarbose has no boxed warning but requires dextrose, not sucrose, to treat combination-therapy hypoglycemia.

Adverse Effects

  • Acarbose: flatulence, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, and dose-related transaminase elevation

  • Pramlintide: nausea, anorexia, delayed gastric emptying, injection burden, and severe insulin-associated hypoglycemia

  • Repaglinide/lispro: meal-mismatch hypoglycemia and weight gain

Contraindications

  • Acarbose: inflammatory bowel disease, colonic ulceration, obstruction risk, major malabsorption, cirrhosis, or severe renal dysfunction under labeling.

  • Pramlintide: confirmed gastroparesis or hypoglycemia unawareness; avoid in patients unable to follow insulin and glucose instructions.

  • Do not mix pramlintide and insulin in the same syringe.

Nursing Essentials

  • Ask where the meal is: no meal means no meal-linked repaglinide or pramlintide dose under the prescribed plan, while insulin decisions require individualized sick-day guidance.

  • A patient on acarbose who becomes hypoglycemic from insulin needs glucose tablets/gel or dextrose because acarbose blocks sucrose breakdown.

Interactions

  • Acarbose can alter digoxin exposure and is antagonized by digestive-enzyme/adsorbent products.

  • Pramlintide delays absorption of time-critical oral drugs; separate them according to labeling.

  • Any added meal-lowering therapy can require mealtime-insulin or secretagogue reduction.

Key Labs

  • Postmeal glucose/CGM and A1c

  • Liver tests during higher-dose acarbose therapy and renal/GI assessment

  • Hypoglycemia frequency, meal size, insulin dose, gastric symptoms, and weight

Nclex Traps

  • Table sugar is not the preferred rescue while acarbose is blocking sucrose digestion; use dextrose.

  • Pramlintide is not insulin and cannot replace basal or meal insulin.

  • A dose timed to food should not be given automatically when the tray is absent or the patient is vomiting.
