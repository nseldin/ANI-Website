Plain Language

Feeding and eating disorders are illnesses that disrupt eating, feeding, or weight-control behavior enough to harm nutrition, physical health, emotions, relationships, or daily life. A person can be seriously ill at any body size, and the behavior is not simply a choice or lack of willpower.

Why It Matters

These disorders can quietly cause dehydration, electrolyte shifts, abnormal heart rhythms, slowed growth, organ injury, refeeding complications, suicide risk, and death. Early, respectful assessment changes whether the patient needs emergency stabilization, specialist treatment, or coordinated outpatient care.

Definition

Feeding and eating disorders are DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 diagnostic groups defined by persistent disturbances of eating or eating-related behavior that impair food intake, health, psychosocial functioning, or both. The group includes anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, pica, rumination disorder, and specified or unspecified presentations. Diagnosis requires the syndrome-specific pattern and exclusions; body size, one behavior, or a screening score does not diagnose the disorder.

Pathology

No single biopsy, scan, body shape, or laboratory pattern defines this group. Harm may arise from inadequate energy or nutrient intake, rapid weight change, recurrent binge eating, vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, water loading, driven exercise, ingestion of nonfood substances, or repeated regurgitation. Medical findings reflect the behavior, duration, rate of change, comorbidity, and individual physiology.

Pathophysiology

  • Biologic vulnerability, reward and threat learning, appetite and interoceptive signaling, mood and anxiety processes, compulsive habits, development, trauma exposure, family and social context, and food access can interact. No one mechanism explains every disorder or patient.

  • Restriction and malnutrition can themselves intensify rigidity, anxiety, depressed mood, slowed thinking, gastrointestinal symptoms, and preoccupation with food, creating a self-reinforcing illness loop.

  • Purging can shift potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, sodium, magnesium, fluid volume, and acid-base balance; rapid nutritional restoration after substantial undernutrition can shift phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, thiamine demand, glucose handling, and fluid balance.

Etiology

Etiology is multifactorial and is not proved by appearance, family dynamics, social media exposure, dieting, trauma, or one stressful event. Genetic susceptibility and temperament can interact with developmental change, psychiatric or neurodevelopmental conditions, gastrointestinal or endocrine illness, weight stigma, food insecurity, sport or occupational pressures, medication effects, and learned avoidance. Clinicians should ask what maintains the current behavior without assigning blame.

Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of an eating disorder, mood or anxiety disorder, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, substance use, or neurodevelopmental difference

  • Recent rapid weight change, restrictive dieting, food insecurity, gastrointestinal symptoms, diabetes requiring insulin, weight-focused sport or occupation, or repeated weight stigma

  • Adolescence, pregnancy or postpartum change, major transition, trauma or abuse, and sexual- or gender-minority stress can alter risk or barriers to care

  • Prior eating disorder remains clinically relevant during relapse-prone periods even when current weight and laboratory results appear ordinary

Signs Symptoms

  • Restriction, narrowing food variety, skipped meals, fear of eating consequences, recurrent binge eating, vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, fasting, driven exercise, secretive eating, nonfood ingestion, or repeated regurgitation

  • Preoccupation with food, weight, shape, calories, purity, control, choking, vomiting, sensory qualities, or access to preferred foods

  • Dizziness, fainting, palpitations, weakness, cold intolerance, constipation, abdominal symptoms, dental erosion, swelling, menstrual or hormonal change, delayed puberty, or deviation from expected growth

  • Shame, irritability, anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, impaired concentration, self-harm, suicidal thinking, or disruption of school, work, relationships, and finances

Diagnostics

  • Use a private, nonjudgmental history of intake, food variety, restriction, binge episodes, loss of control, purging, exercise, medicines and supplements, nonfood ingestion, regurgitation, weight and growth trajectory, body-image concerns, feared consequences, duration, and functional impact. Obtain collateral information with consent or under the appropriate safety framework.

  • Complete a medical and psychiatric assessment including temperature, resting and orthostatic pulse and blood pressure, hydration, cardiopulmonary and abdominal findings, oral and dental findings, neurologic status, growth and puberty when relevant, pregnancy possibility, substance use, diabetes behaviors, suicide risk, and safeguarding.

  • Apply syndrome-specific DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 requirements after excluding better medical, substance, developmental, cultural, religious, and food-access explanations. A questionnaire can identify risk but cannot confirm diagnosis or medical stability.

  • Do not use BMI, weight, illness duration, one electrolyte result, or one normal examination as the sole decision for referral, treatment, or admission.

Labs

  • Select tests from current behavior and medical risk: complete blood count, glucose, renal and liver measures, electrolytes including potassium, bicarbonate, magnesium and phosphorus, urinalysis, pregnancy testing, and an ECG are commonly considered when restriction, rapid change, purging, cardiac symptoms, or QT-risk medicines are present.

  • Add iron or other nutrient studies, endocrine testing, inflammatory or celiac evaluation, toxicology, blood lead testing, imaging, or gastrointestinal testing when the history supports a specific deficiency, exposure, medical differential, or complication.

  • Normal initial laboratory values do not exclude severe restriction, cardiovascular compromise, an eating disorder, or later refeeding risk; trend the patient, not a single panel.

Assessment

First decide whether airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, hydration, electrolytes, ECG, organ function, acute food or fluid refusal, severe purging, altered mental status, pregnancy, suicide risk, abuse, or inability to remain safe requires urgent care. Then establish the eating pattern, medical consequences, diagnosis, supports, treatment access, and safest level of care. Ask permission before weight discussion, explain why measurements are needed, and avoid praise or blame tied to weight change.

Differential

Differentiate a feeding or eating disorder from food scarcity or culturally sanctioned practice; gastrointestinal, endocrine, infectious, malignant, neurologic, dental, swallowing, metabolic, or medication causes; delirium or dementia; depression, psychosis, obsessive-compulsive disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, substance use, autism-related needs, and ordinary developmentally expected selectivity. Medical disease and an eating disorder can coexist, so one explanation must not stop the other evaluation.

Treatments

  • Provide emergency medical stabilization when physiology is compromised, and use an individualized, trained-team nutrition plan when substantial undernutrition or refeeding risk is present. Nutrition may be oral, enteral, or rarely parenteral according to the clinical situation; route does not replace monitoring.

  • Use age-appropriate, eating-disorder-focused psychotherapy and coordinated medical, nutritional, psychiatric, nursing, and social care. Evidence-supported approaches depend on diagnosis and age and include eating-disorder-focused cognitive behavioral therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy for binge-eating disorder, and family-based treatment for many adolescents.

  • Use medication only for a supported diagnosis, population, comorbidity, or selected adjunctive goal. Fluoxetine has an adult bulimia indication; lisdexamfetamine has an adult moderate-to-severe binge-eating disorder indication and is not a weight-loss drug; no medication treats the whole group.

  • Address food access, housing, transport, weight stigma, school or work accommodations, diabetes safety, pregnancy care, trauma and abuse, substance use, and caregiver education because these can determine whether a technically sound plan is usable.

Contraindications

  • Do not prescribe a rapid unsupervised feeding plan to a substantially undernourished or otherwise high-risk patient; use the responsible refeeding protocol with trained monitoring.

  • Bupropion is contraindicated in a patient with a current or prior diagnosis of anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa because seizure risk is higher.

  • Do not use stimulant, laxative, diuretic, thyroid, insulin, or weight-loss medication as a substitute for eating-disorder treatment or without verifying indication, misuse risk, and medical safety.

  • Do not make weight loss the treatment goal for binge-eating disorder, or require weight gain before treating a patient whose illness is medically or psychologically serious at a higher weight.

Safety Considerations

  • Recheck level of care whenever vital signs, ECG, laboratory trends, intake, purging, rate of weight change, cognition, organ function, suicide risk, pregnancy, supports, or outpatient response changes.

  • During nutritional restoration, monitor fluid balance, cardiovascular and neurologic status, glucose, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, and thiamine needs according to risk and protocol; refeeding syndrome can occur after feeding begins.

  • Ask directly about suicide, self-harm, abuse, coercion, poisoning, medication or insulin manipulation, and access to lethal means. Eating-disorder care does not replace a suicide or safeguarding response.

  • Use trauma-informed weighing, meals, observation, searches, feeding support, and escalation. Preserve dignity and the least restrictive safe approach while meeting medical and legal duties.

Nursing Priorities

  • Obtain behavior-specific history, current intake and output, weight and growth trajectory, vital signs including orthostatic change when safe, symptoms, medication or supplement use, suicide risk, and safeguarding concerns without relying on appearance.

  • Trend cardiovascular, neurologic, hydration, renal, gastrointestinal, oral, skin, endocrine, and functional findings; review ECG and laboratory results in context and escalate deterioration promptly.

  • Follow the prescribed meal, fluid, activity, bathroom, observation, electrolyte, thiamine, and refeeding plan consistently. Document objective behavior and response without bargaining, shaming, or power struggles.

  • Coordinate the patient, caregivers when appropriate, medical team, eating-disorder specialists, dietitian, pharmacy, therapy, school or work, obstetric or pediatric care, and discharge follow-up so safety continues across settings.

  • Teach symptoms that require urgent help and confirm the patient knows whom to contact before discharge.

Red Flags

  • Syncope, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, hypothermia, severe weakness, hematemesis, severe abdominal pain or distention, respiratory distress, or reduced consciousness

  • Severe dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, ECG abnormality, acute food or fluid refusal, rapidly declining intake or weight trajectory, uncontrolled vomiting or purging, or signs of emerging organ failure

  • New edema, dyspnea, weakness, delirium, arrhythmia, or neurologic change after nutrition is restarted, suggesting possible refeeding complications

  • Suicidal intent, recent serious self-harm, inability to maintain safety, poisoning, dangerous insulin or medication manipulation, abuse, or a dependent person at risk

  • Pregnancy with dehydration, poor intake, repeated vomiting or purging, syncope, bleeding, reduced fetal movement, or other obstetric concern

Complications

  • Electrolyte and acid-base disorders, dehydration, kidney injury, arrhythmia, cardiac dysfunction, aspiration, gastrointestinal injury, seizures, and refeeding syndrome

  • Malnutrition, anemia or micronutrient deficiency, endocrine suppression, low bone density, infertility, impaired immunity, delayed puberty, and impaired growth or brain development

  • Dental erosion, salivary swelling, esophageal injury, constipation, gastroparesis-like symptoms, bowel injury from substances or nonfood items, and metabolic complications

  • Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, substance use, self-harm, suicide, isolation, school or work loss, financial harm, caregiver strain, and relapse

Prognosis

Recovery is possible and often nonlinear. Earlier treatment, medical stabilization, nutrition rehabilitation, diagnosis-matched therapy, family or social support, and continuity improve the chance of recovery, while delayed care and severe comorbidity can prolong illness. Relapse or symptom migration can occur, so recovery includes physical, behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and functional monitoring rather than a target weight alone.

Prevention

  • Promote regular adequate nutrition, body-neutral and nonstigmatizing healthcare, safe sport and school practices, and early response to restriction, bingeing, purging, food fear, nonfood ingestion, or growth deviation.

  • Screen thoughtfully when weight changes, gastrointestinal symptoms, diabetes instability, menstrual or endocrine change, dental erosion, mood symptoms, or family concern is unexplained; do not wait for an underweight appearance.

  • Create a relapse plan that names the patient's earliest behavioral, medical, emotional, and social warning signs and a rapid route back to care.

Patient Education

  • An eating disorder can be serious at any body size. A normal BMI or normal first blood test does not prove that the heart, hydration, nutrition, or behavior is safe.

  • Tell the team about vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, supplements, driven exercise, fasting, water loading, insulin or medicine changes, fainting, and the true amount and variety eaten; this information changes monitoring and treatment.

  • Nutrition should be restarted with the level of monitoring your current risk requires. Do not copy another person's calorie, electrolyte, or refeeding plan.

  • Seek emergency help for fainting, chest pain, severe palpitations, seizure, confusion, vomiting blood, severe weakness, inability to drink, suicidal intent, or new breathing, swelling, or neurologic symptoms after feeding increases.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents may show slowed height gain, failure to follow a prior growth curve, delayed puberty, school change, irritability, or caregiver-observed behavior before they describe body concerns. Use age-appropriate growth context and involve caregivers without removing the young person's voice or privacy.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require coordinated obstetric and eating-disorder care for nutrition, hydration, fetal growth, medication, vomiting, mood, suicide risk, and postpartum relapse; current or past illness can matter even when symptoms seem quiet.

  • Men, older adults, people in larger bodies, athletes, racial and ethnic minorities, and sexual- or gender-minority patients are often missed because of stereotypes. Apply the same behavior- and physiology-based assessment.

  • Autism, ADHD, intellectual or sensory disability, swallowing or gastrointestinal disease, diabetes, food allergy, and food insecurity require accommodations and careful differentiation; they may coexist with a feeding or eating disorder.

Nclex Traps

  • Do not wait for low BMI: medical instability and atypical anorexia can occur at an average or higher weight.

  • Do not call normal electrolytes reassuring enough to discharge without reviewing vital signs, ECG risk, intake, purging, rate of change, suicide risk, and supports.

  • Do not start an aggressive universal feeding plan in a high-risk patient; use the ordered refeeding protocol and trend phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, glucose, fluid status, and clinical findings.

  • Do not praise weight loss or confront the patient as deceptive. Use direct, calm, behavior-specific, nonstigmatizing questions.

  • Medication never replaces medical stabilization, nutrition, and diagnosis-matched psychotherapy.
