Risk Factors

  • Cardioembolism and carotid atherosclerotic disease can send thrombus into the ACA origin.

  • Anatomic variation in the A1 segments and anterior communicating artery changes collateral protection.

  • Bilateral or dominant A2 disease can produce a larger frontal syndrome because both medial hemispheres lose flow.

Signs Symptoms

  • Contralateral leg weakness and sensory loss greater than face-arm involvement suggests ACA territory.

  • Abulia, slowed initiation, reduced speech, impaired judgment, grasp reflex, gait apraxia, or urinary incontinence reflects medial frontal dysfunction.

  • Bilateral ACA injury can produce profound leg weakness, behavioral change, akinetic mutism, or impaired consciousness.

Diagnostics

  • Compare face, arm, and leg power because the distribution localizes better than the word weakness alone.

  • Test initiation, attention, spontaneous speech, continence context, and bimanual coordination because frontal findings may be mistaken for depression or noncooperation.

  • NCCT excludes hemorrhage, while CTA/MRA and diffusion imaging identify ACA occlusion and medial frontal infarction.

Labs

  • Bedside glucose excludes a reversible mimic because hypoglycemia can change behavior and motor function.

  • Routine acute-stroke safety labs and anticoagulant history inform reperfusion decisions, while ECG/telemetry searches for cardiac embolic source.

  • Urinalysis alone should not explain new incontinence with focal leg weakness because frontal stroke can disrupt bladder control.

Treatments

  • Use the same urgent ischemic-stroke reperfusion pathway when deficits are disabling because ACA location does not make threatened brain less time-sensitive.

  • Protect mobility, continence, skin, and fall safety because leg weakness and initiation failure create functional risk even when arm strength is preserved.

  • Target the embolic or vascular mechanism in secondary prevention because recurrence risk persists beyond the initial territory.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess sudden contralateral leg-predominant weakness or sensory loss, abulia, behavior, speech, continence, gait, and exact onset because medial frontal and parietal ischemia creates a distinctive but variable pattern.

  • Activate the stroke pathway, document last-known-well, check glucose, and prepare emergency brain and vascular imaging because reperfusion eligibility depends on time, tissue, vessel, and hemorrhage exclusion.

  • Maintain airway, oxygenation, ordered blood-pressure targets, temperature, glucose, head position, and nothing-by-mouth status until swallowing is screened because secondary physiologic injury and aspiration worsen outcome.

  • Monitor neurologic examination, leg strength, consciousness, behavior, rhythm, blood pressure, swallowing, bladder, mobility, and reperfusion complications because edema, hemorrhage, seizure, and immobility may alter the initial syndrome.

  • Escalate for worsening deficit, declining consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, seizure, new hypoxemia, or reperfusion-related bleeding because recurrent occlusion, edema, and hemorrhagic transformation require immediate reassessment.

Complications

  • Falls, immobility, DVT, pressure injury, and loss of toileting independence can follow leg weakness and abulia.

  • Bilateral infarction can cause akinetic mutism and severe dependence because both medial frontal networks are impaired.

  • Depression can coexist, but mislabeling abulia as depression may delay appropriate neurologic rehabilitation.

Contraindications

  • Do not assume preserved arm strength excludes stroke because ACA stroke can be leg-predominant.

  • Do not interpret reduced spontaneous speech as Broca aphasia without testing naming, repetition, comprehension, and initiation.

  • Do not attribute new urinary incontinence to age alone when it accompanies sudden leg weakness or behavioral change.

Red Flags

  • Sudden leg-predominant weakness, new abulia, speech change, or loss of bladder control

  • Worsening deficit, reduced consciousness, severe headache, vomiting, or unequal pupils

  • Seizure lasting five minutes or recurrent seizures without neurologic recovery

  • Hemodynamic instability, aspiration, severe hypoxemia, or bleeding after reperfusion treatment

Patient Education

  • Call emergency services for sudden leg weakness, speech change, severe imbalance, or unusual loss of initiative because ACA stroke still requires rapid reperfusion evaluation.

  • Do not give food, drink, aspirin, or extra blood-pressure medicine before emergency assessment because swallowing, hemorrhage, and treatment eligibility must be checked first.

Nclex Traps

  • ACA is the classic leg-more-than-arm territory.

  • Abulia means impaired initiation, not necessarily impaired understanding.

  • Urinary incontinence plus gait and behavior change can localize to medial frontal injury.

Name

Anterior cerebral artery stroke

Category

Neurovascular localization

Definition

Anterior cerebral artery stroke is ischemia or infarction of medial frontal and parietal networks supplied by the ACA. Because the leg motor and sensory representations lie along the medial hemisphere, weakness and sensory loss often affect the contralateral leg more than the face or arm. Frontal-subcortical injury can also impair initiation, motivation, judgment, continence, and interhemispheric coordination, so the patient may appear apathetic or behaviorally changed rather than simply weak.

Etiology

ACA infarction can result from cardiac or artery-to-artery embolism, local atherosclerotic occlusion, dissection, vasculopathy, or extension from internal-carotid disease. Anterior communicating artery anatomy and cross-flow often protect one ACA, which helps explain why isolated ACA stroke is less common than MCA stroke and why bilateral involvement has a distinctive severe frontal syndrome.

Pathology

The ACA supplies medial frontal motor cortex, supplementary motor area, anterior cingulate, medial parietal cortex, and corpus-callosum regions. Damage therefore affects leg movement, initiation of planned action, motivation, continence control, and communication between hemispheres. Deep perforator involvement can add more extensive motor or cognitive deficits.

Pathophysiology

Loss of medial motor cortex causes contralateral leg-predominant weakness. Anterior cingulate and frontal-subcortical disconnection can cause abulia, reduced spontaneous speech, impaired initiation, or akinetic mutism in severe bilateral injury. Callosal involvement can impair coordinated bimanual tasks or produce alien-hand phenomena. Urinary incontinence arises from disruption of frontal inhibition of bladder emptying rather than from a primary urinary-tract disorder.
