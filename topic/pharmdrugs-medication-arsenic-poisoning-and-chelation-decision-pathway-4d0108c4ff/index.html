Used To Treat

Acute inorganic arsenic poisoning with severe gastroenteritis, shock, dysrhythmia, encephalopathy, or multiorgan injury and chronic exposure with neuropathy, skin changes, hematologic effects, or environmental risk.

Description

Inorganic arsenic is a systemic enzyme poison that binds cellular sulfhydryl groups and disrupts ATP production, causing capillary leak, severe gastroenteritis, dysrhythmia, shock, neuropathy, and multiorgan injury. A total urine arsenic can be markedly elevated after recent seafood because it includes arsenobetaine and arsenocholine; a 24-hour speciated urine collection is more useful for recent exposure, while an emergency spot urine arsenic-to-creatinine sample can be collected before treatment. Acute inorganic poisoning causes severe vomiting, abdominal pain, watery or bloody diarrhea, dehydration, vasodilation, capillary leak, QT-related dysrhythmia, encephalopathy, kidney and liver injury, and coagulopathy. Painful peripheral neuropathy and blood-count abnormalities can appear later. Chronic well-water or occupational exposure can cause neuropathy, anemia, characteristic pigmentation and palm or sole keratosis, and increased cancer risk. A critically ill high-probability patient needs aggressive resuscitation and early specialist chelation without waiting for a delayed confirmatory result. An asymptomatic patient with an isolated total level after seafood does not need reflex chelation; source history, speciation, symptoms, and toxicology expertise prevent harmful overtreatment.

Mechanism

Trivalent inorganic arsenic binds sulfhydryl groups on enzymes, while pentavalent forms can substitute for phosphate. These actions disrupt pyruvate dehydrogenase, oxidative phosphorylation, ATP production, endothelial integrity, and cellular repair. Energy failure and capillary leak explain high-output shock, lactic acidosis, gastrointestinal mucosal injury, neuropathy, and multiorgan dysfunction. Dimercaprol provides sulfhydryl groups that bind arsenic into complexes for excretion and is historically used early in severe acute poisoning. Succimer and DMPS are water-soluble chelators used in selected acute or chronic cases under specialists. Chelation works best before arsenic is tightly distributed into tissue, but every chelator can cause toxicity and cannot reverse established neuropathy or make contaminated water safe. Source removal stops the continuing dose and is therefore the central treatment for chronic exposure.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for profuse diarrhea or vomiting, fainting, shock, confusion, seizure, severe weakness, or suspected large inorganic ingestion. Begin airway and circulation support, aggressive but monitored fluid and electrolyte replacement, ECG monitoring, and Poison Help or medical-toxicology consultation.

  • Collect blood and a urine sample before chelation when this causes no delay. In a critically ill high-probability patient, use specialist-selected early dimercaprol or another appropriate chelator based on arsenic form, route, severity, kidney function, pregnancy, and product availability; transition or oral regimens are individualized.

  • For chronic exposure, identify and stop contaminated water, food, remedy, pesticide, smelting, semiconductor, treated-wood, or take-home source. Use speciated urine testing and environmental or public-health support; monitor neurologic, skin, hematologic, renal, hepatic, and cancer-related outcomes.

Nursing Essentials

  • Ask about well water, imported remedies, pesticides, pressure-treated wood or burning, mining or smelting, glass and semiconductor work, hobbies, intentional exposure, coworkers, family members, and pets. Ask specifically about seafood within the previous 48 hours because recent seafood can raise total urine arsenic through less-toxic organic species and therefore alter interpretation.

  • Trend ECG and QT, heart rate, blood pressure, perfusion, fluid losses, glucose, mental status, urine output, abdominal symptoms, stool, skin, sensation, strength, and reflexes. Use strict intake and output and replace potassium and magnesium carefully.

  • Verify chelator, salt, route, measured weight, kidney and liver status, G6PD risk, concurrent iron, and monitoring plan. Dimercaprol is painful intramuscular therapy and has important adverse effects and interaction with medicinal iron; do not administer from a generic metal protocol.

Key Labs

  • Use 24-hour urine arsenic with speciation for recent exposure because speciation distinguishes toxic inorganic arsenic from less-toxic seafood-derived species; spot urine arsenic and creatinine can support an emergency assessment when collection cannot wait. Obtain CBC, glucose, electrolytes, magnesium, blood gas and lactate, kidney and liver studies, coagulation, urinalysis, and serial ECG because gastrointestinal losses, energy failure, QT instability, and multiorgan injury can evolve over time.

  • Total urine arsenic includes nontoxic seafood species, and hair or nail testing is vulnerable to external contamination and lacks accepted clinical reference ranges. Blood arsenic clears quickly and a normal later value does not exclude earlier exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Acute arsenic poisoning can cause hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, severe dehydration, QT prolongation, ventricular dysrhythmia, shock, lactic acidosis, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, kidney or liver failure, delayed pancytopenia, neuropathy, and death.

  • Dimercaprol can cause hypertension, tachycardia, nausea, headache, salivation, fever, painful injections, and hemolysis risk in G6PD deficiency; succimer can cause GI symptoms, rash, neutropenia, and liver-enzyme elevation.

Contraindications

  • Do not chelate an asymptomatic patient solely for a total urine arsenic elevated after seafood or an unvalidated hair test. Do not delay lifesaving chelation in a critically ill high-probability patient while waiting for speciation.

  • Do not combine dimercaprol with medicinal iron because a toxic complex can form. Kidney disease, pregnancy, and G6PD deficiency require expert chelator selection rather than automatic exclusion from rescue.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Shock, dysrhythmia, severe GI loss, encephalopathy, seizure, acidosis, kidney failure, coagulopathy, or rising organ injury requires ICU and toxicology; later neuropathy, weakness, cytopenias, skin lesions, or ongoing source exposure requires environmental-medicine and public-health follow-up.

Interactions

  • Seafood can confound total urine arsenic without representing toxic inorganic exposure. Arsine gas is a distinct inhalational hemolytic poison and should not be managed as ordinary ingested arsenic.

  • QT-prolonging drugs and electrolyte depletion increase dysrhythmia risk. Medicinal iron interacts dangerously with dimercaprol, while other nephrotoxins complicate chelate excretion.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Developing neurologic systems, hand-to-mouth exposure, contaminated water, and household take-home dust require family-wide environmental investigation.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Severe maternal poisoning requires immediate resuscitation and expert chelation; fetal risk from untreated shock is substantial, while chelator evidence is limited.

  • Population

    Kidney disease or G6PD deficiency

    Note

    Chelator complexes and hemolysis risk require individualized agent selection, renal monitoring, and possible extracorporeal support.

Evidence Limitations

  • Chelation comparisons are not supported by large randomized trials, and older dose thresholds do not fit every species or exposure. ATSDR recommends medical-toxicology expertise because early benefit, chelator toxicity, speciation, and chronic outcome evidence differ.

Nclex Traps

  • Recent seafood can raise total urine arsenic, so speciation prevents a false toxic-inorganic diagnosis.

  • Hair and nail arsenic tests are easily contaminated and are not reliable stand-alone treatment triggers.

  • In severe acute poisoning, early chelation can matter more than waiting for delayed laboratory confirmation.

Regulatory Status

Dimercaprol and succimer have defined labeling, but arsenic chelation selection, DMPS access, switching, and duration are medical-toxicology decisions. Environmental source removal and public-health action are essential treatment components.

Boxed Warning

ARSENIC WARNING: call 911 for profuse GI loss, shock, confusion, seizure, or suspected large inorganic exposure. Resuscitate and seek early toxicology-guided chelation without waiting for delayed confirmation, but do not chelate an isolated total urine result until seafood and arsenic speciation are considered.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
