Definition

Antidiuretic hormone, also called arginine vasopressin, is the principal rapid regulator of renal water excretion. Osmolality describes solute concentration, and free-water clearance describes whether the kidneys are excreting water in excess of solute or retaining water relative to solute.

Etiology

Hypothalamic osmoreceptors release ADH when effective plasma tonicity rises, while substantial low effective arterial volume can override osmotic signals and stimulate ADH even when sodium is low. Central deficiency, renal resistance, excessive nonosmotic release, medications, pain, nausea, pulmonary or CNS disease, and impaired GFR can disturb the pathway.

Pathology

ADH is synthesized in hypothalamic nuclei, transported to the posterior pituitary, and released into blood. It binds collecting-duct V2 receptors, activating cAMP and inserting aquaporin-2 channels into the apical membrane; water then crosses toward the medullary osmotic gradient.

Pathophysiology

With ADH and an intact gradient, water is reclaimed without proportional solute, urine osmolality rises, and free-water clearance becomes negative. Without ADH effect, collecting ducts remain relatively water impermeable, urine is dilute, and free-water clearance becomes positive. In SIADH-like states, persistent water retention lowers serum sodium and suppresses urine volume while urine remains inappropriately concentrated. In low effective arterial volume, the same ADH response is physiologically protective for perfusion but can worsen dilutional hyponatremia and edema.

Risk Factors

  • CNS injury, surgery, tumor, infection, or genetic central diabetes insipidus

  • Lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, or tubulointerstitial disease causing nephrogenic resistance

  • Pain, nausea, pulmonary/CNS disease, or medications promoting inappropriate ADH release

  • Heart failure, cirrhosis, or severe volume depletion causing nonosmotic ADH

Signs Symptoms

  • Polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia, dehydration, or hypernatremia with inadequate ADH effect

  • Nausea, headache, confusion, gait change, seizure, or coma with severe hyponatremia

  • Concentrated low-volume urine in low effective arterial volume

  • Edema or ascites when water retention accompanies sodium-retaining disease

Diagnostics

  • Determine whether measured serum osmolality and glucose support hypotonic, isotonic, or hypertonic hyponatremia

  • Pair urine osmolality with urine sodium and a careful volume assessment

  • Review thyroid, adrenal, kidney, medication, glucose, and recent fluid context

  • Use specialist dynamic testing when differentiating central from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus

Labs

  • High serum osmolality with very dilute urine supports impaired ADH effect

  • Low serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine supports persistent ADH activity after other causes are considered

  • Urine sodium interpretation changes with diuretics, CKD, dietary intake, and effective arterial volume

  • Free-water clearance is positive when urine is more dilute than plasma and negative when urine is more concentrated

Treatments

  • Treat the cause and correct water or sodium disturbance at a rate based on chronicity, symptoms, and risk

  • Use desmopressin for appropriate central deficiency and selected controlled correction strategies

  • Use fluid management, solute strategy, or vasopressin antagonism only when the specific disorder and monitoring plan support it

  • Correct lithium, calcium, potassium, adrenal, thyroid, or hemodynamic contributors

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess thirst, intake, urine volume, serum sodium, serum and urine osmolality, volume status, pain, nausea, medications, and neurologic disease because ADH release reflects tonicity plus nonosmotic stress signals.

  • Trend paired serum and urine data, weight, output, glucose, kidney function, and neurologic status because free-water retention or loss changes sodium and brain-cell volume over time.

  • Verify timing around fluids, diuretics, desmopressin, and water restriction because treatment can rapidly change urine osmolality and make an isolated value misleading.

  • Carry out prescribed fluid, saline, or desmopressin plans with explicit sodium-correction limits because overcorrection of chronic dysnatremia can cause osmotic demyelination or cerebral edema.

  • Escalate immediately for seizure, coma, rapidly changing sodium, profound polyuria with shock, or water intoxication symptoms because severe free-water imbalance is a neurologic emergency.

Complications

  • Cerebral edema from acute severe hyponatremia

  • Osmotic demyelination from overly rapid correction of susceptible chronic hyponatremia

  • Hypernatremic dehydration and shock

  • Falls, rhabdomyolysis, seizure, and permanent neurologic injury

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose SIADH before excluding important endocrine, renal, medication, and volume causes

  • Do not give desmopressin reflexively for polyuria until osmotic diuresis and renal function are assessed

Red Flags

  • Seizure, coma, severe confusion, or new focal neurologic finding

  • Serum sodium changing faster than the prescribed correction limit

  • Profound polyuria with hypotension, tachycardia, or acute weight loss

  • Worsening headache, vomiting, agitation, or declining consciousness during correction

Patient Education

  • Explain that ADH tells collecting ducts to retain water, so urine concentration reflects both hormone signaling and the kidney's ability to respond.

  • Teach patients to follow the exact fluid and desmopressin plan because extra water or extra doses can rapidly produce dangerous hyponatremia.

Nclex Traps

  • ADH retains water more directly than sodium

  • Low effective arterial volume can stimulate ADH despite low sodium

  • Urine osmolality answers a different question from urine sodium

  • A sudden water diuresis can rapidly change sodium during treatment

Causal Links

  • High effective osmolality -> ADH release -> V2 signaling -> aquaporin-2 insertion -> water retention

  • Low effective arterial volume -> nonosmotic ADH -> concentrated urine and possible dilutional hyponatremia

  • Absent ADH effect -> positive free-water clearance -> polyuria and hypernatremia risk

  • Abrupt return of dilute urine -> rapid free-water loss -> unexpectedly fast sodium correction
