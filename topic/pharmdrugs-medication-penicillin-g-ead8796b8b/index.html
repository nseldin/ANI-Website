Used To Treat

Severe susceptible streptococcal, pneumococcal, meningococcal, clostridial, actinomycotic, and spirochetal infections; neurosyphilis and other indications use formulation- and site-specific regimens. Culture, MIC, and guideline context determine use because many staphylococci and Gram-negative organisms are resistant.

Mechanism

The beta-lactam carbonyl mimics the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of peptidoglycan precursors. PBPs attack it and become covalently acylated, preventing transpeptidation while autolysins continue opening the wall. Killing therefore depends on active growth and sufficient fT>MIC. Resistance arises through beta-lactamase, altered PBPs, or limited access. Penicillin G does not neutralize preformed toxin or repair tissue, so tetanus/botulism management, gas-gangrene debridement, endocarditis source evaluation, and syphilis follow-up remain essential. A Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction after spirochetal treatment reflects inflammatory response to organism breakdown, not IgE allergy.

Contraindications

  • A history of serious hypersensitivity to penicillin G or a relevant beta-lactam requires phenotype-specific avoidance or specialist management because re-exposure can trigger anaphylaxis or recurrent severe delayed injury.

  • Do not substitute benzathine penicillin G or procaine penicillin G for aqueous IV penicillin G; depot formulations produce different concentrations and cannot treat neurosyphilis or other high-serum/CSF indications interchangeably.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify exact salt and formulation, indication, weight-based or high-dose calculation, infusion concentration, renal function, potassium, sodium/fluid status, allergy phenotype, and culture because formulation errors can turn an appropriate molecule into ineffective or toxic therapy.

  • For syphilis, teach that Jarisch-Herxheimer symptoms can begin within the first day and require assessment, but they do not justify labeling the patient penicillin-allergic; pregnancy still requires fetal and obstetric monitoring when relevant.

Interactions

  • Probenecid blocks tubular secretion and prolongs penicillin G concentrations; this can be intentional in selected regimens but also increases exposure.

  • Potassium supplements, RAAS inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, and kidney failure compound potassium-salt hyperkalemia risk; bacteriologic compatibility and line compatibility must be checked with aminoglycosides.

Key Labs

  • Culture/MIC, CBC during prolonged high-dose therapy, creatinine/eGFR, potassium and sodium, liver tests when indicated, ECG for major electrolyte disturbance, and infection-specific serology or cultures.

  • For syphilis, nontreponemal titer trend evaluates response; persistent treponemal antibody alone does not prove active treatment failure.

Nclex Traps

  • Penicillin G potassium is not a potassium supplement, but its counterion can be clinically significant at high doses.

  • Benzathine penicillin G is long-acting IM depot therapy; aqueous penicillin G is required when sustained high serum or CSF exposure is needed.

    Pregnancy and lactation

    Note

    Many beta-lactams have substantial pregnancy and lactation experience, but infection severity, allergy history, maternal organ function, placental exposure, neonatal bilirubin handling, and the exact agent still matter. Treat serious infection promptly with an evidence-supported regimen rather than applying one class-wide pregnancy rule.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Immediate anaphylaxis can be fatal. High-dose potassium salt can cause dangerous hyperkalemia and arrhythmia, while sodium salt can worsen sodium/volume burden; excessive CNS exposure can cause myoclonus or seizures.

Class Pathway

  • Natural penicillin

  • PBP transpeptidase inhibition

  • High-dose IV/IM therapy for severe susceptible infections

Description

Penicillin G, or benzylpenicillin, is a narrow parenteral natural beta-lactam antibiotic that irreversibly acylates susceptible bacterial PBPs, stops peptidoglycan cross-linking, and causes lysis during active wall remodeling. It remains exceptionally useful when the organism is truly susceptible, including many streptococci, Treponema pallidum, selected meningococci, Clostridium species, and Actinomyces. Gastric acid destroys too much drug for reliable oral systemic exposure, so serious therapy uses IV or IM formulations. The potassium or sodium salt is part of the medication: massive IV doses can deliver clinically important potassium or sodium and can accumulate during renal impairment, causing electrolyte, volume, or neurologic toxicity.

Adverse Effects

  • Urticaria, anaphylaxis, delayed rash, serum sickness-like reactions, DRESS, SJS/TEN, hemolytic anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, interstitial nephritis, diarrhea, and CDI.

  • High-dose electrolyte load, phlebitis, encephalopathy, myoclonus, or seizure can occur, especially with renal impairment; Jarisch-Herxheimer fever, rigors, myalgia, and transient symptom worsening can follow syphilis treatment.
