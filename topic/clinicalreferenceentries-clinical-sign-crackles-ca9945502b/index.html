Definition

Crackles is an auscultatory respiratory sign. It is a clue to sudden reopening of collapsed small airways or alveoli, or air moving through fluid and secretions in distal airways. A positive finding means brief discontinuous popping or crackling sounds are heard, commonly during inspiration; fine crackles are short and high pitched, while coarse crackles are lower and longer.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Crackles means brief discontinuous popping or crackling sounds are heard, commonly during inspiration; fine crackles are short and high pitched, while coarse crackles are lower and longer.

Why It Matters

A positive Crackles response can signal sudden reopening of collapsed small airways or alveoli, or air moving through fluid and secretions in distal airways. Collapsed small airways can snap open as inspiratory pressure rises, and air can bubble or move through fluid or secretions, creating discontinuous sounds. Crackles cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

  • sudden reopening of collapsed small airways or alveoli, or air moving through fluid and secretions in distal airways

Assessment Method

Auscultate matching lung regions during inspiration and expiration, note fine versus coarse quality, timing, location, symmetry, persistence after coughing, and associated oxygenation and work of breathing.

Evidence Limitations

Technique, shallow breathing, dependent atelectasis, hair rubbing, and transmitted upper-airway noise can produce misleading sounds; crackles are not disease-specific. Therefore, Crackles cannot confirm or exclude sudden reopening of collapsed small airways or alveoli, or air moving through fluid and secretions in distal airways by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from rhonchi, pleural friction rub, hair artifact, dependent atelectasis, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Crackles may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current common sign that helps localize pulmonary involvement but does not by itself distinguish edema, infection, atelectasis, fibrosis, or secretions. Safety Precautions

Before assessing Crackles, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Auscultate matching lung regions during inspiration and expiration, note fine versus coarse quality, timing, location, symmetry, persistence after coughing, and associated oxygenation and work of breathing. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Promptly escalate new crackles with hypoxemia, respiratory distress, chest pain, fever with instability, or signs of acute pulmonary edema; reposition or ask for a cough only when safe and document whether the sound changes.
