Used To Treat

Edema associated with heart failure, renal disease, or hepatic disease and adult hypertension under current labeling. Its reliable oral absorption and longer effect can simplify outpatient decongestion, but current evidence does not establish survival superiority over furosemide.

Mechanism

Torsemide is delivered to the proximal tubule, secreted into the lumen, and inhibits the luminal NKCC2 cotransporter in the thick ascending limb. Sodium, potassium, and chloride reabsorption falls, the medullary gradient weakens, and the ROMK-generated positive luminal voltage is lost, increasing calcium and magnesium excretion. Distal sodium delivery and contraction-mediated aldosterone signaling increase potassium and hydrogen secretion, explaining hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. Unlike furosemide, a large fraction of torsemide clearance is hepatic, primarily through CYP2C9 metabolism, while renal secretion still matters for access to NKCC2. Its consistent oral exposure can help when gut absorption of furosemide is unreliable, but severe kidney or liver disease can still change response, protein binding, metabolism, and safety.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Excessive torsemide can cause dehydration, blood-volume reduction, hypotension, worsening renal function, and profound electrolyte depletion. Ototoxicity including tinnitus and hearing loss has been reported, with risk rising at high exposure, severe renal impairment, hypoproteinemia, or concurrent ototoxic therapy. Reliable absorption makes the response more predictable, not harmless.

Contraindications

  • Anuria and hypersensitivity are core label contraindications. Severe sodium, potassium, chloride, or volume depletion and progressive oliguria or azotemia require correction or reassessment before more drug.

  • Cirrhosis with ascites requires cautious titration because an abrupt fluid or electrolyte shift can precipitate hepatic encephalopathy.

  • The current label contraindicates anuria, torsemide hypersensitivity, and hepatic coma.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record weight, congestion, pressure, orthostasis, intake/output, recent response, creatinine/eGFR, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, and magnesium before aggressive dosing.

  • Reassess within the expected route-specific effect window. Teach daily weight and symptom thresholds, morning timing when feasible, fall prevention, and urgent reporting of hearing change or severe weakness.

  • When changing from furosemide, document the approximate conversion and actual prior response. Do not promise longer survival; explain that the selection is based on absorption, duration, adherence, and response.

Interactions

  • NSAIDs can blunt natriuresis and worsen kidney perfusion; aminoglycosides, cisplatin, and other ototoxins increase hearing risk; lithium clearance falls with sodium depletion.

  • RAAS blockers and vasodilators add hypotension and filtration effects, while low potassium or magnesium increases digoxin and arrhythmia risk.

  • CYP2C9 inhibitors or inducers may alter exposure. Cholestyramine can reduce absorption, and high-dose salicylates can become more toxic.

Key Labs

  • Daily weight, intake/output, blood pressure and orthostasis, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, magnesium, BUN, creatinine/eGFR, and calcium or uric acid when clinically relevant.

  • Judge response by decongestion with preserved perfusion and acceptable chemistry, not urine volume alone.

Nclex Traps

  • Loop drugs increase distal sodium delivery, so potassium and hydrogen are lost together and hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis can result.

  • They increase urinary calcium and magnesium; thiazides decrease urinary calcium.

  • Torsemide is more predictably absorbed than furosemide, but TRANSFORM-HF did not show a mortality advantage. Pharmacokinetic convenience and outcome superiority are different claims.

Description

Torsemide, historically branded Demadex and also available as Soaanz, blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb to produce potent sodium-water loss and decongestion. This oral and IV loop diuretic has about 80 percent bioavailability and less variable absorption than furosemide in labeling data; food can delay the peak concentration but does not meaningfully reduce total exposure. A commonly used conversion is torsemide 20 mg to furosemide 40 mg orally, although patient response and local protocols control. Torsemide is more than 99 percent protein bound, has a roughly 3.5-hour half-life, and is cleared largely through hepatic metabolism involving CYP2C9, with the remainder excreted through kidney pathways. Its pharmacokinetics can provide a more sustained outpatient effect, but the pragmatic TRANSFORM-HF trial found no mortality advantage over furosemide. Choose it for patient-specific delivery and response, not an unsupported promise of superior outcomes.

Adverse Effects

  • Dose-related volume depletion, orthostasis, hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, and kidney-function change arise from potent salt loss and downstream aldosterone signaling.

  • Tinnitus or hearing loss becomes more likely with high or rapid IV exposure, kidney impairment, low albumin, or another ototoxin; severe rash, interstitial nephritis, cytopenia, and pancreatitis are less common.
