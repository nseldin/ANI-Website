Plain Language

Panic disorder causes repeated unexpected panic attacks—sudden waves of intense fear or discomfort with symptoms such as a racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, dizziness, trembling, or fear of dying—followed by ongoing worry or behavior changes because another attack might happen.

Why It Matters

Panic symptoms can look like myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, hypoglycemia, intoxication, withdrawal, or another emergency. Nurses must assess acute physical danger first, then recognize the recurrent panic pattern so the patient receives effective CBT and avoids a cycle of fear, emergency visits, substance use, and shrinking activity.

Definition

Panic disorder is an anxiety disorder characterized by recurrent unexpected panic attacks followed by persistent concern about additional attacks or their consequences, or maladaptive behavior change related to attacks, with clinically important distress or impairment. A panic attack is a symptom episode, not automatically panic disorder; attacks caused by substances, medication, medical illness, or only by another disorder require a different formulation. Agoraphobia is assessed separately rather than assumed from panic disorder.

Pathology

Panic disorder involves repeated catastrophic interpretation of otherwise survivable internal sensations and a sensitized alarm response. Fear of the sensations increases autonomic arousal, which intensifies the sensations and reinforces escape, checking, and avoidance. The model helps explain treatment but does not replace evaluation for genuine cardiopulmonary, neurologic, endocrine, toxicologic, or obstetric illness.

Pathophysiology

  • A panic attack can rapidly activate sympathetic arousal, producing tachycardia, sweating, tremor, dyspnea, chest discomfort, nausea, paresthesias, chills or heat, dizziness, derealization, and fear of losing control or dying.

  • Anticipatory anxiety, body scanning, escape, repeated vital-sign checking, and avoidance give short relief but prevent corrective learning; panic-focused CBT uses cognitive work and graded exposure to reverse this cycle.

Etiology

Panic disorder reflects interacting genetic vulnerability, sensitivity to body sensations, learning, stress, and contextual factors. A first attack may occur during illness, sleep loss, stress, stimulant exposure, or apparently without a trigger. Having one panic attack does not mean the disorder will develop.

Risk Factors

  • Family history of panic or other anxiety disorders and high anxiety sensitivity

  • Recent major stress, trauma, sleep loss, postpartum change, bereavement, or a frightening medical event

  • Asthma or other illness that produces feared body sensations, while avoiding the error of assuming symptoms are psychiatric

  • Caffeine, nicotine, stimulants, cannabis, cocaine, decongestants, thyroid medicine, or alcohol and sedative withdrawal

Signs Symptoms

  • Abrupt intense fear or discomfort with palpitations, sweating, tremor, shortness of breath, choking sensation, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, chills or heat, numbness, derealization, or fear of dying or losing control

  • Attacks that peak rapidly and may occur from sleep or outside a consistently predictable trigger

  • Persistent fear of another attack, repeated body checking, emergency visits, carrying rescue items, or avoiding exertion, travel, driving, crowds, or being alone

  • Depression, agoraphobia, health anxiety, alcohol or sedative use, and reduced work, school, or caregiving may coexist

Diagnostics

  • During an acute first or atypical episode, assess vital signs, oxygenation, glucose when indicated, ECG and targeted medical causes according to age, symptoms, examination, pregnancy status, risk factors, and local protocols.

  • After immediate danger is addressed, establish whether attacks are unexpected, recurrent, followed by persistent concern or behavior change, impairing, and not better explained by a substance, medical condition, or another mental disorder.

  • Screening scales can quantify severity and track response but do not confirm panic disorder or exclude myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, seizure, hypoglycemia, or withdrawal.

  • Assess agoraphobia independently by identifying feared situation groups, avoidance, companion dependence, duration, and impairment.

Labs

  • No laboratory test or scan confirms panic disorder.

  • Target ECG, troponin, glucose, CBC, electrolytes, thyroid tests, pregnancy testing, toxicology, respiratory evaluation, or other studies to the clinical presentation; no universal panel fits every patient.

  • A previous panic diagnosis must not prevent reevaluation when the current episode differs in onset, quality, severity, associated findings, or risk context.

Assessment

Stay with the patient, reduce stimulation, use short calm statements, and assess airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, oxygenation, pain features, neurologic status, glucose when indicated, pregnancy context, substances, medications, and withdrawal. Ask what began first, how quickly symptoms peaked, whether this matches prior attacks, and whether syncope, exertional pain, focal deficit, fever, hypoxia, suicidal thinking, or inability to remain safe is present.

Differential

  • Acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, asthma, pneumothorax, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, thyroid disease, anemia, seizure, vestibular disorder, or another medical cause

  • Caffeine or stimulant excess, cocaine or cannabis effects, medication adverse effects, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal

  • Specific phobia, social anxiety disorder, PTSD, obsessive-compulsive disorder, separation anxiety disorder, illness anxiety disorder, or generalized anxiety disorder

  • Agoraphobia with or without panic disorder, major depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, somatic symptom disorder, or functional neurological symptoms

Treatments

  • Explain the panic cycle after acute medical danger is addressed. Use breathing that avoids overbreathing, grounding, sleep and substance review, and a collaborative plan for recurrence rather than repeated nonspecific reassurance.

  • Offer panic-focused CBT with education, cognitive restructuring, interoceptive exposure, in-vivo exposure to avoided situations, and relapse planning; low-intensity CBT-based self-help may suit some mild-to-moderate cases.

  • For adults who choose medication or have persistent impairment, evidence-based options include SSRIs such as sertraline or fluoxetine; start and titrate cautiously according to the exact current label because early activation can mimic panic.

  • Review adherence, diagnosis, comorbidity, safety, and treatment quality before switching or combining care; severe or refractory illness warrants specialist input.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose panic disorder from one attack or because initial tests are normal.

  • Do not prescribe benzodiazepines as routine long-term panic treatment; dependence, withdrawal, sedation, falls, and opioid or alcohol interaction can worsen outcomes.

  • Do not use sedating antihistamines or antipsychotics as routine panic-disorder treatment without another supported indication.

  • Do not combine an SSRI with an MAOI, ignore serotonergic interactions, or stop an SSRI abruptly; follow the current product label and individualized taper.

Safety Considerations

  • Treat the current physiology, not the historical label: new chest pain, syncope, hypoxia, focal neurologic findings, pregnancy-related risk, intoxication, or withdrawal can be life-threatening.

  • Assess suicide, self-harm, depression, hopelessness, substance use, and unsafe avoidance, especially after repeated attacks or major functional loss.

  • Before an antidepressant, screen for bipolar-spectrum symptoms and review current medicines; monitor activation, suicidality in younger patients, serotonin toxicity, bleeding, sodium risk, sexual effects, and discontinuation symptoms.

  • If coaching breathing, avoid forceful deep or rapid breathing that can worsen hypocapnia and paresthesias; use slow comfortable breathing and clinical reassessment.

Nursing Priorities

  • Rule out immediate cardiopulmonary, neurologic, endocrine, obstetric, toxicologic, and withdrawal emergencies.

  • Remain present, reduce noise and crowding, speak in brief concrete phrases, orient the patient to time and place, and explain each assessment step.

  • Document onset, peak, triggers, exact symptoms, objective findings, substances, medications, and how the episode compares with prior attacks.

  • After stabilization, reinforce CBT referral, medication adherence and monitoring, avoidance reduction, and a written plan describing when symptoms need emergency reevaluation.

Red Flags

  • Exertional or persistent chest pain, syncope, hypoxia, hemoptysis, unilateral leg swelling, sustained dysrhythmia, severe hypertension, or shock

  • Focal neurologic deficit, seizure, fever, delirium, head injury, severe headache, or rapidly changing mental status

  • Pregnancy or postpartum symptoms with chest pain, dyspnea, severe headache, hypertension, bleeding, psychosis, or infant-safety concern

  • Suicidal intent, severe self-neglect, intoxication, stimulant toxicity, or alcohol or sedative withdrawal

Complications

  • Agoraphobia, depression, suicide risk, substance misuse, and dependence on sedatives

  • Avoidance of exercise, driving, travel, work, school, health care, or being alone

  • Repeated emergency utilization, low-value testing, and distrust when symptoms are dismissed

  • Delayed diagnosis of genuine medical disease when every later episode is attributed to panic

Prognosis

Panic disorder is treatable. Panic-focused CBT and appropriately selected antidepressants can reduce attacks, anticipatory fear, and avoidance. Some people have episodic relapse; continued exposure practice, medication review, and a clear plan for medically different symptoms improve long-term function and safety.

Prevention

  • Reduce stimulant and excessive caffeine exposure, protect sleep, and address alcohol or sedative dependence safely.

  • Treat avoidance early by gradually returning to safe activities with CBT support rather than building life around preventing every sensation.

  • Create a personalized emergency plan that distinguishes the known panic pattern from symptoms that require new medical evaluation.

Patient Education

  • Panic attacks cause real, intense body symptoms. A panic attack is not the same as panic disorder, and a prior diagnosis does not make every future symptom harmless.

  • The alarm surge is time-limited even when it feels dangerous; slow comfortable breathing and remaining in a safe situation can help while urgent causes are assessed.

  • CBT deliberately retrains fear of body sensations and avoided situations. Antidepressants work gradually and may briefly increase jitteriness early.

  • Seek emergency help for new or different chest pain, fainting, severe breathing difficulty, focal weakness, seizure, pregnancy-related danger signs, or suicidal intent.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents need developmental assessment, family and school input, CBT, and careful SSRI/SNRI monitoring; not every episode of distress is an unexpected panic attack.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum require a lower threshold to assess thromboembolism, cardiac disease, thyroid change, preeclampsia, medication effects, depression, psychosis, and infant safety.

  • Older adults and people with cardiac, pulmonary, endocrine, neurologic, or medication complexity need cautious medical evaluation before attributing symptoms to panic.

  • Autistic people and those with communication, sensory, trauma, or intellectual needs may describe panic differently and need adapted assessment and CBT.

Nclex Traps

  • First address ABCs and medical red flags; do not begin by debating whether the fear is rational.

  • A panic attack is a symptom and can occur in many disorders; recurrent unexpected attacks plus persistent concern or behavior change define panic disorder.

  • Agoraphobia is a separate diagnosis and can occur without panic disorder.

  • Benzodiazepines are not routine long-term treatment; panic-focused CBT and selected antidepressants have stronger durable roles.
