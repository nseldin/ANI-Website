Plain Language

Factitious disorder imposed on self means a person intentionally falsifies, exaggerates, or causes symptoms and presents themselves as ill, while obvious external rewards are not the sole reason. Deception must be supported by evidence—frequent visits, puzzling symptoms, or normal tests alone do not prove it.

Why It Matters

The person can suffer serious injury, infection, overdose, unnecessary surgery, medication complications, or death from induced symptoms and repeated care, while genuine disease may be missed if clinicians dismiss every complaint. Nurses need objective documentation, one coordinated plan, treatment of immediate illness, and respectful nonpunitive engagement that reduces harm without accusing the patient from suspicion alone.

Definition

Factitious disorder imposed on self is a mental disorder in which a person intentionally falsifies physical or psychological signs or symptoms, induces illness or injury, or misrepresents health information and presents themselves to others as ill, impaired, or injured. Identified deception is required, and the behavior is not explained solely by obvious external incentives. The person need not be able to describe a conscious sick-role motive, genuine illness may coexist, and a difficult history, frequent care, inconsistency, or negative testing alone is not diagnostic.

Pathology

The disorder involves deliberate production or falsification of illness behavior, but the psychological motives can be complex, mixed, poorly understood, or not consciously articulated. Repeated presentation across settings can lead to invasive tests, medications, procedures, infection, bleeding, organ injury, and other iatrogenic harm. Diagnosis therefore depends on multiple objective facts, a longitudinal chronology, careful exclusion of genuine disease and misunderstandings, and separation from malingering, in which a clear external incentive is the central purpose. The label must never become permission to deny future medical assessment.

Pathophysiology

  • There is no single established biologic mechanism. Illness behavior, emotional needs, prior adversity, attachment, identity, coping, healthcare experience, and coexisting mental disorders may interact differently across patients.

  • Intentional falsification describes the behavior; it does not mean clinicians can reliably infer the person's complete motive, moral character, future behavior, or capacity from one encounter.

  • Repeated tests and treatments may reinforce the cycle and create real disease, making later assessment more complex because authentic and induced findings can coexist.

Etiology

Etiology is uncertain and multifactorial. Associations may include prior illness or healthcare exposure, trauma or adversity, disrupted relationships, maladaptive coping, identity needs, depression, personality pathology, substance use, or other mental illness, but none proves the disorder. Clinicians should not require a consciously stated desire to occupy the sick role, assume all behavior has one motive, or infer deception from social disadvantage, difficult communication, or unexplained symptoms.

Risk Factors

  • Prior extensive healthcare exposure, recurrent unexplained illness episodes, multiple facilities, fragmented records, or access to medical knowledge or supplies; these are assessment clues, not proof

  • Depression, trauma-related symptoms, personality pathology, substance use, self-harm, suicidality, social isolation, or unstable relationships that may coexist

  • History of repeated invasive tests, lines, wounds, infections, bleeding, hypoglycemia, medication toxicity, or other complications whose timeline requires objective reconciliation

  • Genuine chronic disease, disability, rare illness, poor access, health-literacy barriers, or fragmented care that can create apparent inconsistency and must not be misclassified

Signs Symptoms

  • Objective discrepancies among reported history, observed function, examination, laboratory trends, records, collateral information, and response to supervised care

  • Evidence of falsified information, manipulated samples or devices, self-induced injury or illness, or covert interference with treatment; suspicion without evidence is insufficient

  • Repeated presentation as ill or injured across settings, changing histories, unusual course, or acceptance of risky procedures may prompt review but are not diagnostic alone

  • Real symptoms, chronic disease, treatment complications, depression, anxiety, trauma symptoms, substance use, self-harm, or suicidality may coexist and require treatment

  • The pattern may be episodic or persistent; the person may deny the behavior even when objective evidence is strong

Diagnostics

  • Confirm intentional falsification or induction through converging objective evidence, confirm presentation of self as ill or injured, assess whether obvious external rewards are the sole explanation, and exclude psychosis or another condition that better explains the behavior.

  • Construct one source-attributed chronology using direct observations, original laboratory and procedure data, medication administration, outside records, and collateral information obtained lawfully and with attention to privacy.

  • Evaluate every current symptom and injury on its own evidence. Genuine disease, complications of prior care, and induced illness can coexist, and a factitious diagnosis does not make later complaints false.

  • Use multidisciplinary review—primary team, nursing, psychiatry, relevant specialists, pharmacy, laboratory, ethics, risk management, and social work as indicated—before a high-stakes conclusion or coordinated communication.

Labs

  • No laboratory test, imaging study, psychological scale, record pattern, or validity measure independently diagnoses factitious disorder.

  • Use targeted studies to identify and treat actual illness, injury, infection, bleeding, hypoglycemia, medication toxicity, device complications, or other suspected harm; avoid repeating low-value invasive testing without a coordinated indication.

  • Interpret discordant samples or results with the laboratory and clinical team, verify collection and chain of observation when appropriate, and document facts without publicly exposing security-sensitive details.

Assessment

Stabilize actual illness and injury first. Assess airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, glucose when indicated, infection, bleeding, wounds, device integrity, medication and substance exposure, self-harm, suicide, and access to means. Record exact observations and sources, reconcile the longitudinal timeline, identify genuine diagnoses and treatment complications, and explore the patient's goals and distress without accusation. Separate factitious behavior from misunderstanding, poor health literacy, inconsistent access, somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety, FND, psychosis, substance-related behavior, and malingering.

Differential

  • Genuine medical or psychiatric disease, rare or evolving illness, treatment complication, inconsistent access, communication barrier, fragmented records, misunderstanding, or poor health literacy

  • Somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder, and functional neurological symptom disorder, in which symptoms or illness concerns are not intentionally produced

  • Malingering, which is intentional symptom production for a clear external incentive and is not a mental disorder; secondary gain alone does not settle the distinction

  • Delusional disorder, psychosis, obsessive-compulsive disorder, body-focused behavior, self-harm, eating disorder, substance use, medication-seeking behavior, or personality pathology

  • Factitious disorder imposed on another, in which another person is presented as ill and victim safeguarding becomes the immediate priority

Treatments

  • Treat current illness, injury, infection, toxicity, withdrawal, pain, and other genuine needs according to objective findings; reduce nonbeneficial tests, procedures, and medications through one coordinated plan.

  • Use a consistent, nonpunitive, nonconfrontational approach focused on safety and shared goals. When evidence is reviewed with the patient, present specific facts calmly and offer continued care rather than demanding a confession.

  • Designate a coordinating clinician or team, reconcile records and medicines, define how new symptoms will be evaluated, and communicate the plan across settings within privacy and legal limits.

  • Offer psychotherapy and treatment for separately diagnosed depression, trauma symptoms, substance use, personality pathology, or other needs when the patient engages; evidence for one universal factitious-disorder therapy is limited.

  • No medication treats the core deceptive behavior. Medication is used only for separately diagnosed conditions or genuine medical needs, with attention to overdose, tampering, interactions, and iatrogenic risk.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose from frequent visits, dramatic presentation, medical knowledge, normal tests, inconsistent history, treatment refusal, clinician frustration, or a single suspicious event.

  • Do not shame, ridicule, threaten, publicly expose, or conduct an impulsive solo confrontation; these responses can increase flight from care, self-harm, and fragmented presentation.

  • Do not withhold emergency assessment, analgesia, essential medication, or treatment of objective disease because the diagnosis appears in the record.

  • Do not perform unnecessary invasive tests or procedures simply to prove deception, and do not use covert surveillance or disclosure outside applicable law, policy, ethics, and authorization.

Safety Considerations

  • Assess immediate consequences of induced illness, including hemorrhage, infection, hypoglycemia, poisoning, overdose, wounds, device injury, organ damage, and complications of unnecessary procedures.

  • Assess suicide, self-harm, substance use, access to medications or medical supplies, and escalating behavior; factitious behavior can be life-threatening even without stated suicidal intent.

  • Use one reconciled medication and procedure plan, supervised administration or specimen collection only when clinically justified, and the least restrictive method consistent with current safety.

  • Labeling must be specific, source-attributed, and reviewable. Diagnosis alone does not prove every symptom false or authorize restriction, searches, observation, involuntary treatment, or disclosure.

Nursing Priorities

  • Treat objective illness and injury first, monitor for complications of induced symptoms and prior care, and report acute deterioration through the appropriate medical pathway.

  • Document objective findings, exact statements, observed behavior, specimen and device findings, medication administration, visitors, and responses to care without pejorative terms or unverified motive claims.

  • Maintain a consistent plan across shifts and facilities, reconcile medicines and procedures, reduce unnecessary invasive care, and avoid splitting or contradictory messages.

  • Use calm, respectful, nonconfrontational communication that sets clear boundaries and offers continued care; do not seek a confession or argue about motive at the bedside.

  • Coordinate primary care, specialists, psychiatry, pharmacy, laboratory, social work, ethics, risk management, and crisis services as indicated.

Red Flags

  • Hemodynamic instability, severe bleeding, sepsis, hypoglycemia, poisoning, overdose, respiratory compromise, device disruption, serious wound, or organ injury

  • Suicidal intent, escalating self-injury, access to lethal means or high-risk medical supplies, severe depression, psychosis, intoxication, or withdrawal

  • Rapid accumulation of invasive procedures, high-risk medicines, lines, radiation, surgery, or transfers without one reconciled indication and plan

  • Threats, humiliation, unsafe confrontation, abrupt discharge, or refusal of genuine care based only on suspicion or an old label

Complications

  • Infection, bleeding, poisoning, hypoglycemia, wounds, disability, organ damage, overdose, surgery, device injury, treatment adverse effects, and death

  • Suicide, self-harm, depression, trauma symptoms, substance use, social isolation, and repeated crises

  • Iatrogenic harm from duplicative testing, radiation, polypharmacy, invasive procedures, hospitalization, and delayed recognition of genuine disease

  • Stigma, adversarial care, fragmented records, loss to follow-up, financial and occupational harm, and dismissal of future objective illness

Prognosis

Course may be episodic, recurrent, or persistent, and evidence for specific treatment is limited. Some patients engage in coordinated care and reduce harmful behavior, while others move between settings or decline follow-up. Prognosis depends on severity of induced harm, comorbid illness, therapeutic alliance, continuity of care, and willingness to accept help. A later genuine illness remains possible, and the diagnosis should be revisited when evidence changes.

Prevention

  • There is no proven universal prevention. Coordinated records, one care plan, careful medication and procedure review, and early nonpunitive mental-health support may reduce repeated iatrogenic harm.

  • Use source-attributed documentation and cross-setting communication within privacy rules so clinicians can recognize patterns without converting suspicion into fact.

  • Treat genuine medical and psychiatric needs, reduce avoidable invasive interventions, and plan safe follow-up rather than abruptly terminating care.

Patient Education

  • The care team will treat real illness and injury while trying to reduce tests, medicines, and procedures that may cause more harm. The plan should stay respectful and specific.

  • A factitious-disorder diagnosis requires evidence of intentional falsification or induction; it should not be based only on frequent visits, puzzling symptoms, or normal tests.

  • No medicine treats the core behavior, but therapy and treatment of depression, trauma symptoms, substance use, or other identified conditions may help.

  • Seek urgent help for severe bleeding, infection, low-glucose symptoms, overdose, breathing difficulty, suicidal intent, serious self-harm, or another new emergency.

Special Populations

  • Children and adolescents require developmentally informed assessment, family and safeguarding review, and careful separation of self-imposed behavior from illness fabricated by a caregiver.

  • Older adults may have delirium, neurocognitive disease, polypharmacy, genuine complex illness, or dependence on caregivers; new symptoms require complete medical assessment.

  • Pregnancy and postpartum presentations require maternal, fetal or infant safety, obstetric coordination, medication and substance review, and nonjudgmental suicide and self-harm assessment.

  • People with rare disease, chronic illness, disability, limited health literacy, language barriers, unstable housing, or fragmented access are at particular risk of harmful mislabeling.

Nclex Traps

  • Identified deception is required. Frequent visits, dramatic symptoms, inconsistency, clinician frustration, and normal tests do not diagnose factitious disorder.

  • Obvious external reward as the sole purpose points toward malingering; clinicians should not pretend they can read a person's complete internal motive.

  • Genuine disease and factitious behavior can coexist. Treat objective emergencies and reassess new symptoms rather than dismissing all complaints.

  • Use objective, source-attributed documentation and a coordinated nonconfrontational plan; do not shame the patient or demand confession.

  • There is no core medication. Minimize iatrogenic harm and treat separately diagnosed medical and mental-health conditions.
