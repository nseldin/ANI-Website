Summary

The heart is two pressure-linked pumps arranged in series: the right atrium and right ventricle send systemic venous blood through the lungs, while the left atrium and left ventricle send oxygenated pulmonary venous blood through the body. Four valves open and close because pressure changes across them, not because the valves actively pull themselves open. Understanding that one-way route explains murmurs, congestion, shunts, oxygenation patterns, and why a problem on one side eventually affects the other.

Quick Answer

Trace one red blood cell: venae cavae to right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve, and aorta. Arteries are named for carrying blood away from the heart and veins for carrying blood toward it, so the pulmonary artery is oxygen-poor and the pulmonary veins are oxygen-rich. A valve opens when pressure behind it exceeds pressure ahead and closes when that gradient reverses. The right ventricle normally pumps against low pulmonary resistance; the thicker left ventricle generates pressure for systemic circulation.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    The heart lies in the mediastinum, mostly left of midline, within the pericardial sac. The atria are thin-walled receiving chambers; the ventricles are ejecting chambers. The interatrial and interventricular septa normally separate oxygen-poor right-sided blood from oxygen-rich left-sided blood. The tricuspid valve lies between right atrium and ventricle, the pulmonary valve between right ventricle and pulmonary trunk, the mitral valve between left atrium and ventricle, and the aortic valve between left ventricle and aorta. Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles restrain atrioventricular valve leaflets during ventricular contraction; they do not open the valves. Semilunar valves have no chordae.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: why flow moves forward

    Text

    Blood moves down pressure gradients. Systemic venous return raises right atrial pressure enough to fill the right ventricle through the open tricuspid valve. Ventricular contraction raises right ventricular pressure, closes the tricuspid valve, and then opens the pulmonary valve when ventricular pressure exceeds pulmonary-artery pressure. The same sequence occurs at higher pressure on the left. Valve closure prevents backward flow while the chamber behind the valve is generating or losing pressure. This is why stenosis creates an upstream pressure load and regurgitation creates volume moving in the wrong direction; the location of the valve predicts which chamber first dilates or hypertrophies.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    Filling depends on venous return, relaxation, atrial contribution, valve patency, and enough diastolic time. Ejection depends on coordinated electrical activation, myocardial contractility, valve opening, and the pressure the ventricle must overcome. The pulmonary and systemic circuits are in series, so sustained output must match over time: if the left ventricle cannot eject what the right ventricle delivers, pressure backs into pulmonary veins and lungs. If the right ventricle fails, systemic venous pressure rises. For quantitative treatment of preload, afterload, stroke volume, and cardiac output, follow the dedicated connected entries rather than treating chamber anatomy as a duplicate hemodynamics card.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Left-sided filling or ejection failure raises left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, encouraging fluid to leave pulmonary capillaries and causing exertional dyspnea, crackles, orthopnea, or pulmonary edema. Right-sided failure raises systemic venous pressure, producing jugular venous distention, dependent edema, hepatic congestion, and ascites. Mitral or aortic stenosis obstructs forward flow; regurgitation sends part of each stroke backward and makes the ventricle pump extra volume. Septal defects permit shunting according to pressure and resistance. Acute valve failure is especially dangerous because chambers have not had time to adapt, so shock or pulmonary edema may develop abruptly.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Diuretics can reduce congestion but do not repair a stenotic or leaking valve. Vasodilators can change the resistance against which the left ventricle ejects, while inotropes may temporarily support a failing pump; their exact use depends on the disease and blood pressure. Endocarditis can destroy leaflets, rheumatic disease can scar valves, calcification can narrow the aortic valve, and ischemia can impair papillary muscles. Congenital malformations alter chambers, valves, or septa. Prosthetic valves introduce thrombosis, bleeding, and infection considerations. A medication that slows rate may improve filling time in some conditions but can worsen perfusion when output already depends on heart rate.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    Auscultation suggests timing and location but echocardiography shows chamber size, wall motion, valve anatomy, pressure gradients, regurgitant flow, and estimated ejection performance. Doppler links visible movement to flow velocity. ECG evaluates rhythm and conduction rather than valve anatomy. Chest imaging may show enlargement or pulmonary congestion. Cardiac biomarkers answer injury or wall-stress questions, not simply whether a valve opens. Oxygen saturation cannot identify every shunt, and a normal resting saturation does not exclude important structural disease. Cardiac catheterization measures pressures and oxygen content directly when noninvasive information is insufficient or an intervention is planned.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Assess heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure, pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, mentation, urine output, lung sounds, work of breathing, edema, jugular venous pressure, daily weight, and activity tolerance. These observations connect forward flow with upstream congestion. Compare findings over time and position: new crackles after fluids, falling urine output, narrowing pulse pressure, or increasing oxygen need may reveal deteriorating pump function earlier than a single lab. Administer ordered medicines while watching the physiologic target and adverse response. For prosthetic valves, reinforce anticoagulation and endocarditis-prevention instructions specific to the device and current guideline, and never imply that every murmur requires the same treatment.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Escalate immediately for chest pain with instability, syncope with exertion, sudden severe dyspnea, pink frothy sputum, rapidly rising oxygen requirement, hypotension with cool or altered patient, new cyanosis, sustained dangerous dysrhythmia, or a new murmur after infarction, infection, or procedure. Sudden pulmonary edema can reflect acute mitral or aortic regurgitation. Muffled heart sounds with hypotension and venous distention may indicate pericardial tamponade rather than ordinary heart failure. After valve intervention, new neurologic deficit, absent pulse, major bleeding, fever, conduction change, or hemodynamic collapse requires emergency evaluation.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    Do not label arteries as oxygen-rich and veins as oxygen-poor; direction relative to the heart determines the name. Do not reverse the mitral and tricuspid valves: mitral is left, tricuspid is right. Papillary muscles prevent prolapse during systole; they do not snap leaflets open. S1 mainly reflects atrioventricular valve closure and S2 semilunar valve closure, but sound intensity alone does not diagnose severity. A patient can have serious valve disease without a dramatic murmur, and a loud murmur is not automatically the most severe lesion. Anatomy establishes the route; pressure, timing, and resistance explain the symptoms.
