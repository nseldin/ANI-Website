Definition

Ecchymosis is an observed skin bleeding sign. It is a clue to a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease. A positive finding means a flat nonblanching blue, purple, brown, green, or yellow area consistent with a larger bruise is present.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Ecchymosis means a flat nonblanching blue, purple, brown, green, or yellow area consistent with a larger bruise is present.

Why It Matters

The observed Ecchymosis finding can signal a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease. Blood escaping from damaged vessels spreads through subcutaneous tissue and is gradually broken down, producing a larger color-changing area that does not blanch. Ecchymosis cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease

Assessment Method

Inspect and gently palpate the discolored area, document size, location, tenderness, swelling, pattern, and change over time, and ask about trauma, procedures, medications, and other bleeding.

Positive Finding

A flat nonblanching blue, purple, brown, green, or yellow area consistent with a larger bruise is present.

Mechanism

Blood escaping from damaged vessels spreads through subcutaneous tissue and is gradually broken down, producing a larger color-changing area that does not blanch.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Ecchymosis finding raises concern for a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease because blood escaping from damaged vessels spreads through subcutaneous tissue and is gradually broken down, producing a larger color-changing area that does not blanch. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Ecchymosis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Ecchymosis to focus evaluation of a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease and the reviewed alternatives: hematoma, vascular lesion, postinflammatory pigmentation, Cullen sign. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect and gently palpate the discolored area, document size, location, tenderness, swelling, pattern, and change over time, and ask about trauma, procedures, medications, and other bleeding. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Ecchymosis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Color and size cannot reliably date a bruise or identify its cause; edema, skin pigmentation, lighting, and prior lesions affect appearance. Therefore, Ecchymosis cannot confirm or exclude a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • hematoma

  • vascular lesion

  • postinflammatory pigmentation

  • Cullen sign

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as hematoma, vascular lesion, postinflammatory pigmentation, Cullen sign.

False Negative Situations

Ecchymosis may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of a larger flat area of blood beneath the skin caused by trauma, medication effects, platelet or coagulation disorders, vascular fragility, or systemic disease.

Modern Status

Current common morphology; unexplained, patterned, rapidly enlarging, or widespread bruising can reveal significant bleeding, trauma, medication toxicity, or abuse and needs context-sensitive evaluation. Safety Precautions

Observe Ecchymosis with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect and gently palpate the discolored area, document size, location, tenderness, swelling, pattern, and change over time, and ask about trauma, procedures, medications, and other bleeding. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Escalate rapidly expanding bruising, severe pain or swelling, neurovascular change, major trauma, anticoagulant use with bleeding, signs of abuse, hypotension, or bleeding at multiple sites; do not use bruise color to assign an exact injury date.
