Pathology

Low PaCO2 constricts cerebral vessels, reducing cerebral blood flow and causing lightheadedness, confusion, or syncope. Alkalemia increases calcium binding to albumin, lowering ionized calcium and producing perioral tingling, carpopedal spasm, or tetany even when total calcium is normal. It also shifts potassium and phosphate into cells, which can cause weakness and dysrhythmia. Dismissing the pattern as anxiety can miss pulmonary embolism, sepsis, hypoxemia, pregnancy complications, or salicylate toxicity.

Etiology

Hypoxemia from pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pulmonary edema, high altitude, or severe anemia; early sepsis and fever; pain, panic, pregnancy, liver disease, CNS disease, mechanical overventilation, and early salicylate toxicity can drive hyperventilation. Metabolic acidosis also lowers PaCO2 as compensation and must not be mislabeled primary respiratory alkalosis unless ventilation exceeds the expected range.

Signs Symptoms

  • Tachypnea, lightheadedness, perioral or finger tingling, chest discomfort, palpitations, tremor, carpopedal spasm, confusion, or syncope can occur.

  • Pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, unilateral leg swelling, hypoxemia, fever, hypotension, pregnancy danger signs, tinnitus, or vomiting points beyond simple panic.

  • Chronic pregnancy or liver-disease patterns can be relatively asymptomatic despite low PaCO2.

Diagnostics

  • Confirm a primary low-PaCO2 process, assess oxygenation and perfusion, and compare HCO3 with acute or chronic expected adaptation.

  • Evaluate pulmonary embolism, infection, hypoxemia, pregnancy, liver disease, CNS disease, salicylate exposure, pain, and ventilator settings before assigning anxiety.

  • Calculate anion gap and measure salicylate, lactate, ketones, and electrolytes when HCO3 is lower than expected or a mixed disorder is possible.

Treatments

  • Treat the driver: restore oxygenation, evaluate and treat PE or infection, control fever and pain, adjust excessive ventilator minute ventilation, and manage salicylate toxicity or CNS disease.

  • Use calm coaching for panic only after dangerous causes have been assessed; avoid paper-bag rebreathing because it can worsen unrecognized hypoxemia.

  • Correct severe electrolyte consequences and use toxicology or dialysis pathways for salicylates according to clinical criteria rather than waiting for pH to become acidemic.

Nursing Priorities

  • Confirm pH, carbon dioxide, bicarbonate, oxygenation, timing, and expected acute or chronic compensation because low carbon dioxide may be primary hyperventilation or compensation for metabolic acidosis.

  • Assess respiratory effort, pain, fever, pregnancy, altitude, ventilator settings, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, asthma, neurologic disease, and salicylates because anxiety is a diagnosis of exclusion in dangerous presentations.

  • Trend blood gases, respiratory rate, saturation, ECG, potassium, ionized calcium, lactate, mental status, and cause-specific findings because alkalemia can reduce cerebral blood flow and provoke paresthesias, spasm, or dysrhythmia.

  • Treat hypoxemia, pain, fever, sepsis, obstruction, toxin exposure, or excessive mechanical ventilation as ordered and coach calm breathing only after urgent causes are assessed because paper-bag rebreathing can worsen hidden hypoxia.

  • Escalate for chest pain, syncope, hypoxemia, focal deficit, seizure, severe alkalemia, shock, suspected embolism, or salicylate toxicity because the underlying cause, not the low carbon dioxide itself, often determines mortality.

Complications

  • Cerebral vasoconstriction can cause syncope, confusion, or seizure in severe alkalemia.

  • Low ionized calcium, potassium, and phosphate can cause tetany, weakness, dysrhythmia, or respiratory-muscle dysfunction.

  • Missed PE, sepsis, hypoxemia, or salicylate toxicity can progress despite transient reassurance from a high pH.

Patient Education

  • Do not breathe into a paper bag unless a clinician specifically directs it because hidden lung or clot disease may already be limiting oxygen.

  • Tingling and hand spasm can come from rapid carbon-dioxide loss, but new chest pain, fainting, or neurologic symptoms still need urgent evaluation.

Nclex Traps

  • Respiratory alkalosis may be the earliest acid-base clue in sepsis or pulmonary embolism.

  • Low PaCO2 in DKA can be appropriate compensation, not a second diagnosis.

  • Salicylate toxicity classically creates respiratory alkalosis plus high-gap metabolic acidosis.

Definition

Respiratory alkalosis is a primary fall in PaCO2 caused by alveolar ventilation that exceeds carbon dioxide production. CO2 loss lowers dissolved carbonic acid and raises the bicarbonate-to-CO2 ratio. Acute disease usually causes alkalemia; chronic hyperventilation stimulates renal bicarbonate loss and can return pH toward the reference interval without removing the cause.

Pathophysiology

Acute CO2 removal raises pH before kidneys can excrete bicarbonate. As screening estimates, acute respiratory alkalosis lowers HCO3 about 2 mEq/L and raises pH about 0.08 for every 10-mm Hg PaCO2 fall below 40. Chronic adaptation lowers HCO3 about 4-5 and raises pH about 0.03 per 10-mm Hg. HCO3 lower than expected suggests concurrent metabolic acidosis; higher suggests metabolic alkalosis. In salicylate toxicity, direct brainstem respiratory stimulation causes respiratory alkalosis while uncoupled metabolism and organic acids create high-gap metabolic acidosis, so pH can look deceptively near normal.

Risk Factors

  • Pulmonary embolism risk, hypoxemia, high altitude, pneumonia, edema, and mechanical ventilation increase respiratory drive or imposed ventilation.

  • Sepsis, fever, pregnancy, liver failure, pain, panic, stroke, meningitis, and other CNS stimulation can lower PaCO2.

  • Salicylates and selected toxins or medications can create respiratory alkalosis alone or as part of a mixed disorder.

Labs

  • Primary pattern is low PaCO2 with elevated pH in acute disease; chronic adaptation lowers HCO3 and brings pH closer to normal.

  • Ionized calcium, potassium, magnesium, and phosphate can fall or shift during significant alkalemia and explain neuromuscular findings.

  • PaO2 or reliable arterial oxygenation assessment is needed when hypoxemia is possible; venous PO2 is not a substitute.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose anxiety until hypoxemia, PE, sepsis, pregnancy, salicylate, and ventilator causes are considered.

  • Do not use paper-bag rebreathing.

  • Do not label low PaCO2 as primary respiratory alkalosis when it falls within expected compensation for metabolic acidosis.

Red Flags

  • Chest pain, syncope, hemoptysis, unilateral leg swelling, or suspected pulmonary embolism

  • Hypoxemia, severe respiratory distress, absent breath sounds, or ventilator instability

  • Confusion, focal neurologic deficit, seizure, severe headache, or recent head injury

  • Tinnitus, vomiting, fever, mixed acid-base pattern, or suspected salicylate toxicity
