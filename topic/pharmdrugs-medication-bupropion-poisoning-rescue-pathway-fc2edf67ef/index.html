Used To Treat

Bupropion exposure with agitation, tremor, seizure, QRS or QT abnormality, ventricular dysrhythmia, hypotension, or cardiogenic shock. There is no known antidote; rescue is supportive, with benzodiazepines, airway care, selected decontamination, vasopressors, and early ECMO consideration for refractory cardiotoxicity.

Description

Bupropion is a norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake-inhibiting antidepressant whose sustained- and extended-release products can produce delayed seizures, conduction abnormalities, and cardiogenic shock, so an early normal ECG does not end monitoring. Agitation, tremor, hallucinations, tachycardia, clonus, or vomiting may precede recurrent seizures, coma, QRS or QT prolongation, ventricular dysrhythmia, and cardiogenic shock. The FDA label states that no known antidote exists and reports seizures in about one third of described overdoses. Continuous observation must therefore be formulation- and symptom-aware rather than ending after an early normal ECG. Benzodiazepines are first-line seizure treatment. Sodium bicarbonate is reasonable when sodium-channel blockade or a mixed ingestion is possible, but bupropion QRS widening may reflect impaired gap-junction conduction and can respond poorly. Lipid emulsion and VA-ECMO are rescue considerations with low-quality evidence, not routine antidotes.

Mechanism

Bupropion and hydroxybupropion inhibit norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake and lower seizure threshold. At toxic concentrations, catecholaminergic excitation contributes to agitation, tachycardia, and seizure, while cardiac ion-channel and gap-junction effects slow conduction, prolong repolarization, and impair contractility. Benzodiazepines restore inhibitory GABA-A signaling but do not remove drug. Activated charcoal or selected whole-bowel irrigation can reduce unabsorbed extended-release burden only when timing, bowel function, and airway safety are favorable. VA-ECMO does not detoxify bupropion; it temporarily replaces failing circulation while distribution and metabolism reduce the toxic effect.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 and Poison Help for intentional, large, symptomatic, or unknown bupropion exposure. Place on continuous ECG and seizure precautions, check glucose and electrolytes, and use benzodiazepines promptly for seizure or severe agitation while securing oxygenation and ventilation.

  • Consider activated charcoal early and whole-bowel irrigation only for selected substantial sustained- or extended-release exposures after toxicology review and airway protection. Never induce vomiting and do not intubate solely to perform decontamination without a clear benefit-risk rationale.

  • Treat hypotension and dysrhythmia by physiology and current toxicology guidance. When severe ventricular dysfunction or shock is worsening despite conventional support, contact a VA-ECMO center early; lipid emulsion is an evidence-limited salvage option that can complicate labs and extracorporeal circuits.

Nursing Essentials

  • Confirm immediate-, sustained-, or extended-release product, tablet strength, amount, time, co-ingestants, weight, and preexisting seizure or eating disorder. Similar brand names do not imply the same release kinetics.

  • Maintain padded seizure precautions, frequent neurologic checks, continuous ECG, reliable IV access, aspiration precautions, and temperature monitoring. Recurrent seizure or sudden cardiovascular collapse can follow a period of apparent stability.

  • If lipid emulsion is considered, coordinate toxicology, pharmacy, laboratory, and ECMO teams because hyperlipemia can interfere with assays and oxygenator function. Document the uncertain rationale and response.

Key Labs

  • Serial ECG with QRS and QTc, glucose, electrolytes, magnesium, calcium, blood gas, lactate, CK, creatinine, liver studies, and acetaminophen or other co-ingestion testing. Bedside echocardiography helps distinguish vasodilatory from cardiogenic shock.

  • Routine bupropion concentrations are not useful for early bedside decisions. A normal first ECG or absence of early seizure does not clear an XL exposure.

Adverse Effects

  • Poisoning can cause agitation, hallucination, vomiting, clonus, myoclonus, recurrent seizure, status epilepticus, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, QRS or QT prolongation, ventricular dysrhythmia, cardiogenic shock, coma, respiratory failure, arrest, and death.

  • Benzodiazepines can depress ventilation; bicarbonate can cause alkalemia and sodium load; vasopressors can worsen ischemia; lipid emulsion can cause pancreatitis, fat overload, assay interference, and extracorporeal-circuit problems.

Contraindications

  • Do not rely on phenytoin as first-line treatment for toxin-induced bupropion seizures; benzodiazepines and correction of metabolic drivers are preferred under toxicology protocols.

  • Do not assume every wide QRS will normalize with bicarbonate, and do not call lipid emulsion a proven antidote. Do not discharge an asymptomatic extended-release ingestion after only brief observation.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Repeated seizure, status epilepticus, rising QRS or QT, ventricular dysrhythmia, lactate, hypotension, reduced ejection fraction, or vasopressor escalation requires ICU, toxicology, continuous EEG as indicated, and early ECMO-center consultation.

Interactions

  • MAO inhibitors, stimulants, antipsychotics, theophylline, tramadol, withdrawal, and other seizure-threshold-lowering drugs increase neurologic risk. Other sodium- or potassium-channel blockers increase ECG toxicity.

  • Strong CYP2B6 modifiers can alter therapeutic metabolite patterns, but acute overdose management is guided by physiology rather than waiting for pharmacokinetic predictions.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Children

    Note

    Exploratory ingestion still requires poison-center guidance; adolescent intentional exposure and adult XL tablets can produce delayed seizures.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal seizure, hypoxia, and shock require immediate stabilization; salvage therapies need multidisciplinary risk-benefit decisions.

  • Population

    Seizure disorder, eating disorder, or sedative withdrawal

    Note

    Baseline seizure risk is higher because seizure disorders, eating disorders, and sedative withdrawal all lower the threshold for convulsions; therapeutic bupropion may already be contraindicated, and overdose warrants cautious prolonged monitoring.

Evidence Limitations

  • The FDA label confirms severe delayed neurologic and cardiac toxicity and no known antidote. Bicarbonate response is inconsistent, prophylactic sedation is not standardized, and lipid emulsion and ECMO evidence is mainly case-based. AHA 2025 does not provide a dedicated bupropion algorithm.

Nclex Traps

  • Extended-release bupropion can cause delayed seizures after an initially normal examination.

  • QRS widening may reflect gap-junction as well as sodium-channel effects, so bicarbonate can fail.

  • VA-ECMO supports circulation while toxicity resolves; it is not an antidote or proof that lipid should be given first.

Regulatory Status

No FDA-approved bupropion antidote exists. Benzodiazepines, bicarbonate trials, decontamination, lipid emulsion, and VA-ECMO are supportive or off-label toxicology interventions selected by physiology and expert judgment.

Boxed Warning

BUPROPION-OVERDOSE WARNING: seizure and cardiogenic shock can be delayed, especially after SR or XL products. Call 911 and Poison Help, use continuous ECG and seizure monitoring, and activate ECMO expertise early for worsening refractory shock; sodium bicarbonate and lipid emulsion are uncertain rescue tools, not guaranteed antidotes.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning names the emergency, explains why delay or a wrong rescue choice is dangerous, and separates proven antidotes from supportive or uncertain interventions.
