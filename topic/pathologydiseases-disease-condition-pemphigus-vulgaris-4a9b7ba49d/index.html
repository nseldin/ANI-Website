Definition

Pemphigus vulgaris is a chronic autoimmune blistering disease in which antibodies disrupt adhesion between epidermal keratinocytes. It typically begins with painful oral erosions and can cause fragile, flaccid skin blisters and widespread erosions; infection, fluid loss, malnutrition, and immunosuppressive-treatment toxicity create the major safety burden.

Pathology

IgG autoantibodies target desmosomal adhesion proteins, primarily desmoglein 3 and often desmoglein 1. Loss of cell-to-cell attachment, called acantholysis, creates a suprabasal split inside the epidermis, so the blister roof is thin and ruptures easily rather than remaining tense.

Pathophysiology

  • Desmoglein 3 is especially important in mucosal epithelium, so dominant anti-desmoglein 3 activity often produces painful oral disease. Additional desmoglein 1 involvement permits more extensive skin disease.

  • Because erosions remove a barrier to microbes and increase fluid, protein, heat, and pain burden, a skin-limited appearance can still become systemic illness. Immunosuppression reduces antibody-driven damage but adds infection and organ-toxicity risk.

Etiology

Pemphigus vulgaris arises from immune and genetic susceptibility, and most cases have no single external cause. Selected medications have been associated with pemphigus, but timing and phenotype require specialist review; a drug exposure is not automatically causal and essential therapy should not be stopped without a plan.

Risk Factors

  • Middle or older adulthood, although any age can be affected

  • Genetic and ethnic susceptibility in selected populations

  • Personal or family tendency toward autoimmune disease

  • Exposure to a potentially associated medication in a compatible clinical timeline

Signs Symptoms

  • Painful, persistent oral erosions that may precede skin disease by weeks or months

  • Flaccid bullae that rupture easily, leaving moist, crusted, painful erosions on otherwise normal-appearing or erythematous skin

  • Positive Nikolsky phenomenon in active skin, which supports superficial epidermal fragility but is not specific to pemphigus

  • Dysphagia, odynophagia, hoarseness, poor intake, weight loss, genital lesions, or less commonly ocular involvement

  • Fever, purulence, spreading erythema, malodor, or systemic deterioration when secondary infection develops

Diagnostics

  • Obtain a lesional biopsy for routine histology and a separate perilesional biopsy from intact skin or mucosa for direct immunofluorescence.

  • Expected findings are suprabasal acantholysis on histology and intercellular epidermal IgG, often with C3, on direct immunofluorescence.

  • Serum desmoglein 3 and desmoglein 1 ELISA and indirect immunofluorescence support diagnosis and can help follow activity, but are interpreted with the examination and biopsy.

  • Map mucosal and skin extent, document new-blister formation, and evaluate swallowing, ocular, genital, infection, nutrition, and medication-risk domains before choosing treatment.

Labs

  • CBC, metabolic panel, glucose, liver and kidney tests establish baseline infection, fluid, electrolyte, and treatment risk.

  • Screen for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, tuberculosis, pregnancy, and other risks according to the planned biologic or immunosuppressive therapy.

  • Desmoglein antibody levels may track disease activity but should not be used alone to declare remission or relapse.

  • Culture a clinically infected erosion rather than treating every crust or positive skin swab as invasive infection.

Assessment

Inspect oral, pharyngeal, skin, scalp, genital, and when symptomatic ocular sites; quantify new lesions and body-surface involvement; assess pain, swallowing, hydration, weight, urine output, temperature, wound appearance, and functional effect. Reconcile immunosuppressants and screen for infection before escalating treatment.

Differential

Differentiate pemphigus vulgaris from pemphigus foliaceus, bullous and mucous-membrane pemphigoid, paraneoplastic pemphigus, linear IgA disease, dermatitis herpetiformis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis, erythema multiforme, drug eruption, herpes infection, and other erosive oral disorders. A positive Nikolsky sign does not settle the diagnosis.

Treatments

  • Rituximab combined with a tapering systemic glucocorticoid is an important first-line strategy for many adults with moderate-to-severe disease under dermatology supervision.

  • Systemic glucocorticoids with a steroid-sparing medicine such as azathioprine or mycophenolate are alternatives for selected patients; topical corticosteroids and supportive care complement systemic therapy for limited areas.

  • Refractory disease may require IV immunoglobulin, immunoadsorption, or another specialist immunomodulatory strategy.

  • Use gentle wound and oral care, pain control, nutrition and fluid support, and treatment of documented secondary infection to protect the disrupted barrier and maintain intake. Antimicrobials do not treat the autoimmune mechanism.

Contraindications

  • Do not diagnose pemphigus from a Nikolsky sign or antibody result alone; tissue and clinical correlation are central.

  • Rituximab requires hepatitis B screening, infection review, vaccine planning, and infusion precautions; active severe infection generally requires stabilization before immunosuppressive escalation.

  • Mycophenolate is teratogenic and requires strict reproductive counseling and current product precautions.

  • Azathioprine requires pretreatment and ongoing hematologic and hepatic safety assessment under the local protocol.

  • Do not apply adhesive dressings or vigorous friction to fragile skin, and do not debride intact roofs routinely as if they were necrotic tissue.

Safety Considerations

  • Widespread erosions can cause fluid, protein, heat, and electrolyte loss and create a portal for sepsis.

  • Systemic glucocorticoids increase infection, glucose, psychiatric, blood-pressure, bone, ocular, gastrointestinal, and adrenal risks.

  • Rituximab can cause infusion reactions, hepatitis B reactivation, serious infection, severe mucocutaneous reactions, and rare progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy.

  • Oral and pharyngeal disease can impair intake or threaten the airway; hoarseness and dyspnea are not routine mouth discomfort.

  • SJS/TEN is an acute drug-related emergency with different management and must not be relabeled pemphigus because both can have denuded skin.

Nursing Priorities

  • Document lesion location, new-blister count, mucosal involvement, body-surface extent, pain, temperature, hydration, weight, intake, urine output, and infection findings using a consistent method.

  • Use gentle handling, nonadherent dressings, prescribed topical therapy, careful oral hygiene, and pain control before meals and wound care.

  • Monitor CBC, glucose, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, infection signs, and treatment-specific screening to detect immunosuppressive toxicity and sepsis early.

  • During rituximab infusion, follow the premedication and reaction protocol and assess fever, rigors, rash, dyspnea, hypoxia, hypotension, or angioedema promptly.

  • Coordinate nutrition, dental or oral medicine, ophthalmology, wound, pharmacy, and infection support when those systems are involved.

Red Flags

  • Fever, hypotension, confusion, rapidly spreading erythema, purulence, or another sepsis sign

  • Inability to drink, oliguria, severe weight loss, electrolyte disturbance, or acute kidney injury

  • Dyspnea, stridor, severe hoarseness, or rapidly progressive pharyngeal involvement

  • Painful red eye, photophobia, or visual change

  • Rapidly expanding denudation or a new drug-associated systemic eruption raising concern for SJS/TEN

Complications

  • Secondary bacterial, viral, or fungal infection and sepsis

  • Fluid, electrolyte, protein, and heat loss

  • Malnutrition, dehydration, severe pain, sleep disruption, and reduced function

  • Scarring or functional damage at selected mucosal sites

  • Glucocorticoid, rituximab, or other immunosuppressive toxicity and recurrent disease

Prognosis

Modern immunosuppressive and B-cell-directed treatment has markedly improved survival, and many patients reach remission. Relapse is common and treatment toxicity can exceed direct skin injury if monitoring is weak. Remission is judged by sustained absence of new lesions and healing under a safe taper, not by one antibody value.

Prevention

There is no proven primary prevention. Reduce complications through early diagnosis, pre-immunosuppression vaccine and infection planning, careful wound and oral care, nutrition support, and prompt treatment of infection. Review potentially associated medicines with the prescriber rather than stopping them abruptly.

Patient Education

  • The blisters are caused by an autoimmune loss of skin-cell adhesion, not by ordinary friction or poor hygiene.

  • Use gentle skin and oral care and avoid adhesive products or trauma that tears fragile epithelium.

  • Report fever, rapidly increasing pain or redness, reduced urine, inability to drink, breathing or eye symptoms, or a severe infusion reaction immediately.

  • Do not stop glucocorticoids suddenly or change immunosuppressive therapy without the prescriber.

Special Populations

  • Pregnancy and pregnancy potential require early review because mycophenolate is teratogenic and other treatment choices also carry reproductive risks.

  • Older or frail adults need aggressive infection, bone, glucose, nutrition, and functional-risk prevention.

  • Children require specialist confirmation and pediatric dosing because pemphigus vulgaris is uncommon in childhood.

  • Patients with hepatitis B exposure, recurrent infection, malignancy, or major organ disease need coordinated treatment selection and monitoring.

Nclex Traps

  • A positive Nikolsky sign is not specific and cannot replace biopsy with direct immunofluorescence.

  • Use a perilesional intact sample for direct immunofluorescence; eroded tissue may be nondiagnostic.

  • Antibiotics treat a secondary infection, not the pemphigus autoantibody mechanism.

  • Pemphigus vulgaris has fragile flaccid intraepidermal blisters; bullous pemphigoid more often has tense subepidermal blisters.
