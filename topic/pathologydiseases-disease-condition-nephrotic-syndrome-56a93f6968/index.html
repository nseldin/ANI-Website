Pathology

Nephrotic syndrome develops when glomerular filtration-barrier injury permits heavy protein loss, lowering oncotic pressure and altering lipid and coagulation pathways. The characteristic pattern is heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, generalized edema, hyperlipidemia, lipiduria, and increased thrombosis and infection risk. Confirm or stage it with urinalysis and quantified protein with serum albumin, creatinine, lipids, and sediment; distinguish it from nephritic syndrome, heart or liver edema, protein-losing enteropathy, malnutrition, and venous obstruction by urine protein, sediment, albumin, and organ findings. The priority is to control sodium and edema with careful diuresis because venous thrombosis can complicate Nephrotic syndrome. can follow if the process advances. Mechanistically, glomerular filtration-barrier injury permits heavy protein loss, lowering oncotic pressure and altering lipid and coagulation pathways.

Etiology

Common causes include hypoperfusion, diabetes/hypertension, infection, obstruction, stones, autoimmune disease, nephrotoxins, and congenital risk.

Signs Symptoms

  • urine output change

  • flank pain or dysuria when infectious/stone

  • edema

  • hypertension

  • fatigue

  • electrolyte symptoms

Diagnostics

  • urinalysis and quantified protein with serum albumin, creatinine, lipids, and sediment

  • cause testing for diabetes, autoimmune, infection, medication, malignancy, or genetic disease

  • kidney biopsy when age, cause, or treatment uncertainty requires tissue

  • Distinguish Nephrotic syndrome from nephritic syndrome, heart or liver edema, protein-losing enteropathy, malnutrition, and venous obstruction by urine protein, sediment, albumin, and organ findings.

Treatments

  • control sodium and edema with careful diuresis

  • reduce proteinuria and treat the glomerular or systemic cause

  • prevent and rapidly treat thrombosis, infection, malnutrition, and medication toxicity according to individual risk

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate for sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, unilateral swelling, fever, peritonitic pain, oliguria, pulmonary edema, or shock because embolism, severe infection, acute kidney injury, or volume complication can progress rapidly.

  • Assess edema distribution, daily weight, blood pressure, urine output and foam, dyspnea, infection, thrombosis symptoms, diabetes, autoimmune disease, medicines, and malignancy risk because nephrotic syndrome is a protein-loss pattern with many causes.

  • Trend urine protein, albumin, creatinine, electrolytes, lipids, blood count, glucose, cause-specific studies, and biopsy results when ordered because severity and etiology determine prognosis and immunosuppressive decisions.

  • Manage sodium and individualized fluid intake, skin protection, mobility, diuretics, renin-angiotensin blockade, lipid therapy, anticoagulation, or immunosuppression as prescribed because edema control must be balanced against intravascular depletion and treatment risks.

  • Monitor leg asymmetry, chest symptoms, temperature, abdominal pain, kidney function, urine output, orthostasis, and medication toxicity because urinary immunoglobulin and anticoagulant-protein loss increases infection and thrombosis while diuresis can reduce perfusion.

Complications

  • venous thrombosis can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

  • pulmonary embolism can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

  • spontaneous infection can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

  • acute kidney injury can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

  • severe edema can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

  • progressive kidney disease can complicate Nephrotic syndrome.

Patient Education

  • Weigh yourself under similar conditions and report rapid gain, falling urine, breathlessness, fever, or one swollen leg because these trends reveal fluid, kidney, infection, or clot complications.

  • Do not change diuretics, fluids, protein supplements, or anti-inflammatory medicines independently because edema can coexist with low effective circulating volume and fragile kidney perfusion.

Nclex Traps

  • Edema does not mean the circulation will tolerate aggressive diuresis

  • Heavy proteinuria with active red-cell casts suggests a mixed or nephritic process

Red Flags

  • Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, or hypoxemia

  • Unilateral leg swelling, severe flank pain, hematuria, or suspected venous thrombosis

  • Fever, rigid or severely tender abdomen, hypotension, or toxic appearance

  • Oliguria, rapidly rising creatinine, severe edema with respiratory compromise, or shock
