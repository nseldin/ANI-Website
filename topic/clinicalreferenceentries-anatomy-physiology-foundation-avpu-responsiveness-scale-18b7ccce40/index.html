Quick Answer

AVPU is a rapid trend and escalation tool, not a substitute for the Glasgow Coma Scale, neurologic examination, airway assessment, or cause-directed evaluation.

Sections

  • Label

    How to assess

    Text

    Observe spontaneous alertness, speak clearly, and assess response according to local neurologic-assessment policy. Use the least harmful effective stimulus and document the exact response rather than only one letter.

  • Label

    Why change matters

    Text

    Movement from alert toward voice, pain, or unresponsive can reflect hypoxemia, hypoglycemia, stroke, seizure, intoxication, infection, shock, trauma, medication effect, or rising intracranial pressure. The trend is often more informative than one isolated category.

  • Label

    Immediate priorities

    Text

    Any new depressed responsiveness requires airway and breathing assessment, glucose when indicated, vital signs, neurologic screening, medication review, and urgent escalation.

  • Label

    Documentation

    Text

    Record baseline, time, stimulus, eye, voice, and motor response, confounders such as sedation or language barrier, and response to interventions.

Summary

AVPU rapidly categorizes whether a person is alert, responds to voice, responds only to an appropriate painful stimulus, or is unresponsive.

    Cue

    Expansion
    • Cue

      A

      Meaning

      Alert. Spontaneous wakefulness and appropriate interaction suggest the highest category of responsiveness.

    • Cue

      V

      Meaning

      Responds to Voice. A response only after speech indicates depressed spontaneous alertness.

    • Cue

      P

      Meaning

      Responds to an Appropriate Painful Stimulus. A response only to pain signals more severe impairment and requires urgent cause assessment.

    • Cue

      U

      Meaning

      Unresponsive. No meaningful response creates immediate airway, breathing, and neurologic risk.

    Id

    mnemonic:avpu

    Limitations

    • AVPU is not a shortened Glasgow Coma Scale.

    • Sedation, paralysis, hearing, language, and disability can confound response.

    • Document the stimulus and behavior, not only the letter.
