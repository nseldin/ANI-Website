Used To Treat

Selected primary hyperlipidemia or severe triglyceride disorders when expected benefit justifies toxicity. It is not routine add-on therapy to an effective statin because improving HDL-C or triglycerides did not reliably add cardiovascular outcomes benefit in modern trials.

Description

Prescription extended-release niacin is gram-range nicotinic acid, not ordinary vitamin B3 supplementation. It changes triglyceride, LDL, and HDL metabolism but also causes dose-dependent flushing, liver injury, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia or gout, hypotension, and muscle toxicity with statins. The usual prescription range is titrated from 500 mg at bedtime to a maximum of 2000 mg nightly after a low-fat snack; exact dosing follows the current product. Immediate-release, sustained-release supplement, and prescription extended-release products have different peak and hepatic exposure and must not be exchanged milligram for milligram. Flushing is caused by skin prostaglandin release and may be reduced by approved aspirin premedication, slow titration, and avoiding alcohol, hot drinks, or spicy food near the dose. A rising HDL-C is not itself proof of fewer cardiovascular events, which is why use is now selective.

Mechanism

At pharmacologic concentrations, nicotinic acid activates hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (GPR109A). In adipocytes it acutely suppresses lipolysis and free-fatty-acid delivery to liver; hepatic triglyceride synthesis and ApoB-containing VLDL secretion fall through additional pathways, and reduced ApoA-I catabolism raises HDL-C. In skin immune cells, GPR109A triggers prostaglandin D2 and E2 release, dilating dermal vessels and producing warmth, redness, and itching. Aspirin inhibits cyclooxygenase upstream of those prostaglandins, explaining partial flush prevention. Slow hepatic exposure can increase toxic metabolite burden, explaining why some sustained-release preparations are more hepatotoxic. Niacin can worsen insulin resistance and reduce uric-acid excretion, linking the mechanism to glucose elevation and gout.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Severe hepatotoxicity, including fulminant hepatic necrosis, has occurred when sustained-release niacin was substituted for an equal dose of immediate-release product. Never interchange formulations milligram for milligram. Stop and evaluate persistent nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice, dark urine, severe fatigue, or marked laboratory deterioration.

Adverse Effects

  • Flushing, warmth, itching, headache, dizziness, hypotension, nausea, diarrhea, dyspepsia, and rash are common and dose related.

  • Hepatotoxicity, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia or gout, myopathy or rhabdomyolysis with statins, reduced platelets, increased INR, arrhythmia, and cystoid macular edema are serious concerns.

Contraindications

  • Active liver disease or unexplained persistent transaminase elevation, active peptic ulcer, arterial bleeding, and serious hypersensitivity are major contraindications.

  • Do not use a supplement marketed as 'no flush' in place of prescription nicotinic acid; inositol hexanicotinate may neither deliver equivalent active niacin nor reproduce evidence.

Nursing Essentials

  • Verify formulation, strength, titration history, bedtime schedule, low-fat snack, statin, alcohol, diabetes, gout, liver disease, bleeding, and all supplements. Swallow extended-release tablets whole.

  • If the drug is interrupted for more than the label-defined interval, contact the prescriber because restarting the previous full dose can recreate severe flushing or toxicity. Do not self-escalate after missed doses.

Interactions

  • Statins add muscle risk; alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs add liver risk; antihypertensives add hypotension; anticoagulants add bleeding and INR changes.

  • Bile-acid sequestrants can bind niacin and require spacing. Glucose-lowering therapy may need adjustment as glycemia changes.

Key Labs

  • Lipid panel, ALT/AST and bilirubin, fasting glucose or A1c, uric acid, platelets, and INR when indicated; CK, creatinine, potassium, and urinalysis for significant muscle symptoms.

  • Judge benefit by the indication and total atherogenic-risk plan, not by HDL-C rise alone.

Nclex Traps

  • Niacin extended release is pharmacologic nicotinic acid. Niacinamide can treat deficiency but does not substitute for the lipid drug.

  • Flushing may become less noticeable through tolerance while hepatic, glycemic, uric-acid, and muscle risks continue.
