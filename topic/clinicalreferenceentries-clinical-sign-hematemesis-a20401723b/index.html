Definition

Hematemesis is an observed gastrointestinal bleeding sign. It is a clue to bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract. A positive finding means the patient vomits visible red blood, clots, or dark granular coffee-ground material.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Hematemesis means the patient vomits visible red blood, clots, or dark granular coffee-ground material.

Why It Matters

A positive Hematemesis result can signal bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract. Blood entering the stomach is expelled by vomiting; acid partially oxidizes hemoglobin during slower or temporarily stopped bleeding, producing dark coffee-ground material. Hematemesis cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract

Assessment Method

Assess airway and hemodynamic stability first, then document the amount and whether material is bright red, dark red, clotted, or coffee-ground; distinguish vomiting from coughing and inspect when safe.

Positive Finding

The patient vomits visible red blood, clots, or dark granular coffee-ground material.

Mechanism

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Hematemesis result raises concern for bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract because blood entering the stomach is expelled by vomiting; acid partially oxidizes hemoglobin during slower or temporarily stopped bleeding, producing dark coffee-ground material. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Hematemesis does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Hematemesis to focus evaluation of bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract and the reviewed alternatives: hemoptysis, epistaxis with swallowed blood, food discoloration. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Assess airway and hemodynamic stability first, then document the amount and whether material is bright red, dark red, clotted, or coffee-ground; distinguish vomiting from coughing and inspect when safe. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Hematemesis as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Swallowed nasal or oral blood and food can mimic hematemesis, while a large bleed may occur without a witnessed episode. Therefore, Hematemesis cannot confirm or exclude bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz that is vomited from the upper gastrointestinal tract by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • hemoptysis

  • epistaxis with swallowed blood

  • food discoloration

False Positive Situations

A false-positive test can result from method deviation, subjective interpretation, or an alternative cause such as hemoptysis, epistaxis with swallowed blood, food discoloration.

False Negative Situations

Hematemesis may be missed when technique, timing, cooperation, or disease stage prevents the defined response.

Modern Status

Current emergency bleeding sign because upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage can threaten the airway and rapidly cause hypovolemic shock. Interpret Hematemesis in the assessment context defined for this card because its reviewed limitation is topic-specific: Swallowed nasal or oral blood and food can mimic hematemesis, while a large bleed may occur without a witnessed episode.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Hematemesis, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Assess airway and hemodynamic stability first, then document the amount and whether material is bright red, dark red, clotted, or coffee-ground; distinguish vomiting from coughing and inspect when safe. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Protect the airway and activate urgent evaluation for active or large-volume hematemesis, altered consciousness, aspiration risk, hypotension, tachycardia, syncope, or continued bleeding; do not delay stabilization to obtain a perfect history.
