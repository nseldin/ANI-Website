Used To Treat

A clinical reasoning card for suspected or proven invasive pulmonary, sinus, CNS, cardiac, bone, or disseminated aspergillosis in high-risk hosts.

Description

Invasive aspergillosis occurs when Aspergillus hyphae invade tissue and blood vessels, especially during prolonged neutropenia, hematologic malignancy, stem-cell or solid-organ transplantation, high-dose corticosteroid therapy, or severe immune dysfunction. Angioinvasion causes thrombosis, infarction, hemorrhage, and dissemination, explaining pleuritic pain, hemoptysis, nodules with a halo of hemorrhage, cavitation, cerebral lesions, skin lesions, or organ-specific ischemia. Voriconazole is a principal primary therapy under IDSA guidance; isavuconazole is another approved mold-active option, and liposomal amphotericin B is used when azoles are unsuitable, resistance is suspected, or another mold such as Mucorales remains possible. Treatment begins promptly when suspicion is high because waiting for culture proof can allow irreversible vascular invasion.

Mechanism

CT pattern, bronchoscopy, culture, histopathology, galactomannan, and host risk contribute different evidence. Galactomannan reflects Aspergillus cell-wall antigen but sensitivity changes with specimen, antifungal exposure, and host population. Histologic acute-angle septate hyphae support invasive mold but do not always provide species; culture and molecular testing refine it. Voriconazole exposure varies nonlinearly, so a trough after steady state helps distinguish underexposure from resistance or uncontrolled disease. Surgery matters for selected localized sinus, cardiac, bone, or hemorrhage-threatening lesions because infarcted tissue has poor drug delivery and a mechanical complication cannot be metabolically inhibited away.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. FDA boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Suspected invasive mold disease is time-sensitive. Obtain cultures and pathology when feasible, but do not delay active therapy in a deteriorating high-risk patient while waiting for a perfect test.

Adverse Effects

  • Progression can cause massive hemoptysis, stroke-like CNS injury, disseminated infarcts, cardiac emboli, and death.

  • Treatment toxicity depends on the drug: voriconazole adds hepatic, visual, neurologic, phototoxic, QT, and interaction risks; amphotericin adds renal and electrolyte injury.

Contraindications

  • Do not use fluconazole for invasive aspergillosis; it lacks reliable Aspergillus activity.

  • Do not assume every broad, nonseptate, or culture-negative mold syndrome is Aspergillus. Mucormycosis requires a different drug and often urgent debridement.

Nursing Essentials

  • Escalate hemoptysis, new focal neurologic signs, orbital pain/swelling, skin necrosis, or sudden pleuritic deterioration. Protect neutropenic patients from construction dust and follow unit environmental precautions.

  • Track exact azole formulation, loading doses, levels when ordered, liver tests, QT/electrolytes, interacting medicines, imaging trajectory, and immune recovery.

Interactions

  • Voriconazole has extensive CYP2C19/2C9/3A interactions; transplant immunosuppressants and oncology drugs often require preemptive adjustment and close levels.

  • Corticosteroid reduction and neutrophil recovery can be central to cure when clinically feasible because antifungal therapy cannot fully replace host immunity.

Key Labs

  • CBC with neutrophil trend, renal/liver tests, electrolytes, serum or BAL galactomannan in appropriate populations, culture, histopathology, susceptibility, voriconazole trough when indicated, and serial CT based on clinical course.

  • Early CT can worsen despite therapy as neutrophils recover; interpret radiology with timing, immune status, symptoms, and microbiology rather than one image alone.

Nclex Traps

  • A halo sign is suggestive in the right host, not pathognomonic. It represents hemorrhage around a lesion and has other causes.

  • Voriconazole treats Aspergillus but not Mucorales; the wrong mold assumption can be fatal.

Source Note

IDSA aspergillosis guideline and current U.S. voriconazole, Cresemba, and liposomal amphotericin labels.
