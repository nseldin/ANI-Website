Definition

Continuous kidney replacement therapy provides extracorporeal fluid and solute removal over most or all of the day, allowing gentler moment-to-moment shifts than intermittent HD. It is commonly used in critically ill patients when ongoing fluid control, hemodynamic instability, cerebral edema risk, or severe catabolism makes slower continuous management advantageous.

Etiology

CKRT treats severe AKI or kidney failure with refractory complications in critical illness. Modality variants emphasize convection, diffusion, or both; the choice reflects local expertise, access, anticoagulation, fluid goals, solute needs, and patient physiology.

Pathology

Blood passes through a hemofilter via a dialysis catheter. Continuous venovenous hemofiltration uses convective replacement fluid, hemodialysis uses dialysate diffusion, and hemodiafiltration combines both; net balance also includes every medication, nutrition, blood product, and non-CKRT fluid.

Pathophysiology

Slower continuous removal reduces abrupt plasma volume and osmolality changes, but cumulative daily clearance can be substantial when the circuit runs reliably. Prescribed dose differs from delivered dose because downtime, clotting, access alarms, procedures, and filter performance reduce actual effluent. Citrate anticoagulation chelates circuit calcium and can prolong filter life, but impaired citrate metabolism or protocol errors can cause calcium and acid-base disturbances. Continuous clearance also removes heat, nutrients, phosphate, amino acids, and selected drugs, so dosing and replacement differ from both no dialysis and intermittent HD.

Risk Factors

  • Shock requiring vasopressors, severe fluid overload, cerebral edema risk, or multiorgan failure

  • Poor catheter flow, hypercoagulability, frequent procedures, or inadequate anticoagulation causing downtime

  • Liver or shock-related impaired citrate metabolism

  • High nutrition, medication, and electrolyte replacement complexity

Signs Symptoms

  • Hemodynamics may tolerate CKRT better, but hypotension can still occur

  • Circuit pressure changes, visible clotting, reduced effluent, or recurrent alarms signal delivery failure

  • Hypothermia, bleeding, hypocalcemia symptoms, weakness, or arrhythmia can arise

  • Persistent overload or azotemia despite a running machine suggests inadequate delivered therapy or ongoing production

Diagnostics

  • Track prescribed versus delivered effluent and circuit downtime

  • Audit net balance from all inputs and outputs, not machine ultrafiltration alone

  • Monitor access, filter pressures, anticoagulation, ionized calcium, acid-base, electrolytes, temperature, and nutrition

  • Review every antimicrobial and narrow-therapeutic-index drug for CKRT dosing

Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate/pH, phosphate, magnesium, ionized calcium, sodium, glucose, BUN, and creatinine change continuously

  • Total-to-ionized calcium pattern and rising calcium requirements can suggest citrate accumulation in the right context

  • Phosphate depletion is common during prolonged therapy

  • Drug concentrations may be needed because critical illness and CKRT alter volume and clearance unpredictably

Treatments

  • Select diffusion, convection, or combined therapy and set net fluid goals according to the critical-care plan

  • Use protocolized citrate or other anticoagulation when appropriate

  • Replace electrolytes, heat, nutrition, and medicines based on delivered therapy

  • Troubleshoot access, clotting, and downtime promptly and transition modalities as stability changes

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify modality, prescribed dose, blood and replacement-fluid rates, net fluid goal, access, anticoagulation, and solution composition because small setup errors accumulate continuously across many hours.

  • Trend hourly fluid balance, circuit pressures, filter life, temperature, hemodynamics, electrolytes, glucose, phosphate, magnesium, ionized calcium, and acid-base status because CKRT can quietly remove heat, solute, nutrients, and medications.

  • Use strict aseptic access care and trace every line from patient to machine before connection or repositioning because catheter infection, disconnection, air entry, or blood loss can be catastrophic.

  • Coordinate antimicrobial, anticonvulsant, nutrition, and other dose adjustments with pharmacy and nephrology because continuous clearance and interruptions change drug exposure unpredictably.

  • Stop or escalate per protocol for air detection, major blood leak, access disconnection, refractory hypotension, severe citrate-related calcium disturbance, or uncontrolled electrolyte change because continued therapy may worsen immediate instability.

Complications

  • Bleeding, citrate accumulation, hypocalcemia, alkalosis or acidosis

  • Hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia, hypothermia, nutrient loss, and drug underexposure

  • Catheter infection, thrombosis, air or blood loss

  • Underdelivery from downtime and inaccurate fluid balance

Contraindications

  • No single hemodynamic criterion automatically mandates CKRT; use depends on the clinical problem and resources

  • Regional citrate requires protocol expertise and close monitoring and may be unsuitable when metabolism is severely impaired

  • Do not assume a running machine is delivering the prescribed dose

Red Flags

  • Air alarm, visible circuit air, line disconnection, or major blood leak

  • Refractory hypotension or escalating vasopressor requirement during therapy

  • Falling ionized calcium with rising total-to-ionized calcium relationship

  • Dangerous potassium, phosphate, glucose, temperature, or acid-base shift

Patient Education

  • Explain that continuous therapy removes fluid and wastes slowly for unstable patients, but it still requires uninterrupted monitoring and may pause for alarms or procedures.

  • Teach families not to touch access or machine lines because a loosened continuous blood circuit can cause infection, air entry, or rapid blood loss.

Nclex Traps

  • CRRT and CKRT refer to continuous kidney support, not rapid response team activity

  • Net machine removal is not the same as total patient fluid balance

  • Continuous therapy can remove medications and phosphate substantially

  • Prescribed dose and delivered dose differ when downtime occurs

Causal Links

  • Slow continuous removal -> smaller hourly osmotic and volume shifts -> improved tolerance in selected unstable patients

  • Circuit downtime -> lower delivered clearance -> persistent solute and fluid burden

  • Citrate chelation -> circuit anticoagulation plus lower ionized calcium -> need for monitored calcium replacement

  • Continuous effluent loss -> phosphate, heat, nutrients, and drug removal -> replacement and dose changes
