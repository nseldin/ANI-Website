Used To Treat

Major depressive disorder; clinically selected when depression coexists with insomnia, low appetite, nausea or weight loss, while recognizing that sedation and weight gain may be harmful in another patient.

Mechanism

Alpha-2 autoreceptors normally inhibit norepinephrine release and alpha-2 heteroreceptors inhibit serotonin release; antagonism disinhibits both systems. Blocking 5-HT2A/2C and 5-HT3 channels the increased serotonin signal toward other receptors such as 5-HT1A, which is associated with anxiolytic/antidepressant effects while limiting some sexual, activation and nausea pathways. Potent H1 inverse agonism suppresses arousal and increases appetite. Minimal direct muscarinic activity reduces classic anticholinergic burden, though dry mouth and constipation still occur. CYP1A2, CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 metabolize the drug, so combined inhibition or organ impairment can increase sedation.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING: Antidepressants increase suicidal thoughts and behaviors in pediatric and young-adult patients in short-term studies. Monitor every age group for clinical worsening, emerging suicidality, agitation, or abrupt behavioral change, especially after starting or changing the dose. Warn about severe sedation and impaired driving until individual response is known; do not use dose escalation as a self-directed strategy to escape sedation.

Contraindications

  • Concurrent/recent MAOI use or serotonergic linezolid/methylene blue outside emergency guidance

  • Product hypersensitivity

  • Assess severe obesity/metabolic disease, untreated sleep-disordered breathing, neutropenia history, hepatic/renal impairment and QT risk

Nursing Essentials

  • Measure appetite, weight, waist/metabolic trend, daytime alertness and fall risk; improved sleep is beneficial only if daytime function and cardiometabolic safety remain acceptable.

  • Escalate fever, sore throat, stomatitis or infection because rare neutropenia can remove the neutrophil response needed to contain infection.

Interactions

  • MAOIs and other serotonergic drugs add serotonin syndrome risk.

  • Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines and antipsychotics add impairment/falls.

  • Strong CYP3A4 or multi-enzyme inhibitors can raise exposure; inducers can reduce efficacy; other QT drugs add repolarization risk.

Key Labs

  • Weight/BMI, appetite, glucose/lipids based on risk

  • CBC with infection symptoms; liver/renal function for dosing risk

  • Sodium, ECG/electrolytes and mood/mania/suicide assessment when indicated

Nclex Traps

  • Mirtazapine is not merely a sleeping pill; the antidepressant mechanism is alpha-2 disinhibition plus selective serotonin-receptor shaping.

  • Lower sexual/GI burden than many SSRIs does not mean no serotonin syndrome.

  • Do not promise that a higher dose will always be less sedating.

Description

Mirtazapine is a tetracyclic antidepressant that increases norepinephrine and selected serotonin signaling by blocking presynaptic alpha-2 brakes, while blocking H1, 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors. The combination explains its identity: antidepressant monoamine enhancement with prominent sleepiness and appetite/weight gain, less 5-HT2-mediated sexual dysfunction, and less 5-HT3-mediated nausea than many SSRIs. Sedation is often strongest early, but the popular claim that it reliably becomes activating at higher doses is not dependable enough to guide unsupervised dosing.

Adverse Effects

  • Somnolence, increased appetite, weight gain, dry mouth and constipation

  • Dizziness, orthostasis, abnormal dreams and edema

  • Rare severe neutropenia/agranulocytosis, serious skin reactions and liver injury

  • Serotonin syndrome, hyponatremia, mania, seizures and QT prolongation in susceptible patients
