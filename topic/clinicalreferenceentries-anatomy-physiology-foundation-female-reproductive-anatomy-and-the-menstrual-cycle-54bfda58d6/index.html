Summary

The female reproductive system produces oocytes and hormones, permits sperm transport and fertilization, supports implantation and pregnancy, and provides the birth canal. During a typical reproductive cycle, ovarian follicle development, ovulation, and corpus-luteum function are coordinated with endometrial growth and shedding. The cycle is not controlled by one hormone: pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH, pituitary FSH and LH, ovarian estrogen and progesterone, inhibin, and local tissue signals form a changing feedback network.

Quick Answer

The ovaries lie beside the uterus and release an oocyte that is normally captured by a uterine tube; fertilization most often occurs in the tube, while implantation normally occurs in the uterine endometrium. Early in the cycle, FSH supports follicle growth and rising estrogen rebuilds endometrium. Sustained high estrogen near midcycle changes from mainly negative to positive feedback and helps trigger an LH surge, which causes ovulation and luteinization. The corpus luteum then produces progesterone that stabilizes a secretory endometrium. If implantation and embryonic hCG do not sustain the corpus luteum, estrogen and progesterone fall, spiral arteries constrict, and the functional endometrial layer sheds as menstruation. Cycle length and bleeding vary; physiology should be interpreted with age, pregnancy possibility, medicines, illness, and the person's baseline.

Sections

  • Label

    Structure and location

    Text

    External structures include the vulva, labia, clitoris, vestibule, and openings of urethra and vagina. The vagina is a distensible muscular canal leading to the cervix. The uterus has a muscular myometrium surrounding an endometrial cavity; its fundus lies above the uterine-tube openings, and the cervix projects into the upper vagina. Each uterine tube has fimbriae near the ovary and ciliated, muscular segments that move gametes and an early embryo. Ovaries contain follicles in a cortical region and produce oocytes and hormones. Pelvic ligaments, fascia, muscles, vessels, lymphatics, and autonomic nerves support these organs. Individual anatomy and prior surgery change position and pathways.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: ovarian cycle

    Text

    At the start of a cycle, loss of luteal hormones releases hypothalamic and pituitary feedback, allowing FSH to recruit a follicle cohort. Granulosa and theca cells cooperate to make estrogen, and one follicle usually becomes dominant because it responds effectively as FSH later declines. Sustained high estradiol near midcycle promotes an LH surge rather than its usual inhibitory feedback. LH triggers resumption of oocyte maturation, follicle rupture, and conversion of remaining cells into a corpus luteum. The corpus luteum produces progesterone, estrogen, and inhibin. Without hCG from an implanted conceptus, it regresses; hormone withdrawal starts the next menstruation. Ovulation is an event, not the entire fertile window.

  • Label

    Causal mechanism: uterine cycle and fertilization

    Text

    Menstruation sheds the functional endometrial layer after progesterone and estrogen withdrawal destabilize vessels and tissue. During the proliferative phase, estrogen restores glands, stroma, and surface epithelium. After ovulation, progesterone converts this lining to a secretory, implantation-supporting state and reduces excessive uterine contractility. Cervical mucus also changes across the cycle, becoming more permissive around ovulation and thicker under progesterone influence. Sperm may remain viable for several days, whereas the ovulated oocyte has a shorter fertilizable period. Tubal cilia and muscle transport the early embryo toward the uterus. Implantation outside the endometrial cavity, most often in a tube, cannot develop normally and can rupture maternal vessels.

  • Label

    Regulation and relationships

    Text

    The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis depends on pulsatile GnRH; frequency and energy availability affect LH and FSH patterns. Stress, major weight change, intensive exercise, lactation, chronic disease, hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disorders, age, and medications can suppress or distort cycling. Inhibin helps restrain FSH. Most of the cycle uses negative feedback, but the preovulatory estrogen-driven LH surge is a clinically important positive-feedback exception. Ovarian hormones influence bone, breast, vasculature, thermoregulation, brain, and urogenital tissue, so menopause and ovarian failure have effects beyond menstruation. Pregnancy changes the endocrine system substantially and must be considered before interpreting bleeding or giving potentially harmful therapy.

  • Label

    Failure consequences and why they happen

    Text

    Anovulation means no normal corpus luteum and less organized progesterone exposure, so endometrium may proliferate and shed irregularly. Polycystic ovary syndrome can combine ovulatory dysfunction and androgen excess with metabolic features, but ovarian cysts alone do not establish it. Endometriosis places endometrium-like tissue outside the uterine cavity and can cause cyclic inflammation, pain, adhesions, and infertility. Fibroids arise from uterine smooth muscle and can distort bleeding or pressure. Tubal scarring raises infertility and ectopic risk. Ovarian torsion compromises venous then arterial flow. Menopause reflects depletion of functional follicles, lowering ovarian hormones and removing feedback so gonadotropins usually rise. Heavy or prolonged bleeding can cause iron deficiency or hemodynamic instability.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: medications and diseases

    Text

    Combined hormonal contraceptives suppress ovulation mainly through axis feedback and also alter cervical mucus and endometrium; progestin-only methods vary in their balance of effects. Emergency contraception delays or prevents ovulation depending on agent and timing and does not terminate an established implanted pregnancy. Fertility medicines stimulate follicular development or trigger ovulation and require monitoring because multiple follicles and ovarian hyperstimulation can occur. Estrogen can increase thrombotic risk in susceptible patients; individual contraindications matter. GnRH agonists given continuously can suppress an axis after an initial stimulation. Treatments for abnormal bleeding, endometriosis, menopause, or infertility use these mechanisms differently and belong in disease-specific guidance.

  • Label

    Clinical connections: labs and diagnostics

    Text

    A pregnancy test is fundamental in reproductive-age patients with missed menses, pelvic pain, bleeding, syncope, or before selected procedures and medicines. Urine and serum hCG differ in sensitivity and timing; one early negative result may need reassessment. CBC and ferritin evaluate bleeding consequences. TSH, prolactin, androgens, FSH, LH, progesterone, and other tests answer selected questions only when timed and interpreted appropriately. Pelvic ultrasound evaluates uterus, endometrium, ovaries, masses, pregnancy location, and blood flow but does not diagnose every cause of pain. Pelvic examination, cervical testing, endometrial sampling, hysteroscopy, or laparoscopy address narrower concerns. Menstrual dates are useful estimates, not proof of ovulation or gestational age.

  • Label

    Nursing assessment and actions with rationale

    Text

    Ask privately and inclusively about last menstrual period, usual cycle and flow, pregnancy possibility and intent, pain, discharge, fever, contraception, medicines, sexual exposure, prior pregnancies, surgeries, bleeding disorders, anemia symptoms, and safety concerns. Quantify bleeding with pad or tampon frequency, clots, dizziness, vital signs, and hemoglobin trend rather than words such as heavy alone. With pelvic pain or bleeding, monitor hemodynamics and escalate pregnancy-location concerns promptly. Provide analgesia and comfort measures while evaluation continues. Teach medication adherence, warning signs, iron use when prescribed, and that irregular bleeding does not guarantee infertility or contraception. Use trauma-informed consent, chaperone practices, and respect for gender identity without assuming anatomy.

  • Label

    Urgent red flags

    Text

    Positive pregnancy test or possible pregnancy with unilateral pelvic pain, shoulder pain, syncope, dizziness, or bleeding may indicate ectopic pregnancy and needs urgent evaluation. Sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain with nausea can signal ovarian torsion, where time affects tissue survival. Heavy bleeding with tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, chest discomfort, dyspnea, or altered mentation requires emergency response. Fever, pelvic pain, purulent discharge, pregnancy-related bleeding, severe pain after a procedure, or rapidly worsening abdominal findings also demand prompt care. Postmenopausal bleeding requires evaluation even when small. Never reassure solely because pain or bleeding temporarily decreases; rupture and intermittent torsion can change symptoms.

  • Label

    Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    Text

    The uterine tube is not physically sealed to the ovary; fimbriae help capture the oocyte. Fertilization usually occurs in a tube, not the uterus, while normal implantation is uterine. Menstruation is hormone-withdrawal shedding, not the body removing toxins. Ovulation does not always occur on cycle day 14, and an app estimate does not prove it. A patient can ovulate before the first postpartum or postmenarchal bleed. Combined contraception does more than create a barrier, and emergency contraception is not the same as abortion medication. Endometriosis cannot be excluded by a normal basic ultrasound. High FSH after menopause reflects reduced ovarian feedback, not stronger ovarian function.
