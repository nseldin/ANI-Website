Definition

Myocarditis develops when infectious, immune, toxic, or systemic inflammation injures myocytes and can impair contraction or electrical stability. The characteristic pattern is chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, syncope, heart failure, arrhythmia, or shock after a viral or inflammatory illness. Confirm or stage it with ECG, troponin, natriuretic peptide, echocardiography, and rhythm monitoring; distinguish it from acute coronary syndrome, stress cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, and sepsis cardiomyopathy by coronary anatomy, MRI, echo, and context. The priority is to restrict exercise and treat heart failure and arrhythmias because ventricular arrhythmia can complicate Myocarditis. can follow if the process advances.

Mechanistically, infectious, immune, toxic, or systemic inflammation injures myocytes and can impair contraction or electrical stability.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation is present, often from infection, autoimmune injury, obstruction, or tissue damage depending on the condition.

Signs Symptoms

  • fever may be present but older adults/newborns may be atypical

  • localized pain/swelling/drainage when tissue infection is present

Diagnostics

  • ECG, troponin, natriuretic peptide, echocardiography, and rhythm monitoring

  • cardiac MRI for edema and injury pattern

  • coronary evaluation and selected endomyocardial biopsy when ischemia or a treatable fulminant subtype must be distinguished

  • Distinguish Myocarditis from acute coronary syndrome, stress cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, and sepsis cardiomyopathy by coronary anatomy, MRI, echo, and context.

Labs

  • troponin may rise with myocardial injury

  • BNP may rise with heart failure strain

  • potassium and magnesium shifts increase dysrhythmia risk

  • creatinine matters before contrast and medication dosing

Treatments

  • restrict exercise and treat heart failure and arrhythmias

  • stop a causal toxin and treat specific immune or infectious subtypes when established

  • use mechanical circulatory support or transplantation for refractory fulminant shock

Nursing Priorities

  • Escalate immediately for ventricular arrhythmia, high-grade block, syncope, rapidly worsening heart failure, hypotension, or rising lactate because mechanical circulatory support may be needed.

  • Assess recent infection or immune exposure with chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, syncope, and exercise intolerance because myocarditis can mimic infarction or progress to pump failure.

  • Obtain and trend electrocardiograms, troponin, inflammatory markers, echocardiographic function, and perfusion findings because injury severity can change rapidly.

  • Limit strenuous activity and administer ordered heart-failure or arrhythmia therapy because exertion during active myocardial inflammation may increase instability.

  • Monitor rhythm, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, urine output, lactate, and pulmonary congestion to detect ventricular arrhythmia and cardiogenic shock.

Complications

  • ventricular arrhythmia can complicate Myocarditis.

  • sudden death can complicate Myocarditis.

  • cardiogenic shock can complicate Myocarditis.

  • heart block can complicate Myocarditis.

  • thrombus can complicate Myocarditis.

  • dilated cardiomyopathy can complicate Myocarditis.

Red Flags

  • Ventricular arrhythmia or high-grade conduction block

  • Syncope or rapidly worsening exercise intolerance

  • Acute pulmonary edema or severe ventricular dysfunction

  • Hypotension, oliguria, or rising lactate

Patient Education

  • Teach avoidance of strenuous exercise until the cardiology team confirms recovery because symptoms may improve before inflammation resolves.

  • Explain that recurrent chest pain, fainting, palpitations, breathlessness, or swelling requires prompt reassessment.

Nclex Traps

  • A young age or recent virus does not make chest pain benign

  • Normal coronary arteries do not prove myocarditis without compatible evidence
