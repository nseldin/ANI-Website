Pathology

Loss of gastric HCl adds bicarbonate to blood, but healthy kidneys would usually excrete that bicarbonate. Volume and chloride depletion activate RAAS and increase proximal sodium-bicarbonate reclamation, while low distal chloride limits pendrin-mediated bicarbonate secretion. Aldosterone and distal sodium delivery increase hydrogen and potassium secretion, and hypokalemia shifts hydrogen into cells and increases renal ammoniagenesis, creating a self-reinforcing alkalosis. This explains why isotonic chloride and potassium often correct a vomiting or remote-diuretic pattern more effectively than water alone.

Etiology

Gastric hydrogen and chloride loss from vomiting or suction, loop or thiazide diuretics, effective arterial volume depletion, mineralocorticoid excess, severe potassium depletion, post-hypercapnic states, alkali administration with impaired excretion, Bartter or Gitelman physiology, and rare chloride diarrhea or tubular transport disorders cause metabolic alkalosis. Reduced GFR, chloride depletion, aldosterone effect, and hypokalemia commonly maintain it after the original loss stops.

Signs Symptoms

  • Weakness, cramps, paresthesias, tetany, confusion, seizure, hypoventilation, and dysrhythmia arise from alkalemia and associated potassium, magnesium, chloride, and ionized-calcium changes.

  • Volume depletion causes orthostasis and dry mucosa, while mineralocorticoid states more often produce hypertension and potassium wasting.

  • A normal pH can conceal alkalosis when high-gap acidosis or chronic respiratory acidosis coexists.

Diagnostics

  • Confirm a primary high-HCO3 process, calculate expected PaCO2, and evaluate for a mixed disorder.

  • Measure spot urine chloride and interpret it with BP, volume status, potassium, magnesium, medication timing, and renal function.

  • Investigate renin and aldosterone, cortisol or exogenous mineralocorticoid effects, vomiting or covert losses, diuretic exposure, and inherited tubulopathy only as the pattern directs.

Treatments

  • Stop gastric losses when possible and replace chloride, effective circulating volume, and potassium for chloride-responsive alkalosis.

  • Stop or adjust causative diuretics, treat mineralocorticoid excess, replace magnesium and potassium, and use acetazolamide or potassium-sparing strategies selectively according to volume, renal, and BP context.

  • Severe refractory alkalemia may require monitored acid infusion or dialysis in specialized care; these are rescue therapies with substantial hazards, not routine bedside correction.

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess vomiting, suction, diuretics, alkali intake, blood pressure, volume, potassium, magnesium, kidney function, and mineralocorticoid clues because alkalosis persists when bicarbonate excretion is limited by chloride, potassium, perfusion, or hormonal effects.

  • Trend pH, bicarbonate, PaCO2, chloride, potassium, magnesium, creatinine, urine chloride when ordered, rhythm, and neurologic status because severe alkalemia increases dysrhythmia, weakness, seizure, and hypoventilation risk.

  • Verify ongoing gastric losses, diuretic timing, saline exposure, and urine-chloride context because chloride-responsive and chloride-resistant patterns require different treatment and urine results are medication sensitive.

  • Administer prescribed chloride, potassium, magnesium, volume, diuretic adjustment, acetazolamide, or cause-specific therapy with frequent reassessment because correcting maintenance factors permits renal bicarbonate excretion.

  • Escalate immediately for pH above 7.60, ventricular dysrhythmia, seizure, severe weakness, tetany, or hypoventilation with hypoxemia because extreme alkalemia impairs cardiac, neurologic, and respiratory function.

Complications

  • Dysrhythmia, neuromuscular irritability, seizure, reduced cerebral blood flow, hypoventilation, and reduced tissue oxygen delivery can occur in severe alkalemia.

  • Hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia increase digitalis toxicity and ventricular arrhythmia risk.

  • Persistent alkalosis can impair ventilator weaning and promote further kidney potassium and hydrogen loss.

Patient Education

  • Explain that vomiting or diuretics can generate alkalosis, but chloride depletion, low potassium, reduced filtration, or hormones often keep it from resolving.

  • Teach patients not to overuse bicarbonate, antacids, or diuretics and to report persistent vomiting, palpitations, cramps, confusion, or severe weakness.

Nclex Traps

  • Vomiting causes metabolic alkalosis because gastric acid is lost; diarrhea usually causes metabolic acidosis because intestinal bicarbonate is lost.

  • Urine chloride is more useful than the vague phrase contraction alkalosis, but recent diuretics can raise it.

  • Compensatory hypoventilation is limited by the need to oxygenate.

Definition

Metabolic alkalosis is a primary process that raises serum bicarbonate through hydrogen loss, alkali gain, or movement of hydrogen into cells. Generation explains how bicarbonate first rises; maintenance explains why kidneys do not excrete the excess. The process often causes alkalemia, but pH can be normal or low when respiratory acidosis or metabolic acidosis coexists. Respiratory compensation raises PaCO2 through hypoventilation, but hypoxemia limits how far ventilation can fall.

Pathophysiology

Metabolic alkalosis requires both a generating event and a maintenance environment. A useful screening estimate is expected PaCO2 approximately 40 + 0.7 x (HCO3 - 24), with an approximate +/-2 to 5 mm Hg range depending on the reference and setting. PaCO2 below expected suggests added respiratory alkalosis; above expected suggests added respiratory acidosis. Spot urine chloride often organizes causes: below about 20 mEq/L suggests a chloride-responsive state such as vomiting, remote diuretic exposure, or post-hypercapnia, whereas 20 mEq/L or higher suggests ongoing diuretic action, mineralocorticoid excess, severe potassium depletion, or salt-wasting tubulopathy. Thresholds are approximate, and recent diuretics, CKD, fluid therapy, and timing can misclassify the pattern.

Risk Factors

  • Persistent vomiting, nasogastric suction, high-output gastric drainage, and chloride-poor replacement remove HCl and volume.

  • Loop or thiazide therapy increases distal sodium delivery, aldosterone effect, potassium loss, and hydrogen secretion.

  • Hyperaldosteronism, Cushing physiology, licorice-like mineralocorticoid effects, severe hypokalemia, chronic hypercapnia correction, and large alkali loads increase risk.

Labs

  • High HCO3 with alkalemia supports metabolic alkalosis; blood gas distinguishes it from compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis.

  • Urine chloride below about 20 mEq/L usually indicates chloride responsiveness, while 20 or higher suggests a chloride-resistant or ongoing renal-loss pattern, with important timing limitations.

  • Hypokalemia, hypochloremia, hypomagnesemia, and low ionized calcium increase clinical risk even when total calcium is normal.

Contraindications

  • Do not call every high bicarbonate metabolic alkalosis; chronic respiratory acidosis can raise bicarbonate as compensation.

  • Do not give isotonic saline automatically to an edematous or hypertensive chloride-resistant patient.

  • Do not recommend acid infusion or hydrochloric acid outside specialist-monitored rescue care.

Red Flags

  • pH above 7.60 or rapidly worsening alkalemia

  • Ventricular dysrhythmia or severe potassium or magnesium depletion

  • Seizure, delirium, tetany, or declining level of consciousness

  • Hypoventilation with rising PaCO2, hypoxemia, or respiratory fatigue
