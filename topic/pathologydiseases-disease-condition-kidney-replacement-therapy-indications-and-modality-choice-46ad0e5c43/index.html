Definition

Kidney replacement therapy uses dialysis, hemofiltration, or transplantation to replace selected failed kidney functions. Initiation is based on the clinical burden of refractory complications, symptoms, trajectory, and patient goals—not a fixed eGFR, creatinine, or BUN threshold.

Etiology

KRT is considered when severe AKI or kidney failure cannot maintain safe electrolyte, acid-base, fluid, toxin, or uremic balance despite appropriate medical treatment. Modality choice depends on hemodynamics, urgency, solute and fluid goals, neurologic risk, access, peritoneal and vascular factors, home capacity, lifestyle, transplant plan, prognosis, and informed preference.

Pathology

Intermittent hemodialysis clears through an extracorporeal dialyzer; peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's peritoneal membrane; continuous kidney replacement therapy provides slower ongoing extracorporeal clearance in critical illness; transplantation restores broader living kidney function. Each replaces only part of normal kidney physiology and carries different access and complication burdens.

Pathophysiology

The educational AEIOU mnemonic groups urgent problems: severe refractory Acidosis, dangerous Electrolyte disturbance, selected dialyzable Intoxications, refractory fluid Overload, and symptomatic Uremia. It is a memory aid, not a guideline threshold or automatic order. Diffusion favors removal of small solutes, convection carries solute with ultrafiltrate, and ultrafiltration removes plasma water. Hemodynamic tolerance improves when removal is slower, but slower therapies need time and reliable delivery; modality can change as the patient's condition changes.

Risk Factors

  • Advanced CKD with progressive symptoms or refractory complications

  • Severe AKI with shock, catabolism, oliguria, or multiorgan failure

  • Dangerous hyperkalemia, acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremic complications, or selected poisoning

  • Late referral, no permanent access, or limited home and caregiver resources

Signs Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, hypoxemia, edema, or severe hypertension from refractory overload

  • Weakness, arrhythmia, or ECG change from hyperkalemia

  • Confusion, asterixis, seizure, pericarditic pain, bleeding, pruritus, nausea, anorexia, or severe functional decline from uremia

  • Toxin-specific neurologic, visual, acid-base, or cardiovascular findings

Diagnostics

  • Assess whether the dangerous problem is refractory to timely medical management

  • Identify toxin properties and consult toxicology for poisoning

  • Evaluate hemodynamics, intracranial risk, vascular and peritoneal access, residual kidney function, and urgency

  • Use shared decision-making and an ESKD Life-Plan for chronic modality and access choices

Labs

  • Potassium, bicarbonate/pH, oxygenation, volume trajectory, and ECG define urgency better than creatinine alone

  • BUN and eGFR support context but have no universal start threshold

  • Toxin concentration, osmol gap, anion gap, or drug level can guide selected poisonings

  • Delivered clearance, fluid balance, electrolytes, and symptoms assess response

Treatments

  • Use intermittent HD for rapid clearance when hemodynamics permit

  • Use PD for appropriate home or chronic therapy and selected acute settings

  • Use continuous therapy when slower continuous fluid and solute control best fits critical illness

  • Pursue transplant or comprehensive conservative kidney care according to eligibility, goals, and informed choice

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess potassium, acid-base status, volume and oxygenation, uremic complications, toxin exposure, urine output, hemodynamics, bleeding risk, and goals because kidney replacement begins for dangerous physiology rather than one creatinine or BUN threshold.

  • Trend refractory abnormalities and response to medical treatment while preparing access, consent, laboratory studies, and equipment because delay becomes unsafe when hyperkalemia, acidosis, overload, or uremia cannot be controlled.

  • Match intermittent hemodialysis, continuous therapy, prolonged intermittent therapy, or peritoneal dialysis to urgency, hemodynamic stability, brain risk, access, resources, and patient priorities because modalities differ in speed and tolerance.

  • Coordinate medication dosing, nutrition, anticoagulation, fluid goals, and specimen timing with the selected modality because clearance and treatment interruptions change drug and electrolyte management.

  • Activate immediate nephrology and critical-care response for refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremic encephalopathy or pericarditis, or dialyzable poisoning because these indications can deteriorate before routine scheduling.

Complications

  • Hypotension, arrhythmia, bleeding, infection, air or circuit events, and access loss

  • Dialysis disequilibrium or overly rapid osmotic change

  • Underdialysis, malnutrition, and medication removal or accumulation errors

  • Treatment burden and modality-access mismatch

Contraindications

  • Do not initiate chronic dialysis from one eGFR value alone

  • Do not treat AEIOU as a rigid checklist that replaces clinical judgment

  • Do not use blanket fistula-first or one-modality-fits-all rules

Red Flags

  • Refractory hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram change or dysrhythmia

  • Severe persistent acidosis with shock or respiratory exhaustion

  • Pulmonary edema or oxygen failure despite medical fluid management

  • Uremic encephalopathy, pericarditis, uncontrolled bleeding, or dialyzable toxin

Patient Education

  • Explain that dialysis is started for complications the kidneys can no longer control, not automatically at one creatinine, eGFR, or BUN value.

  • Teach patients that modality choice balances speed, blood pressure tolerance, access, lifestyle, and goals, so the safest option can change during acute illness.

Nclex Traps

  • AEIOU is an urgency mnemonic, not an eGFR cutoff

  • Dialysis removes potassium and water; insulin only shifts potassium temporarily

  • KRT does not replace every endocrine kidney function

  • Hemodynamic instability often favors slower therapy but does not create one automatic modality
