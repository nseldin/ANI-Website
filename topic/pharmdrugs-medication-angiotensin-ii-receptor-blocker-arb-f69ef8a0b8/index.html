Used To Treat

Hypertension and selected heart-failure, post-MI, diabetic-nephropathy, proteinuric-kidney-disease, stroke-risk, or cardiovascular-risk indications depending on the specific ARB. ARBs are often used when ACE-inhibitor cough is intolerable, but each drug retains fetal, potassium, pressure, and renal-perfusion risks.

Description

ARBs selectively block angiotensin II at the AT1 receptor. They do not inhibit ACE and therefore do not directly prevent bradykinin breakdown. That is why dry cough is far less characteristic than with ACE inhibitors, although rare angioedema can still occur. Blocking AT1 relaxes arterioles, lowers aldosterone-mediated sodium retention, dilates the glomerular efferent arteriole, reduces albuminuria, and limits maladaptive cardiac and vascular remodeling. Angiotensin II concentration may rise because renin feedback is released, but it cannot produce its usual AT1 effect while the receptor is occupied. The class is not interchangeable by indication: losartan and irbesartan have labeled diabetic-nephropathy roles, valsartan has heart-failure and post-MI roles, candesartan has a heart-failure outcome indication, telmisartan has a selected cardiovascular-risk indication, and olmesartan's defining adverse-effect clue is delayed sprue-like enteropathy.

Mechanism

AT1 is a Gq-coupled receptor. Angiotensin-II binding activates phospholipase C, inositol trisphosphate, diacylglycerol, intracellular calcium, vascular contraction, aldosterone release, proximal sodium transport, sympathetic facilitation, inflammation, hypertrophy, and fibrosis. ARBs occupy AT1 and interrupt those signals without blocking AT2 receptors or ACE. Efferent-arteriole dilation lowers glomerular capillary pressure, which can reduce albumin leakage and long-term nephron stress but can also lower GFR when renal inflow is compromised. Reduced aldosterone decreases potassium secretion in the collecting duct, explaining hyperkalemia. Because fetal kidneys require intact angiotensin signaling, the same receptor blockade causes oligohydramnios and fetal toxicity. Lack of direct bradykinin accumulation explains the lower cough rate, not freedom from all airway swelling or class safety concerns.

Boxed Warning

BOXED WARNING - FETAL TOXICITY: When pregnancy is detected, discontinue as soon as possible and arrange an appropriate alternative. Drugs that act directly on the renin-angiotensin system can injure or kill the developing fetus. During the second and third trimesters, fetal renal perfusion and filtration depend on angiotensin II signaling; blocking that pathway can cause fetal anuria and oligohydramnios. Because later amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine, prolonged oligohydramnios can produce pulmonary hypoplasia and skeletal or skull deformation. Neonatal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, and death can follow. Every systemic ARB carries fetal-toxicity warning language. An ARB is not a pregnancy-safe substitute for an ACE inhibitor.

Adverse Effects

  • Dizziness, hypotension, hyperkalemia, creatinine increase, fatigue, and less commonly headache or GI symptoms; cough rates are generally near placebo rather than the ACE-inhibitor pattern.

  • Severe AKI, dangerous hyperkalemia, angioedema, shock in volume depletion, fetal toxicity, and olmesartan-associated sprue-like enteropathy are high-consequence concerns.

Contraindications

  • Pregnancy and coadministration with aliskiren in patients with diabetes. Avoid duplicate ACE inhibitor, ARB, or ARNI therapy without a specific specialist rationale.

  • Clarify severe hyperkalemia, symptomatic hypotension, evolving AKI, marked volume depletion, or suspected bilateral renal-artery stenosis.

Nursing Essentials

  • Match the drug to its actual indication instead of treating every -sartan as identical. Check pressure, orthostasis, potassium, creatinine/eGFR, volume, pregnancy status, albuminuria goal, and every other RAAS or potassium-raising drug.

  • Ask about chronic diarrhea and weight loss with olmesartan, gout/uric-acid context with losartan, heart-failure dosing with valsartan or candesartan, and diabetic-nephropathy evidence with losartan or irbesartan.

Interactions

  • Potassium products, MRAs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, lithium, diuretics, and other RAAS blockers increase hyperkalemia, renal, pressure, or lithium risk.

  • Sacubitril/valsartan already contains valsartan. Adding a separate ARB duplicates AT1 blockade; adding an ACE inhibitor also creates the contraindicated ARNI/ACE combination.

Key Labs

  • Blood pressure, orthostasis, creatinine/eGFR, BUN, potassium, volume status, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio when indicated, and agent-specific liver or GI assessment.

  • Recheck creatinine and potassium after initiation or titration. A fall in eGFR of 30% or more warrants evaluation for AKI drivers, volume depletion, NSAIDs, or renal-artery stenosis.

Nclex Traps

  • ARBs block the receptor; they do not reduce angiotensin II production and do not directly increase bradykinin. Saying they work by lowering angiotensin II is mechanistically wrong.

  • Less cough does not mean no monitoring. Fetal renal injury, hyperkalemia, hypotension, and hemodynamic kidney injury remain class-defining.
