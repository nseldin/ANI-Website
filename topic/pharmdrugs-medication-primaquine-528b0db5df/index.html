Used To Treat

Radical cure and relapse prevention after blood-stage treatment of P. vivax or P. ovale malaria, terminal prophylaxis in selected travelers, and transmission-blocking gametocyte treatment in specified protocols. Quantitative G6PD testing is required before exposure.

Mechanism

Hepatic CYP metabolism produces multiple primaquine metabolites capable of redox cycling. Oxidative reactive oxygen species and altered electron transport injure parasite mitochondria, membranes, and other macromolecules in hypnozoites and gametocytes; no single molecular target explains all activity. Human red cells are collateral targets because they lack nuclei and mitochondria and cannot replace damaged proteins. G6PD drives the first pentose-phosphate step that generates NADPH. NADPH allows glutathione reductase to maintain reduced glutathione, which detoxifies peroxides. In deficiency, oxidant metabolites denature hemoglobin and damage spectrin and membrane lipids, producing bite cells, Heinz bodies, dark urine, jaundice, anemia, and potentially kidney injury.

Contraindications

  • Severe G6PD deficiency, pregnancy, incompatible breastfeeding status, and concurrent marrow-suppressive quinacrine or major marrow disease under label restrictions.

  • Do not start without completing an effective blood-stage regimen and confirming that radical cure is indicated.

Nursing Essentials

  • Record the numeric G6PD result and reference range. Recent hemolysis or transfusion can make the result falsely reassuring by enriching the sample with younger or donor red cells.

  • Give with food if GI upset occurs and teach hemolysis and methemoglobinemia symptoms. Do not dismiss cyanosis with a pulse-oximetry plateau as anxiety.

Interactions

  • Other oxidant drugs, including dapsone, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, or rasburicase contexts, can add hemolytic or methemoglobin stress.

  • Marrow suppressants and QT-active drugs require patient-specific review.

Key Labs

  • Quantitative G6PD, baseline CBC/hemoglobin, pregnancy assessment, species and blood-stage treatment confirmation

  • CBC, reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, urinalysis, creatinine, oxygenation and methemoglobin if symptoms develop

Nclex Traps

  • Primaquine targets dormant liver hypnozoites; it is not a stand-alone treatment for acute blood-stage malaria.

  • G6PD testing must precede treatment because waiting for hemolysis is waiting for preventable red-cell destruction.

Boxed Warning

Serious hemolytic anemia can occur in G6PD deficiency. Obtain quantitative G6PD activity before treatment, because qualitative tests may miss intermediate activity in heterozygous females. Primaquine is contraindicated in pregnancy because fetal G6PD status is unknown and in breastfeeding when the infant is G6PD deficient or status is unknown. Stop and evaluate dark urine, pallor, jaundice, dyspnea, tachycardia, dizziness, or falling hemoglobin. If hemolysis is suspected, monitor CBC and reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, and urine because severe red-cell destruction can cause tissue hypoxia and hemoglobin-pigment kidney injury. Methemoglobinemia can impair oxygen delivery even when PaO2 is normal; unexplained cyanosis or a pulse-oximetry plateau warrants co-oximetry rather than assuming anxiety or simple hypoxemia.

Description

Primaquine is an 8-aminoquinoline antimalarial whose oxidative metabolites damage and kill dormant Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale liver hypnozoites, eliminating the reservoir responsible for relapse after blood smears clear. It also kills mature Plasmodium falciparum gametocytes, reducing transmission, but is not sufficient blood-stage monotherapy for acute malaria. The same redox chemistry can overwhelm erythrocytes with low glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase activity. G6PD supplies NADPH that keeps glutathione reduced. Without that protection, oxidized hemoglobin forms Heinz bodies and membranes become fragile, causing intravascular and splenic hemolysis. Quantitative testing, pregnancy exclusion, symptom teaching, and follow-up therefore arise directly from mechanism.

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea, abdominal cramps, headache, dizziness, pruritus, and methemoglobinemia

  • Dose-related hemolysis, severe anemia, leukopenia or agranulocytosis, arrhythmia at excessive exposure, and acute kidney injury secondary to hemolysis
