Definition

Peritoneal dialysis fills the abdominal cavity with sterile dialysate so the peritoneal capillary membrane can exchange solute and water during a dwell, after which fluid drains and a new exchange begins. Clearance depends on membrane transport, dwell time, fill volume, solution, number of exchanges, residual kidney function, and reliable technique.

Etiology

PD treats kidney failure and selected AKI when the peritoneum and abdominal access are usable. Continuous ambulatory PD uses manual daytime exchanges, while automated PD uses a cycler, commonly overnight; the best schedule is individualized.

Pathology

A catheter delivers dialysate into the peritoneal cavity. Capillary blood and dialysate are separated by endothelium, interstitium, and mesothelium; effective surface area and transport characteristics vary among patients and over time.

Pathophysiology

Urea, potassium, and other small solutes diffuse from blood into dialysate down concentration gradients. Dextrose or icodextrin creates an osmotic force that draws water across the membrane; the gradient weakens during long dwells as glucose is absorbed, so ultrafiltration can fall or reverse. Fast transporters equilibrate small solutes quickly but may lose the crystalloid osmotic gradient early, while slower transporters need longer dwells for solute clearance. Residual kidney function contributes substantially to total clearance and volume control and should be preserved.

Risk Factors

  • Breaks in connection or hand-hygiene technique

  • Prior abdominal surgery, hernia, leaks, obesity, respiratory limitation, or ostomy affecting feasibility

  • High glucose exposure, diabetes, or loss of membrane ultrafiltration

  • Constipation and catheter migration causing poor drainage

Signs Symptoms

  • Clear effluent and comfortable exchanges are expected

  • Cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, fever, nausea, or drainage change suggests peritonitis

  • Poor inflow/outflow, alarms, constipation, leak, genital edema, hernia, or new dyspnea can reflect mechanical problems

  • Weight gain, hyperglycemia, edema, or inadequate clearance can reflect prescription or membrane issues

Diagnostics

  • Inspect every drained bag for clarity, volume, fibrin, or blood

  • Send effluent cell count with differential, Gram stain, and culture promptly when peritonitis is suspected

  • Assess catheter exit site and tunnel

  • Evaluate adequacy, residual clearance, volume, transport status, mechanical function, nutrition, and glucose exposure

Labs

  • Effluent leukocytes and neutrophil predominance support peritonitis in the correct dwell and symptom context

  • Culture identifies organism and guides therapy but can be negative

  • Potassium may be low because PD continuously removes it

  • Glucose, albumin, electrolytes, bicarbonate, phosphorus, and total clearance require longitudinal review

Treatments

  • Use aseptic exchanges and individualized dwell, solution, and schedule

  • Treat peritonitis promptly with route and agents guided by ISPD recommendations, local resistance, organism, severity, and residual function

  • Correct constipation, catheter position, leaks, hernias, or membrane ultrafiltration failure

  • Remove the catheter when refractory, relapsing, fungal, or other high-risk infection criteria make source control necessary

Nursing Priorities

  • Verify the prescribed solution, fill volume, dwell time, number of exchanges, additives, last treatment, residual kidney function, and catheter history because clearance and ultrafiltration depend on the complete individualized prescription.

  • Monitor daily weight, blood pressure, edema, breath sounds, intake and output, drain volume, effluent clarity, glucose, electrolytes, uremic symptoms, exit site, and ordered adequacy measures because underdialysis, fluid overload, infection, and metabolic effects may develop gradually.

  • Perform each connection with meticulous hand hygiene, mask use, aseptic technique, correct warming, complete drainage, and accurate timing because touch contamination, shortened dwell, or flow obstruction reduces effectiveness and increases peritonitis risk.

  • Coordinate transport testing and prescription adjustment when ultrafiltration, residual function, body size, nutrition, or clinical goals change because membrane transport characteristics determine which dwell lengths and solutions achieve useful clearance.

  • Escalate immediately for cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, fever, hypotension, inability to drain, new bloody effluent, severe dyspnea, or rapidly increasing weight because peritonitis, catheter obstruction, bleeding, or dangerous volume overload requires urgent treatment.

Complications

  • Peritonitis, exit-site or tunnel infection, and sepsis

  • Catheter obstruction or migration, leak, hernia, hydrothorax, or genital edema

  • Hyperglycemia, weight gain, dyslipidemia, protein loss, malnutrition, or hypokalemia

  • Ultrafiltration failure, membrane fibrosis, and technique failure

Contraindications

  • PD feasibility must be individualized; an unusable peritoneum, uncorrectable mechanical problem, or inability to perform safe exchanges without support may require another plan

  • Do not dismiss cloudy effluent because pain or fever is absent

Red Flags

  • Cloudy effluent with abdominal pain, fever, or systemic illness

  • Absent or sharply reduced drainage with pain or progressive distention

  • New persistent bloody effluent, hypotension, or falling hemoglobin

  • Severe dyspnea, hypoxemia, pulmonary crackles, or rapid weight gain

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to inspect every drained bag against good light because cloudy effluent may be the earliest visible sign of peritonitis.

  • Explain that dwell timing and prescribed volumes should not be shortened for convenience because transport and total clearance depend on completing the full regimen.

Nclex Traps

  • PD uses the peritoneum as the membrane, not the stomach

  • Cloudy effluent is an urgent infection clue

  • Dwell time has different effects in fast and slow transporters

  • Drainage problems are often mechanical but infection must still be considered
