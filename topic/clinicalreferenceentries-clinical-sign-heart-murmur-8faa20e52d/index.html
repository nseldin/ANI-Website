Definition

Heart murmur means an extra sound is heard while listening to blood move through the heart or nearby great vessels. It describes an auscultatory finding, not a diagnosis of one valve disorder.

Plain Language

A heart murmur is an extra whooshing, blowing, rumbling, or harsh sound heard between or across the usual heart sounds.

Why It Matters

Some systolic murmurs are harmless flow sounds, but others reveal valve narrowing or leakage, a congenital opening, infection, or another structural problem. Timing, location, radiation, symptoms, and echocardiography determine the cause; a new murmur with fever, fainting, breathing difficulty, chest pain, low blood pressure, or sudden deterioration can signal an emergency.

Associated Conditions

  • flow-induced vibration from increased velocity or volume, valve narrowing, valve leakage, intracardiac shunting, outflow obstruction, a dilated great vessel, reduced blood viscosity, or an innocent physiologic flow state

Assessment Method

Auscultate systematically in a warm quiet setting with the diaphragm and bell while identifying S1 and S2 and timing the sound with the carotid pulse; document systolic, diastolic, or continuous timing, point of maximum intensity, radiation, pitch, quality, configuration, duration, intensity grade, associated thrill or extra sounds, position and respiration effects, symptoms, pulse findings, and dynamic maneuvers only when safe and clinically appropriate.

Positive Finding

An extra sustained or patterned sound is heard between, through, or across normal heart sounds and arises from the heart or great vessels.

Mechanism

Accelerated or redirected blood flow and pressure gradients create vibrations in blood, valves, chambers, or vessel walls that transmit to the chest; the pattern depends on timing, anatomy, flow, and loading conditions.

Evidence Limitations

Detection and grading vary with room noise, body habitus, heart rate, blood pressure, anemia, pregnancy, fever, position, stethoscope technique, and examiner experience; loudness does not reliably equal lesion severity and an absent murmur does not exclude important valve disease. False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from normal heart sounds, S3 gallop, S4 gallop, pericardial friction rub, pleural friction rub, carotid bruit, innocent flow murmur, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Heart murmur may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Current core cardiovascular finding but not a diagnosis; many systolic murmurs are innocent or flow related, while structural causes and diastolic or continuous patterns require cause-directed evaluation and echocardiography when indicated. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not label a murmur innocent or diagnose a specific valve lesion from one sound alone; a new murmur with chest pain, syncope, severe dyspnea, hypotension, pulmonary edema, fever or bacteremia, embolic findings, acute neurologic change, or deterioration after myocardial infarction can signal critical valve failure, endocarditis, dissection, or mechanical complication and requires urgent evaluation.
