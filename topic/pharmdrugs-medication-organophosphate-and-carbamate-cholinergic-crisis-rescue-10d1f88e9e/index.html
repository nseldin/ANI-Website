Used To Treat

Suspected severe poisoning by organophosphate pesticides, anticholinesterase nerve agents, or selected carbamates when excess acetylcholine causes dangerous secretions, bronchospasm, bradycardia, weakness, seizures, or respiratory failure. This pathway integrates rescuer safety, decontamination, atropine, pralidoxime when indicated, and organ support.

Description

Cholinergic crisis is a time-critical syndrome caused by excessive acetylcholine at muscarinic, nicotinic, and central synapses. The same mechanism creates a recognizable but variable combination: wet airways and secretions, bronchospasm, vomiting/diarrhea, sweating and miosis from muscarinic signaling; fasciculations, weakness and paralysis from nicotinic signaling; and agitation, seizures or coma from central signaling. Rescue works only when those domains are treated together. Atropine immediately blocks muscarinic receptors, pralidoxime can restore organophosphate-inhibited acetylcholinesterase before aging, benzodiazepines suppress seizures, and ventilation buys time while enzyme and neuromuscular function recover. Decontamination protects both the patient and staff from continued absorption.

Mechanism

Acetylcholinesterase normally ends synaptic signaling by hydrolyzing acetylcholine. Organophosphates phosphorylate the enzyme and carbamates carbamylate it, so acetylcholine accumulates. Atropine competes only at muscarinic receptors and therefore dries secretions and improves bronchospasm/bradycardia but cannot reverse nicotinic respiratory-muscle failure. Pralidoxime nucleophilically removes many organophosphate groups before the enzyme ages and thereby restores hydrolysis at muscarinic and nicotinic synapses; carbamylated enzyme often reactivates spontaneously, making oxime benefit in pure carbamate poisoning less certain. Benzodiazepines enhance inhibitory GABA signaling to interrupt seizures. PPE and clothing/skin decontamination remove an external toxin reservoir, preventing ongoing dermal absorption and secondary poisoning of rescuers.

Administration Timing

  • Rescuer safety comes first: use incident-appropriate PPE, move away from ongoing exposure if safe, remove contaminated clothing, bag it, and irrigate skin or eyes according to emergency guidance while airway suction, oxygen, monitoring, and IV access begin.

  • Give atropine immediately and rapidly titrate to improving oxygenation/ventilation and control of bronchorrhea and bronchospasm. Do not wait for miosis to resolve or for a cholinesterase assay.

  • Start pralidoxime early for life-threatening organophosphate or nerve-agent poisoning, alongside atropine rather than after it. Ask medical toxicology whether it is appropriate for a suspected pure carbamate because agent chemistry and evidence differ.

  • Treat seizures promptly with benzodiazepines and intubate/ventilate for fatigue, hypoxemia, hypercapnia, coma, or weakness. Avoid assuming a dry airway means neuromuscular recovery.

Nursing Essentials

  • Create hot, warm, and clean zones when contamination is possible, limit personnel, protect skin/eyes/airway, and handle clothing and secretions as hazardous because secondary organophosphate exposure can disable the care team.

  • Assign separate roles for suction/airway, decontamination, atropine titration, pralidoxime preparation, seizure treatment, monitoring, and documentation so the antidote sequence does not delay ventilation.

  • Use bronchial secretion burden, wheeze, oxygenation, capnography, chest compliance, heart rate/blood pressure, fasciculations, proximal strength, and mental status as repeated physiologic endpoints. Pupil size is not an atropine endpoint.

  • Track all atropine auto-injectors and hospital doses plus pralidoxime formulation because combination devices can create hidden duplicate dosing. Reassess contaminated skin folds, hair, wounds, and gut exposure when requirements remain high.

  • For intubation, alert anesthesia that succinylcholine and mivacurium may be prolonged by cholinesterase inhibition; use the emergency protocol's paralytic strategy.

Key Labs

  • Blood gas, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, CBC, renal/hepatic function, CK, ECG, chest imaging when aspiration or pulmonary injury is suspected, and continuous pulse oximetry/capnography.

  • Plasma butyrylcholinesterase and red-cell acetylcholinesterase can support diagnosis and trend recovery, but baseline variation and assay delays limit acute decisions. Treat the toxidrome first.

  • Document exposure route/time, suspected product, odor or container information without contaminating clean areas, decontamination, cumulative antidotes, ventilator measures, seizures, urine output, and recurrence.

Adverse Effects

  • Atropine excess can cause hyperthermia, tachydysrhythmia, ileus, urinary retention, delirium and dry flushed skin; these findings must be balanced against the lethal risk of undertreated bronchorrhea.

  • Rapid pralidoxime can cause hypertension, tachycardia, rigidity, laryngospasm, or transient weakness. Decontamination can cause hypothermia, and aggressive suction/intubation carries usual airway risks.

Contraindications

  • No routine atropine contraindication should delay treatment of life-threatening muscarinic toxicity because untreated bronchorrhea and bronchospasm can rapidly prevent ventilation. Pralidoxime benefit falls after enzyme aging because the phosphorylated bond becomes resistant to oxime cleavage, while carbamylated enzyme may reactivate spontaneously; therefore, do not use a blanket oxime rule for every wet toxidrome.

  • Anticholinergic poisoning is the mechanistic opposite and does not belong on this pathway. Muscarinic mushroom poisoning can respond to atropine but does not automatically require pralidoxime because acetylcholinesterase was not phosphorylated.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Persistent hypoxemia, rising carbon dioxide, wet lungs, shock, seizure, or worsening weakness despite antidotes requires immediate critical-care toxicology support, mechanical ventilation, continued atropine titration, and reassessment of the agent and absorption route.

  • Intermediate syndrome with delayed neck, proximal, cranial, or respiratory weakness can occur after the acute cholinergic phase. Continue respiratory surveillance and do not discharge solely because secretions are controlled.

  • Recurrent atropine need after weaning suggests ongoing absorption or redistribution; return to the last effective regimen and repeat exposure/decontamination assessment.

Interactions

  • Succinylcholine and mivacurium depend on cholinesterase and can be prolonged. Other antimuscarinic drugs can add to atropine toxicity, while beta blockers may obscure tachycardia without correcting secretions.

Evidence Limitations

  • AHA 2025 gives immediate atropine a Class 1, Level A recommendation and considers pralidoxime reasonable in life-threatening organophosphate poisoning. Oxime trials remain heterogeneous because agents, aging rates, dose, and treatment delays differ.

  • This pathway does not replace a hazardous-materials plan or product-specific public-health instructions. Unknown pesticide mixtures require real-time poison-center interpretation.

Nclex Traps

  • Wet lungs are the atropine endpoint: titrate until ventilation improves rather than until pupils dilate because pupil size does not measure airway muscarinic burden.

  • Atropine treats muscarinic effects; pralidoxime may restore the enzyme and improve nicotinic weakness before aging.

  • A dry chest does not prove the diaphragm is strong. Continue neuromuscular and ventilatory assessment.

  • Protect the care team before touching a contaminated patient.

Regulatory Status

Integrated evidence-based emergency pathway. Atropine and pralidoxime products have relevant FDA-labeled indications, while exact sequence, dosing, PPE, and carbamate decisions follow the current emergency and poison-center protocol.
