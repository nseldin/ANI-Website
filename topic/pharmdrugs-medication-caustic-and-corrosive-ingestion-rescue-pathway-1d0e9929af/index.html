Used To Treat

Suspected acid, alkali, oxidizer, or other corrosive ingestion causing oropharyngeal, esophageal, gastric, airway, or systemic injury, including initially asymptomatic high-risk exposures.

Description

A caustic or corrosive is a concentrated acid, alkali such as sodium hydroxide, or oxidizer that damages mucosal tissue directly on contact, so deep necrosis can progress after the swallow. Alkalis tend to saponify fat and cause liquefactive necrosis that penetrates deeply, while acids often produce coagulation necrosis and gastric injury. Concentration, amount, contact time, formulation, and tissue location matter more than the simple acid-versus-base label. Oral burns, drooling, dysphagia, odynophagia, chest or abdominal pain, vomiting, hematemesis, hoarseness, stridor, and respiratory distress raise concern, but a normal-looking mouth does not exclude severe esophageal or gastric injury. Making the patient vomit exposes the esophagus and airway again. Neutralizing acid with base or base with acid can generate heat and worsen injury. Routine dilution, charcoal, blind nasogastric placement, and lavage can add aspiration, obscure evaluation, or perforate injured tissue. Rescue protects the airway, keeps the patient NPO, identifies the product, controls pain and vomiting, and uses expert CT, endoscopy, surgical, ENT, and GI assessment to stage injury and anticipate perforation or later stricture.

Mechanism

Hydrogen ions, hydroxide ions, oxidants, and other reactive chemicals denature proteins, disrupt cell membranes, thrombose microvasculature, and extend necrosis beyond the visible surface. Edema can progressively narrow the upper airway, while full-thickness esophageal or gastric injury permits mediastinal or peritoneal contamination, sepsis, and shock. Preventing emesis and repeated swallowing limits renewed contact; airway control before edema peaks preserves oxygenation. CT can identify transmural necrosis and extra-luminal injury, while endoscopy directly grades mucosal damage when used in the appropriate patient and time window. Neither test is a universal substitute for expert examination. Healing collagen then contracts, explaining dysphagia and strictures weeks later. Because neutralization releases heat and cannot reverse established necrosis, treatment supports tissue, detects depth, and intervenes on complications instead of trying home chemistry.

Administration Timing

  • Call 911 for drooling, trouble swallowing, hoarseness, stridor, breathing difficulty, chest or abdominal pain, vomiting blood, severe pain, or intentional ingestion; call Poison Help immediately for any possible corrosive. Bring the original container without exposing rescuers.

  • Keep the patient NPO, position to reduce aspiration, suction secretions, give oxygen and analgesia, and secure the airway early with the most experienced team when voice change, stridor, edema, or respiratory distress suggests progression. Do not induce vomiting, neutralize, lavage, or give charcoal, food, drink, or a blind tube.

  • Consult gastroenterology, surgery, ENT, radiology, and toxicology early. Use contrast-enhanced CT and/or carefully timed endoscopy according to stability, product, symptoms, and local caustic-injury protocol; perforation, necrosis, peritonitis, mediastinitis, or shock requires immediate surgical escalation.

Nursing Essentials

  • Identify product name, ingredients, concentration, pH if safely available from documentation, amount, time, physical form, intent, co-ingestants, vomiting, and any first aid already attempted. Do not ask staff to smell or directly test an unknown chemical.

  • Trend voice, work of breathing, drooling, oral secretions, pain location, abdominal rigidity, emesis or bleeding, fever, perfusion, urine output, and mental status because airway edema, perforation, hemorrhage, sepsis, and shock can evolve after presentation. Maintain strict NPO status because oral intake can provoke vomiting or aspiration and interfere with urgent airway or endoscopic planning; avoid repeated oral examinations because gagging can traumatize injured tissue or trigger emesis.

  • After acute staging, coordinate nutrition, swallow safety, dental or oral care, psychosocial assessment, and explicit follow-up for new dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, reflux, chest pain, fever, or bleeding because strictures and fistulas can emerge later.

Key Labs

  • Obtain CBC, electrolytes, bicarbonate, creatinine, liver studies, blood gas and lactate for systemic illness, coagulation, type and screen, and pregnancy testing when relevant because bleeding, shock, acidosis, and organ injury can require immediate intervention. Use imaging to evaluate chest and abdominal injury because normal laboratory results do not exclude localized deep burns.

  • Product pH alone does not grade tissue injury, and absence of oral burns does not exclude esophageal injury. CT and endoscopy answer different questions and must be interpreted with symptoms, timing, stability, and surgical findings.

Adverse Effects

  • Complications include airway obstruction, aspiration, esophageal or gastric necrosis, hemorrhage, perforation, mediastinitis, peritonitis, fistula, sepsis, shock, stricture, malnutrition, dysmotility, and later malignancy risk after severe chronic injury.

  • Intubation can be difficult through edema; endoscopy or blind tubes can perforate selected high-risk injuries; opioids can depress ventilation; and unnecessary antibiotics or corticosteroids add adverse effects without universal stricture-prevention benefit.

Contraindications

  • Never induce vomiting, perform gastric lavage, neutralize the chemical, or give routine charcoal. Do not blindly pass a nasogastric or orogastric tube through potentially necrotic tissue.

  • Do not use a normal mouth examination, early pain improvement, or one normal radiograph to rule out severe injury because each can understate deeper esophageal or gastric necrosis. Do not use routine corticosteroids or prophylactic antibiotics without injury-specific specialist rationale because benefit and harm depend on burn depth, infection, and perforation risk.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Stridor, respiratory distress, severe chest or abdominal pain, rigidity, shock, rising lactate, free air, mediastinal or peritoneal signs, hematemesis, or tissue necrosis requires ICU and urgent airway or surgical care. Weeks later, progressive dysphagia, vomiting, weight loss, fever, or bleeding requires prompt stricture or fistula evaluation.

Interactions

  • Hydrofluoric acid and some industrial agents add systemic electrolyte or organ toxicity that requires a separate substance-specific antidote pathway. Household bleach concentration is often lower, but mixing, aspiration, large volume, or concentrated industrial hypochlorite changes risk.

  • Sedatives, alcohol, or neurologic co-ingestants increase aspiration and obscure pain. Activated charcoal can hide mucosa without meaningfully binding many corrosives and can complicate endoscopy.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Young children

    Note

    Exploratory sips may be small but the product, concentration, and symptoms—not caregiver reassurance—determine need for urgent expert assessment.

  • Population

    Intentional adult ingestion

    Note

    Volumes and concentrations are often larger and injury more extensive; early airway, CT, surgery, and mental-health coordination are critical.

  • Population

    Pregnancy

    Note

    Maternal airway, perforation, sepsis, and shock are immediate fetal threats; do not delay indicated imaging or surgery while adding obstetric support.

Evidence Limitations

  • Caustic-management studies are heterogeneous and CT-versus-endoscopy algorithms vary by center. WSES guidance supports preventing repeat exposure and using expert injury staging, while steroid, antibiotic, feeding, and surveillance choices remain grade and context dependent.

Nclex Traps

  • The absence of mouth burns does not rule out deep esophageal or gastric injury.

  • Neutralizing an acid with base or alkali with acid can create an exothermic second burn.

  • Induced vomiting exposes the injured esophagus and airway to the corrosive again.

Regulatory Status

This is an injury-staging and rescue pathway, not an antidote indication. Endoscopy, CT, antibiotics, corticosteroids, feeding, and surgery follow local specialist protocols; no universal neutralizing drug exists.

Boxed Warning

CAUSTIC-INGESTION WARNING: call 911 for drooling, hoarseness, breathing trouble, chest or abdominal pain, bleeding, or intentional exposure and call Poison Help immediately. Keep NPO; never induce vomiting, neutralize, lavage, give charcoal, or pass a blind tube because repeat contact, heat, aspiration, or perforation can worsen injury.

Boxed Warning Specificity

The visible warning identifies the emergency, explains the mechanism of harm, and separates immediate stabilization from specialist antidote or elimination decisions.
