Definition

Exophthalmos, also called proptosis, means one or both eyeballs are displaced forward from the bony eye socket.

Plain Language

Exophthalmos means the eye is pushed or bulging forward.

Why It Matters

Forward displacement can expose and injure the cornea or compress the optic nerve. Pain, redness, fever, vision change, abnormal eye movement, trauma, pulsation, or rapid progression can indicate infection, hemorrhage, vascular disease, or orbital compartment syndrome and requires urgent evaluation.

Associated Conditions

  • thyroid eye disease, orbital inflammation or cellulitis, hemorrhage, vascular fistula, tumor, trauma, or other processes that increase orbital contents or push the globe forward

Assessment Method

Inspect from the front and above without pressing on the eye, compare sides and prior photographs, assess eyelid closure, corneal exposure, visual acuity, color vision, pupils, eye movements, diplopia, pain, redness, fever, pulsation or bruit, thyroid findings, trauma and surgery history, and measure globe projection with exophthalmometry when trained.

Positive Finding

One or both eyeballs are displaced forward relative to the bony orbital rims, sometimes with widened lid opening, incomplete closure, corneal exposure, impaired movement, or optic-nerve dysfunction.

Mechanism

Swelling, immune-cell infiltration, enlarged muscles, expanded fat, blood, vascular congestion, infection, or a mass raises pressure or volume within the confined orbit and displaces the globe anteriorly.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Exophthalmos finding raises concern for thyroid eye disease, orbital inflammation or cellulitis, hemorrhage, vascular fistula, tumor, trauma, or other processes that increase orbital contents or push the globe forward because swelling, immune-cell infiltration, enlarged muscles, expanded fat, blood, vascular congestion, infection, or a mass raises pressure or volume within the confined orbit and displaces the globe anteriorly. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Exophthalmos does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Exophthalmos to focus evaluation of thyroid eye disease, orbital inflammation or cellulitis, hemorrhage, vascular fistula, tumor, trauma, or other processes that increase orbital contents or push the globe forward and the reviewed alternatives: eyelid retraction, pseudoproptosis from a large globe, contralateral enophthalmos, facial asymmetry. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Inspect from the front and above without pressing on the eye, compare sides and prior photographs, assess eyelid closure, corneal exposure, visual acuity, color vision, pupils, eye movements, diplopia, pain, redness, fever, pulsation or bruit, thyroid findings, trauma and surgery history, and measure globe projection with exophthalmometry when trained. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Exophthalmos as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Normal globe projection varies with orbital anatomy, age, sex, ancestry, refractive anatomy, and measurement technique; eyelid retraction or a large globe can mimic protrusion, and a photograph alone cannot establish true exophthalmos or its cause. Therefore, Exophthalmos cannot confirm or exclude thyroid eye disease, orbital inflammation or cellulitis, hemorrhage, vascular fistula, tumor, trauma, or other processes that increase orbital contents or push the globe forward by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • eyelid retraction

  • pseudoproptosis from a large globe

  • contralateral enophthalmos

  • facial asymmetry

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as eyelid retraction, pseudoproptosis from a large globe, contralateral enophthalmos, facial asymmetry.

False Negative Situations

Exophthalmos may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of thyroid eye disease, orbital inflammation or cellulitis, hemorrhage, vascular fistula, tumor, trauma, or other processes that increase orbital contents or push the globe forward.

Modern Status

Current descriptive orbital sign, also called proptosis; it does not identify the cause, and severe exposure, optic-nerve compression, infection, hemorrhage, or orbital compartment syndrome can threaten vision. Safety Precautions

Observe Exophthalmos with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect from the front and above without pressing on the eye, compare sides and prior photographs, assess eyelid closure, corneal exposure, visual acuity, color vision, pupils, eye movements, diplopia, pain, redness, fever, pulsation or bruit, thyroid findings, trauma and surgery history, and measure globe projection with exophthalmometry when trained.
