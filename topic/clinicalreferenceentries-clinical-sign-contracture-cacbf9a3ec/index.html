Definition

Contracture is a fixed loss of passive range of motion caused by shortening or stiffening of muscle, tendon, ligament, joint capsule, fascia, or skin. Unlike spasticity, the restriction does not become greater simply because the examiner moves faster.

Plain Language

A contracture means a joint or body part has become fixed in a shortened or stiff position and cannot be moved through its full range even when the person relaxes.

Why It Matters

Contracture can impair mobility, hygiene, positioning, skin protection, comfort, and independence. Early recognition supports positioning and rehabilitation before restriction becomes more fixed, while forceful stretching can injure tissue and does not establish the cause.

Assessment Method

Inspect resting posture and alignment, ask about duration and function, compare active and slow passive range on both sides, identify the restricted tissue and end feel, and measure with a goniometer when trending; also assess pain, tone, strength, skin, sensation, and spasticity at different movement speeds.

Positive Finding

A joint or body part cannot reach its expected passive range because a fixed shortened or stiff tissue consistently limits movement regardless of movement speed.

Mechanism

Prolonged shortening, fibrosis, scar formation, muscle imbalance, or joint-capsule change reduces tissue extensibility and fixes the body part within a restricted range.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Contracture finding raises concern for shortening or loss of elasticity in muscle, tendon, ligament, joint capsule, fascia, or skin after immobility, scarring, neurologic injury, or chronic joint disease because prolonged shortening, fibrosis, scar formation, muscle imbalance, or joint-capsule change reduces tissue extensibility and fixes the body part within a restricted range. Evidence Limitations

Pain, voluntary guarding, rigidity, spasticity, positioning, and an unrelaxed patient can mimic a fixed endpoint; one examination does not establish the involved tissue or reversibility. Therefore, Contracture cannot confirm or exclude shortening or loss of elasticity in muscle, tendon, ligament, joint capsule, fascia, or skin after immobility, scarring, neurologic injury, or chronic joint disease by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

  • joint ankylosis

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as spasticity, rigidity, pain-limited motion, joint ankylosis.

False Negative Situations

Contracture may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of shortening or loss of elasticity in muscle, tendon, ligament, joint capsule, fascia, or skin after immobility, scarring, neurologic injury, or chronic joint disease.

Modern Status

Current structural finding; it is distinct from velocity-dependent spasticity and may become increasingly fixed without positioning, mobility, and cause-directed rehabilitation. Safety Precautions

Observe Contracture with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Inspect resting posture and alignment, ask about duration and function, compare active and slow passive range on both sides, identify the restricted tissue and end feel, and measure with a goniometer when trending; also assess pain, tone, strength, skin, sensation, and spasticity at different movement speeds. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not force a contracted joint or apply unsupervised prolonged stretch; protect fragile skin and neurovascular structures, and escalate rapidly increasing pain, new deformity, skin breakdown, impaired perfusion, or loss of function.
