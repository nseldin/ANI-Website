Definition

Moses sign is a bedside calf-compression maneuver. It is a clue to possible deep venous thrombosis, historically. A positive finding means pain is greater with anteroposterior compression.

Plain Language

In plain language, a positive Moses sign means pain is greater with anteroposterior compression.

Why It Matters

A positive Moses sign response can signal possible deep venous thrombosis, historically. Deep inflamed veins were thought to be stressed differently by compression direction. Moses sign cannot confirm that association by itself; assess it using the method and limits defined for this finding.

Associated Conditions

  • possible deep venous thrombosis, historically

Assessment Method

Compress the calf gently in anteroposterior then lateral direction only if no safer pathway is available.

Positive Finding

Pain is greater with anteroposterior compression.

Mechanism

Deep inflamed veins were thought to be stressed differently by compression direction.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Moses sign response raises concern for possible deep venous thrombosis, historically because deep inflamed veins were thought to be stressed differently by compression direction. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Moses sign does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Moses sign to focus evaluation of possible deep venous thrombosis, historically and the reviewed alternatives: cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, hematoma. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Compress the calf gently in anteroposterior then lateral direction only if no safer pathway is available. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Moses sign as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Pain is nonspecific and diagnostic accuracy is inadequate. Therefore, Moses sign cannot confirm or exclude possible deep venous thrombosis, historically by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

Differential Diagnoses

  • cellulitis

  • ruptured Baker cyst

  • hematoma

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, hematoma, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Moses sign may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.

Modern Status

Outdated, unreliable, and unnecessary. Moses sign remains searchable because learners still encounter the term, but it cannot confirm or exclude possible deep venous thrombosis, historically. Apply the reviewed limitation for Moses sign: Pain is nonspecific and diagnostic accuracy is inadequate.

Safety Precautions

Before assessing Moses sign, explain the method, obtain consent, preserve privacy, and use only the technique described here: Compress the calf gently in anteroposterior then lateral direction only if no safer pathway is available. Stop if that technique becomes unsafe. The reviewed precaution for this maneuver is: Do not provoke calf pain; use pretest probability, D-dimer, and ultrasound.
