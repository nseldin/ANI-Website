Summary

The spinal cord is a segmented central pathway that carries ascending sensory information, descending motor commands, and autonomic signals while generating rapid reflexes. Dorsal columns carry vibration, fine touch, and conscious position sense; spinothalamic pathways carry pain, temperature, and crude touch; corticospinal pathways carry skilled voluntary movement. Their different crossing points create recognizable lesion patterns.

Quick Answer

A reflex arc can act through receptor, sensory neuron, spinal integration, motor neuron, and effector before conscious awareness, but the brain still modulates it. Spinal-cord injury can therefore remove descending control while leaving local circuits intact, producing an early flaccid spinal-shock phase followed later by hyperreflexia or spasticity below many upper motor-neuron lesions.

Sections

    • Cord, roots, and segments

    • The spinal cord extends from medulla through the vertebral canal and usually ends near the L1-L2 vertebral level in adults, continuing as the conus medullaris and cauda equina roots. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves connect through dorsal sensory roots and ventral motor roots. A dorsal-root ganglion contains sensory neuron cell bodies. Gray matter forms central horns containing interneurons and motor or autonomic neurons; white-matter columns contain ascending and descending axons. Cord segment does not always align with the same-numbered vertebra, especially inferiorly, so clinical level, imaging level, and vertebral injury must be described precisely.

    • Dorsal-column pathway

    • Dorsal columns carry vibration, discriminative touch, and conscious proprioception upward on the same side of the spinal cord. Fibers synapse in the medulla and then cross before reaching thalamus and cortex. A unilateral cord lesion can therefore reduce these modalities below the lesion on the same side. Bedside testing includes vibration, joint position, and refined touch, interpreted with peripheral nerve and root patterns. Loss produces sensory ataxia that worsens when visual compensation is removed, which is why a positive Romberg pattern differs from primary cerebellar ataxia.

    • Spinothalamic and corticospinal pathways

    • Pain and temperature fibers enter the cord, synapse, cross within a short distance through the anterior white commissure, and ascend contralaterally in the anterolateral system. This creates contralateral loss beginning slightly below a unilateral cord lesion. Corticospinal fibers descend after crossing mainly in the lower medulla, so a spinal lesion produces ipsilateral upper motor-neuron weakness below the lesion. At the injured segment, anterior-horn or root damage can produce lower motor-neuron findings. These crossing patterns explain dissociated deficits in Brown-Sequard syndrome and why one sensory level never replaces a full examination.

    • Reflex arc and descending control

    • A receptor detects stretch, pain, or another stimulus; a sensory afferent enters the cord; one or more synapses integrate the signal; a motor efferent activates muscle or gland. The patellar stretch reflex can be largely monosynaptic, while withdrawal and crossed-extensor reflexes use interneuron networks. Descending pathways normally tune reflex gain and inhibit inappropriate responses. Acute severe cord injury can transiently suppress reflexes below the lesion through spinal shock. As circuits recover without normal descending control, hyperreflexia, clonus, spasticity, and extensor plantar responses can emerge.

    • Autonomic and respiratory relationships

    • Cervical cord segments contribute to diaphragm drive through phrenic motor neurons, while thoracic segments support intercostal and abdominal muscles. High injury can therefore impair ventilation and cough even when lungs are structurally normal. Sympathetic outflow arises from thoracolumbar cord; injury above T6 can separate spinal sympathetic circuits from brain control and permit autonomic dysreflexia after the acute phase. Sacral pathways coordinate bladder, bowel, and sexual function. Neurogenic shock is acute loss of sympathetic tone with vasodilation and often bradycardia, whereas spinal shock is transient loss of reflex and motor activity below injury; they are not synonyms.

    • Failure patterns and disease links

    • Compression from trauma, epidural abscess or hematoma, tumor, disc disease, or severe degenerative narrowing can injure cord. Infarction, inflammation, demyelination, vitamin B12 deficiency, infection, syrinx, and hereditary disease produce other tract patterns. Cauda equina injury affects peripheral roots rather than cord and can produce asymmetric lower motor-neuron weakness, saddle sensory loss, severe radicular pain, and bladder dysfunction. Conus injury can be more symmetric and early in sphincter involvement. Complete versus incomplete injury depends on sacral sensory or motor preservation, not whether the patient can move one limb.

    • Clinical connections, labs, and diagnostics

    • Examination maps strength by myotome, sensation by dermatome and modality, reflexes, rectal tone when clinically indicated, sacral sparing, gait, coordination, respiratory function, and bladder status. MRI is central for cord, disc, ligament, abscess, hematoma, tumor, and inflammatory lesions; CT defines bone and is rapid in trauma. CBC, inflammatory markers, cultures, coagulation, B12, copper, infectious, autoimmune, or cerebrospinal studies are selected by cause. Anticoagulants raise epidural-hematoma concern, sedatives can hide progression, corticosteroid decisions are disease-specific, and antibiotics plus urgent source control are time-sensitive in epidural infection.

    • Nursing assessment and actions

    • In trauma, maintain spinal motion restriction according to protocol while securing airway and ventilation. Establish and repeat motor strength, sensation by side and level, reflexes when appropriate, pain, rectal or sacral findings when ordered, respiratory rate and depth, cough, vital capacity, blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, skin, bladder volume and output, and bowel function. Logroll with coordinated staff, protect pressure points, prevent aspiration and venous thrombosis, manage temperature, and avoid unplanned neck or trunk movement. Report any ascending sensory level, new weakness, or sphincter change immediately because neurologic loss can become irreversible.

    • Urgent red flags

    • New urinary retention or incontinence with saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness, or severe radicular pain requires emergency cauda equina evaluation. Back pain with fever, immunosuppression, injection-drug use, bacteremia, or neurologic deficit raises epidural abscess concern. Sudden severe back pain and weakness in an anticoagulated or recently instrumented patient can indicate epidural hematoma. High injury with shallow breathing, weak cough, hypotension, or bradycardia threatens ventilation and circulation. Severe hypertension, pounding headache, flushing or sweating above a chronic lesion, and bradycardia suggests autonomic dysreflexia.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Spinal shock is loss of reflex activity; neurogenic shock is hemodynamic sympathetic failure. A cord lesion can produce ipsilateral motor and vibration loss with contralateral pain-temperature loss because pathways cross at different levels. A normal limb pulse does not assess cord function. Do not delay imaging for a bladder scan or routine laboratory panel when compression is clinically suspected. Return of the bulbocavernosus reflex helps mark the end of spinal shock but does not prove useful motor recovery. Never place a patient with suspected unstable spine into unnecessary flexion for convenience.
