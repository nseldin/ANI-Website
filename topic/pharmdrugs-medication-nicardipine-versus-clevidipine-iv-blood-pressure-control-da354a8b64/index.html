Used To Treat

Choosing and safely administering a titratable IV arterial vasodilator when oral therapy is not feasible or desirable and the clinical team has defined a condition-specific blood-pressure target and rate of reduction.

Description

Nicardipine and clevidipine are both IV dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, but they solve different operational problems. Nicardipine is a water-based infusion with a slower offset, broad critical-care familiarity, and a labeled 5 to 15 mg/hour titration range; it can irritate peripheral veins and requires a large vein or central access with peripheral-site changes every 12 hours. Because nicardipine has more pharmacologic carryover, titrating faster than the response can declare itself can overshoot the pressure target; therefore dose changes must respect the ordered interval and continuous response. Clevidipine is an ultra-short-acting lipid emulsion hydrolyzed by blood and tissue esterases, so pressure can change within minutes of titration and falls rapidly after stopping. Its lipid vehicle creates soy/egg, lipid-metabolism, calorie, triglyceride, sterility, line, and daily-volume constraints. Because clevidipine disappears quickly, an interrupted infusion can permit rapid pressure rebound, which explains why transition therapy and uninterrupted pump/line planning matter. The best choice depends on target precision, access, allergy/lipid status, cardiac function, transition plan, and institutional protocol rather than one being universally stronger.

Mechanism

Both inhibit Cav1.2 channels in arterial smooth muscle, lowering calcium-calmodulin activation of myosin light-chain kinase and rapidly reducing systemic vascular resistance. Nicardipine is hepatically metabolized and its action falls by roughly half about 30 minutes after discontinuation, so titration has more carryover. Clevidipine contains an ester bond rapidly hydrolyzed by esterases in blood and extravascular tissues; its initial half-life is about one minute and clearance is largely independent of renal or hepatic function. That pharmacokinetic difference explains why clevidipine allows finer minute-to-minute control but also why an oral or longer-acting bridge must be timed before the infusion disappears. Neither drug treats the cause of hypertension or protects against abrupt beta-blocker withdrawal.

Boxed Warning

No current U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: Both can cause excessive hypotension, reflex tachycardia, ischemia from over-rapid pressure reduction, and possible negative inotropy. Nicardipine is contraindicated in advanced aortic stenosis and can cause phlebitis or extravasation injury. Clevidipine is contraindicated with soy/egg allergy, defective lipid metabolism, and severe aortic stenosis; it contributes 0.2 g lipid and 2 kcal per mL, should not share a medication line, must be discarded 12 hours after puncture, and is limited by a recommended 1,000 mL per 24-hour lipid load.

Adverse Effects

  • Both: headache, hypotension, tachycardia, nausea/vomiting, flushing, and worsening heart failure in susceptible patients.

  • Nicardipine: infusion-site burning, phlebitis, thrombosis, swelling, or extravasation. Clevidipine: lipid/calorie burden, hypertriglyceridemia context, contamination risk, rebound hypertension without transition, and emulsion allergy.

Contraindications

  • Advanced/severe aortic stenosis and serious hypersensitivity; condition-specific contraindications to rapid pressure reduction also matter.

  • Clevidipine additionally: allergy to soybeans/soy products or eggs/egg products and defective lipid metabolism such as pathologic hyperlipemia, lipoid nephrosis, or hyperlipidemic acute pancreatitis.

Nursing Essentials

  • Define the target and permitted rate of pressure reduction before titrating. Use continuous pressure and heart-rate monitoring, reassess neurologic and renal perfusion, and avoid chasing one transient reading with repeated rapid escalations.

  • For nicardipine, use a central line or large peripheral vein and change a peripheral site every 12 hours. For clevidipine, invert gently, use a calibrated pump and dedicated compatible line, maintain strict asepsis, count lipid calories/volume, and discard at 12 hours.

Interactions

  • Nicardipine may increase cyclosporine or tacrolimus concentrations; monitor troughs. Additive hypotension and possible myocardial depression occur with other antihypertensives or beta blockers.

  • Clevidipine has low CYP interaction potential because esterases clear it, but other IV lipid sources add to the total lipid/calorie load and all antihypertensives add pressure-lowering effect.

Key Labs

  • Continuous BP/HR, perfusion, urine output, neurologic status, IV site, renal/hepatic function, and tacrolimus/cyclosporine troughs when nicardipine is coadministered.

  • For prolonged/high-volume clevidipine, review triglycerides, pancreatitis/lipid-metabolism context, total parenteral/enteral lipids and calories, and the cumulative 24-hour infusion volume.

Nclex Traps

  • Cardene and Cleviprex are not the same bag. Nicardipine's line issue is venous irritation; clevidipine's distinctive issue is a soy/egg-containing lipid emulsion with a 12-hour puncture limit.

  • An ultra-short offset improves titration but creates rebound risk if the next antihypertensive has not begun working.
