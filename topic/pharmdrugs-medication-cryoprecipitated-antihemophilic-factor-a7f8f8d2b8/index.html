Used To Treat

Replacement of fibrinogen and selected cryoprecipitate proteins during clinically important acquired hypofibrinogenemia or dysfibrinogenemia, including protocol-directed rescue of major post-thrombolytic hemorrhage. Product content varies by unit and pool; cryoprecipitate is not a universal anticoagulant reversal agent.

Description

Cryoprecipitated antihemophilic factor provides concentrated fibrinogen, factor VIII, factor XIII, von Willebrand factor, and fibronectin in relatively low volume and restores fibrin substrate and clot strength when low or dysfunctional fibrinogen contributes to bleeding. It is the cold-insoluble fraction prepared from plasma, and its most common modern rescue role is fibrinogen replacement. It begins working only after compatible units are thawed, pooled when required, issued, and transfused, so early blood-bank activation and repeat fibrinogen testing are part of the treatment.

Mechanism

Thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptides from fibrinogen, creating fibrin monomers that polymerize; factor XIII then cross-links the network. When fibrinogen is depleted by fibrinolysis, consumption, dilution, liver failure, or massive bleeding, platelets and coagulation enzymes cannot build a durable scaffold even if their numbers appear adequate. Cryoprecipitate supplies concentrated fibrinogen and factor XIII, thereby restoring substrate for clot formation and stabilization. It does not inhibit plasmin, neutralize heparin or a direct oral anticoagulant, or repair a bleeding vessel.

Administration Timing

  • Activate transfusion medicine early, send fibrinogen and type-and-cross testing, and use the institutional component-dose calculation because fibrinogen content and pool size are not identical everywhere.

  • Verify patient, component, pool, expiration after thawing, compatibility policy, indication, and ordered rate at the bedside; transfuse promptly after issue through the approved filter.

  • Recheck fibrinogen and clinical hemostasis after the protocol-defined interval and repeat only for persistent deficit or bleeding, not solely to normalize a laboratory number.

Nursing Essentials

  • Notify the blood bank as soon as major hypofibrinogenemic bleeding is suspected because thawing and pooling take time while hemorrhage continues.

  • Perform full bedside transfusion verification and monitor temperature, blood pressure, pulse, oxygenation, skin, lungs, and symptoms because even a small-volume plasma component can cause severe reaction.

  • Trend bleeding source, wound or drain output, neurologic status, and perfusion because a higher fibrinogen cannot prove that the vessel is controlled.

  • Document pool size and post-transfusion specimen timing because interpreting an inadequate rise requires knowing how much fibrinogen was delivered and whether bleeding or dilution continued.

Key Labs

  • Serial Clauss fibrinogen when available, CBC/hemoglobin/platelets, PT/INR, aPTT, type and cross, ionized calcium, temperature, blood gas, lactate, and viscoelastic testing when used.

  • Track component totals, estimated blood loss, drain output, hemodynamics, urine output, and repeat imaging or procedural findings because the expected fibrinogen increment changes with body size, pool content, dilution, and ongoing consumption.

Adverse Effects

  • Febrile or allergic transfusion reactions, anaphylaxis, transfusion-associated circulatory overload, transfusion-related acute lung injury, bacterial contamination, and residual infectious transmission risk can occur.

  • Excess or unnecessary fibrinogen replacement may contribute to thrombosis, while an inadequate dose or ongoing consumption can leave bleeding uncontrolled.

Contraindications

  • Do not use cryoprecipitate when fibrinogen is adequate and the bleeding mechanism is unrelated because adding fibrinogen cannot correct a different hemostatic defect; do not substitute it for a specific factor concentrate when an appropriate licensed concentrate is available because targeted replacement reduces unnecessary donor-plasma exposure.

  • A prior severe plasma-protein reaction or IgA-associated anaphylaxis risk requires transfusion-medicine planning; in exsanguinating hemorrhage, risk is managed rather than simplistically treated as an absolute prohibition.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Fever, rigors, dyspnea, wheeze, hypotension, chest or back pain, hemoglobinuria, or new hypoxemia during transfusion requires immediate stop, line maintenance with compatible fluid, blood-bank notification, and reaction workup.

  • Persistent bleeding or a poor fibrinogen rise requires reassessment for ongoing fibrinolysis, dilution, DIC, liver failure, wrong component delivery, or uncontrolled anatomy rather than blind repeated pools.

Interactions

  • Concurrent alteplase, tenecteplase, anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, hypothermia, acidosis, and thrombocytopenia can continue to impair hemostasis after fibrinogen replacement; each mechanism needs separate correction.

  • Tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid can preserve newly formed fibrin in selected hyperfibrinolytic states, but combination therapy increases thrombosis concern and must follow the hemorrhage protocol.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Infants and small children

    Note

    Pool volume and fibrinogen dose have a larger weight-adjusted effect, and ABO selection may be more important; use pediatric transfusion calculations and close volume monitoring.

  • Population

    Patients with heart or kidney failure

    Note

    Monitor lungs, blood pressure, and fluid balance because repeated cryoprecipitate pools and accompanying resuscitation can still produce circulatory overload despite the component's relatively low volume.

  • Population

    Patients with prior severe transfusion reactions or IgA deficiency

    Note

    Plasma proteins can trigger recurrent anaphylaxis, so involve transfusion medicine for product selection and emergency-preparedness planning.

Evidence Limitations

  • Fibrinogen content varies among units and institutions, so a fixed pool is not a universal dose. The AABB Circular and local blood-bank specification control handling.

  • Evidence for post-thrombolytic hemorrhage rescue is limited and protocol based; clinical hemostasis and repeated fibrinogen matter more than a single traditional target copied across settings.

Nclex Traps

  • Cryoprecipitate is concentrated fibrinogen replacement, not frozen plasma in a smaller bag.

  • It does not neutralize heparin, warfarin, or direct oral anticoagulants.

  • A transfusion reaction requires immediate stop and evaluation even during life-threatening bleeding.

Regulatory Status

FDA-regulated human blood component prepared and used under blood-bank standards. Specific use for post-thrombolytic hemorrhage is protocol and consensus directed rather than a direct-antidote label indication.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): cryoprecipitate can cause anaphylaxis, TRALI, circulatory overload, contamination, or thrombosis. Verify the component and monitor continuously because early airway, pulmonary, and hemodynamic changes may be the first evidence of a severe transfusion reaction; stop immediately because continuing the implicated component can intensify injury.

Boxed Warning Specificity

Specific educational safety warning; verify whether the current product carries an FDA boxed warning.
