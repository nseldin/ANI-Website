Definition

Hepatitis B virus, abbreviated HBV, is an enveloped DNA virus that infects hepatocytes and can cause acute hepatitis, chronic infection, cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. The virus and hepatitis B disease are not the same identity: HBV is the agent, while the clinical outcome depends strongly on age at acquisition, immune response, viral activity, and duration of infection.

    Id

    definition

    Title

    Definition

  • Content

    HBV belongs to the hepadnavirus group and uses reverse transcription despite having a DNA genome. Serologic markers such as surface antigen, surface antibody, and core antibody are viral components or immune responses, not separate organisms. Their combinations answer different questions about current infection, past infection, vaccination, and susceptibility, so one isolated marker can be misleading.

    Id

    identity-classification

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    HBV is an enveloped virus with a small, partially double-stranded circular DNA genome and an icosahedral nucleocapsid. Viral DNA is repaired in the hepatocyte nucleus to form stable covalently closed circular DNA, while an RNA intermediate is reverse transcribed during replication. This persistent nuclear template helps explain why antiviral suppression may be long term and why loss of circulating DNA does not necessarily mean the infection has been eradicated.

    Id

    structure-traits

    Title

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Humans with acute or chronic infection are the reservoir. Infectious blood and body fluids transmit HBV through percutaneous, mucosal, sexual, and perinatal exposure. The virus is not spread by casual contact. Perinatal and early-childhood acquisition is especially important because immature immune responses are much more likely to permit chronic infection than infection acquired in immunocompetent adulthood.

    Id

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    HBV enters hepatocytes and replicates, but much of the liver injury results from the host immune response against infected cells rather than direct viral destruction. A strong coordinated response can clear acute infection, whereas inadequate clearance allows persistence. Repeated inflammation and repair drive fibrosis and cirrhosis; ongoing replication and viral-host genomic effects increase hepatocellular-carcinoma risk even before end-stage liver failure develops.

    Id

    pathogenesis

    Title

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Acute infection may be asymptomatic or cause fatigue, nausea, right-upper-quadrant discomfort, dark urine, pale stools, and jaundice. Chronic infection is often silent until fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, or cancer emerges. Altered mental status, bleeding, severe jaundice, hypoglycemia, or rising coagulation time can signal acute liver failure and requires urgent escalation. Pregnancy screening matters because timely neonatal prophylaxis can prevent chronic infection.

    Id

    clinical-significance

    Title

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    The initial adult screening triple panel includes hepatitis B surface antigen, antibody to surface antigen, and total antibody to core antigen. The pattern distinguishes current infection, resolved infection, vaccine immunity, and susceptibility; IgM core antibody helps identify recent acute infection. HBV DNA quantifies replication and guides monitoring or treatment decisions. Liver enzymes reflect injury, not viral identity, and normal values do not exclude chronic infection.

    Id

    diagnostic-identification

    Title

    Diagnostic identification

  • Content

    Vaccination is the primary prevention strategy. Additional measures include screening donated blood, safe injection and sharps practices, avoiding shared injection equipment, barrier protection based on exposure risk, and prompt postexposure management. Standard Precautions apply in healthcare. Chronic treatment is individualized by viral activity, liver disease, pregnancy, comorbidity, and medication resistance or safety; stopping suppressive therapy without guidance can precipitate a serious flare.
