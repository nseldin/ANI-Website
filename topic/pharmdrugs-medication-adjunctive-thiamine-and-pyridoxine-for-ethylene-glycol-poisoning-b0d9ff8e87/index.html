Used To Treat

Adjunctive cofactor support sometimes used during ethylene-glycol poisoning in an attempt to divert glyoxylic acid toward less-toxic metabolites. Human outcome benefit is unproven; thiamine and pyridoxine never replace alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade with fomepizole or ethanol, correction of physiologic derangements, or EXTRIP-directed extracorporeal treatment.

Description

Thiamine and pyridoxine are biologically plausible but secondary adjuncts in ethylene-glycol poisoning. Ethylene glycol itself causes intoxication, but acid metabolites drive high-anion-gap acidosis, kidney injury, and calcium-oxalate deposition. Vitamins may support minor metabolic routes that convert glyoxylic acid to less-toxic products. Because clinical benefit has not been demonstrated reliably in humans, the safe educational message is hierarchy: stabilize first, block alcohol dehydrogenase promptly, assess dialysis indications, and add cofactors only through a toxicology protocol.

Mechanism

Alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase convert ethylene glycol to glycolate and then glyoxylate. Glycolate drives much of the acidosis, while glyoxylate can become oxalate that binds calcium and injures kidneys. Thiamine supports conversion of glyoxylate toward alpha-hydroxy-beta-ketoadipate, and pyridoxal phosphate derived from pyridoxine supports transamination toward glycine. These minor shunts could reduce oxalate formation, but their capacity in poisoned humans is uncertain; blocking the upstream enzyme and removing toxin and metabolites therefore remain mechanistically dominant.

Administration Timing

  • Immediately stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature, ECG, electrolytes, calcium, and acid-base status, and call Poison Help; do not wait for a measured ethylene-glycol concentration when the clinical suspicion and surrogate data justify blockade.

  • Start protocol-directed fomepizole or ethanol and assess EXTRIP criteria in parallel. Cofactors may be added after decisive therapy is underway, not used as a diagnostic trial.

  • Continue serial anion/osmol gaps, blood gas, renal function, calcium, urine output, and neurologic assessment; stop or continue vitamins only through the toxicology plan rather than assuming more is better.

Nursing Essentials

  • Label thiamine and pyridoxine clearly as adjuncts in handoff because calling them 'the antidote' can delay fomepizole or dialysis.

  • Track the alcohol-dehydrogenase blocker schedule carefully during hemodialysis because extracorporeal clearance changes fomepizole or ethanol requirements while the vitamin schedule is secondary.

  • Monitor for hypocalcemic symptoms, worsening acidosis, oliguria, pulmonary edema, and neurologic decline because these indicate toxic-metabolite injury that vitamins cannot reverse.

  • Verify IV formulation, dose, and rate because parenteral thiamine can rarely cause anaphylaxis and excessive repeated pyridoxine can cause neurologic toxicity.

Key Labs

  • Serial blood gas, bicarbonate, anion gap, measured osmolality/osmol gap, lactate interpreted for analyzer interference, ethylene-glycol concentration when available, glucose, electrolytes, ionized calcium, magnesium, creatinine, and urinalysis.

  • Continuous urine output, ECG, neurologic and respiratory monitoring; thiamine or pyridoxal levels are not useful gatekeepers for acute toxic-alcohol treatment.

Adverse Effects

  • Parenteral thiamine can cause infusion reactions or rare anaphylaxis; pyridoxine can cause nausea, headache, and with excessive or prolonged exposure sensory neuropathy and ataxia.

  • The principal pathway harm is therapeutic delay: severe acidosis, hypocalcemia, renal failure, pulmonary edema, coma, or death can progress while an ineffective vitamin-only plan is attempted.

Contraindications

  • Known serious hypersensitivity to a vitamin formulation requires alternative planning, but it does not change the need for fomepizole or ethanol and indicated extracorporeal treatment.

  • Do not use disappearance of an osmol gap, absence of oxalate crystals, or availability of vitamins to exclude poisoning because late toxic-metabolite disease can have a normal osmol gap after the parent alcohol has already been consumed.

Escalation Recurrence

  • Severe or worsening acidosis, coma, seizure, kidney failure, marked glycolate burden, major electrolyte disturbance, or high ethylene-glycol exposure requires urgent EXTRIP-guided dialysis assessment.

  • Rebound concentration or acidosis after dialysis suggests redistribution, incomplete blockade, or ongoing absorption and requires repeat toxicology-nephrology assessment.

Interactions

  • Fomepizole or ethanol blocks the upstream alcohol-dehydrogenase step and is the decisive partner; cofactors must never displace or delay that therapy, and dialysis changes blocker dosing.

  • Pyridoxine can reduce the effect of levodopa when levodopa is given without carbidopa, while large chronic doses add neuropathy risk; reconcile medicines even though the emergency toxicology hierarchy remains primary.

Population Risks

  • Population

    Patients with alcohol-use disorder or malnutrition

    Note

    Thiamine deficiency may coexist and independently threaten neurologic energy metabolism, but treating deficiency still does not validate vitamin-only management of ethylene glycol.

  • Population

    Patients with kidney failure or severe acidosis

    Note

    Metabolite clearance and buffering are failing, so vitamins are least able to compensate; urgent extracorporeal-treatment assessment has priority.

  • Population

    Pregnancy, lactation, and pediatric care

    Note

    Weight, developmental physiology, and fetal concerns affect dosing and dialysis decisions, but untreated toxic-alcohol injury is life threatening; involve toxicology and nephrology immediately.

Evidence Limitations

  • ATSDR notes that human benefit from thiamine and pyridoxine in ethylene-glycol poisoning is unknown. Recommendations are based on biochemical plausibility and expert practice rather than outcome trials.

  • No cofactor response can confirm the diagnosis or define readiness for discharge; toxic alcohol and metabolite trends plus clinical recovery control decisions.

Nclex Traps

  • Fomepizole or ethanol blocks toxic metabolism; vitamins only support theoretical minor shunts.

  • A normal osmol gap late in illness does not exclude ethylene-glycol poisoning because parent alcohol may already be metabolized.

  • Do not delay hemodialysis for a vitamin trial in severe poisoning.

Regulatory Status

OFF-LABEL adjunctive toxicology use with unproven human outcome benefit. Alcohol-dehydrogenase blockade and EXTRIP-directed extracorporeal treatment remain the evidence-based priorities.

Boxed Warning

High-yield warning context (not necessarily an FDA boxed warning): thiamine and pyridoxine are not stand-alone ethylene-glycol antidotes. Never delay fomepizole or ethanol, correction of life-threatening physiology, or indicated hemodialysis while giving cofactors.

Boxed Warning Specificity
