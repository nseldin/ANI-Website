Definition

Anisocoria means the two pupils are different sizes. The difference may be a harmless normal variant or may result from a problem affecting the iris, eye medicines, trauma, or the nerves that constrict and dilate the pupil.

Plain Language

Anisocoria means one pupil is larger than the other.

Why It Matters

A long-standing stable difference may be harmless, but sudden anisocoria can signal third-nerve compression, acute Horner syndrome, brain herniation, or eye trauma. Lighting, pupil reactions, eyelid position, eye movements, pain, consciousness, and neurologic findings determine urgency.

Associated Conditions

  • physiologic variation, Horner syndrome, third-nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, iris trauma or disease, unilateral medication or chemical exposure, prior eye surgery, or intracranial compression

Assessment Method

Measure both pupils while the patient looks at a distant target in bright and dim light, document size, shape and direct and consensual light responses, determine whether asymmetry increases in light or darkness, review prior photographs and eye drops or patches, and assess visual acuity, eyelids, extraocular movements, near response, headache, eye or neck pain, trauma, and the complete neurologic examination.

Positive Finding

The pupils differ in diameter; greater asymmetry in bright light suggests the larger pupil is failing to constrict, while greater asymmetry in darkness suggests the smaller pupil is failing to dilate.

Mechanism

Unequal parasympathetic constrictor or sympathetic dilator activity, structural iris injury, local pharmacologic exposure, or normal asymmetric autonomic tone produces unequal pupil size.

Clinical Interpretation

The observed Anisocoria finding raises concern for physiologic variation, Horner syndrome, third-nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, iris trauma or disease, unilateral medication or chemical exposure, prior eye surgery, or intracranial compression because unequal parasympathetic constrictor or sympathetic dilator activity, structural iris injury, local pharmacologic exposure, or normal asymmetric autonomic tone produces unequal pupil size. Compare the response with the reviewed assessment and differentials; Anisocoria does not establish the diagnosis alone.

Diagnostic Usefulness

Use Anisocoria to focus evaluation of physiologic variation, Horner syndrome, third-nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, iris trauma or disease, unilateral medication or chemical exposure, prior eye surgery, or intracranial compression and the reviewed alternatives: physiologic anisocoria, third-nerve palsy, Horner syndrome, Adie tonic pupil, pharmacologic pupil, traumatic iris injury. Follow this card's assessment method and limits: Measure both pupils while the patient looks at a distant target in bright and dim light, document size, shape and direct and consensual light responses, determine whether asymmetry increases in light or darkness, review prior photographs and eye drops or patches, and assess visual acuity, eyelids, extraocular movements, near response, headache, eye or neck pain, trauma, and the complete neurologic examination. Then anticipate or support cause-directed testing selected by the responsible clinician for the suspected condition rather than treating Anisocoria as a diagnosis.

Evidence Limitations

Pupil size changes with lighting, accommodation, age, medications, and measurement technique; a small stable difference can be physiologic, baseline photographs may be unavailable, and anisocoria is not the same as a relative afferent pupillary defect. Therefore, Anisocoria cannot confirm or exclude physiologic variation, Horner syndrome, third-nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, iris trauma or disease, unilateral medication or chemical exposure, prior eye surgery, or intracranial compression by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

  • physiologic anisocoria

False Positive Situations

A false-positive observation can result from lighting, baseline anatomy, transient physiology, or a mimic such as physiologic anisocoria, third-nerve palsy, Horner syndrome, Adie tonic pupil, pharmacologic pupil, traumatic iris injury.

False Negative Situations

Anisocoria may be missed when it is intermittent, subtle, concealed by skin or body habitus, or absent at the observed stage of physiologic variation, Horner syndrome, third-nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, iris trauma or disease, unilateral medication or chemical exposure, prior eye surgery, or intracranial compression.

Modern Status

Current descriptive sign that is often physiologic but can reveal a compressive third-nerve palsy, acute Horner syndrome, intracranial herniation, eye trauma, or another emergency. Safety Precautions

Observe Anisocoria with consent, privacy, adequate exposure, and only the manipulation required by this assessment: Measure both pupils while the patient looks at a distant target in bright and dim light, document size, shape and direct and consensual light responses, determine whether asymmetry increases in light or darkness, review prior photographs and eye drops or patches, and assess visual acuity, eyelids, extraocular movements, near response, headache, eye or neck pain, trauma, and the complete neurologic examination. The reviewed precaution for this finding is: Do not label sudden anisocoria benign from size alone; new asymmetry with severe headache, eye or neck pain, ptosis, diplopia, abnormal eye movement, vision loss, focal neurologic deficit, trauma, reduced consciousness, or rapid deterioration requires emergency imaging and specialist assessment.
