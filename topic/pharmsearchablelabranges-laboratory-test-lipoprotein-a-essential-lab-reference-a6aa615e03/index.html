Range

Interpret Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), only in the unit the laboratory reports. The 2024 National Lipid Association statement describes <75 nmol/L or <30 mg/dL as lower risk, 75 to <125 nmol/L or 30 to <50 mg/dL as intermediate risk, and >=125 nmol/L or >=50 mg/dL as high risk. Risk rises continuously rather than switching on at one diagnostic cutoff; assays differ, and a fixed factor must not be used to convert between mg/dL and nmol/L. Current U.S. guidance supports measuring Lp(a) at least once in adulthood, with repeat testing selected for the clinical context rather than assumed for everyone.

Why

An elevated Lp(a) result is associated with higher inherited risk from an apolipoprotein(a)-bearing LDL-like particle for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and calcific aortic-valve stenosis, even when LDL-C appears acceptable. Assess personal and first-degree-family history of premature coronary disease, stroke, or peripheral-artery disease, known familial hypercholesterolemia, aortic-stenosis symptoms or history, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, kidney disease, pregnancy context, and current lipid therapy. Cause-directed evaluation includes the complete lipid profile, LDL-C and non-HDL-C, selective ApoB, overall ASCVD-risk assessment, and clinician-directed consideration of family testing and more intensive control of LDL-C and other modifiable risks. An Lp(a) result has no stand-alone emergency threshold; acute chest pain, a new focal neurologic deficit, acute limb ischemia, or syncope or dyspnea with suspected severe aortic stenosis is urgent because of the symptoms, not the marker number. One high result does not diagnose coronary disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral-artery disease, or aortic stenosis and does not by itself select aspirin or a specific lipid-lowering drug.

High Causes

  • Inherited LPA-related elevation, which accounts for most adult variation

  • A family pattern of elevated Lp(a) or premature ASCVD

  • Residual inherited ASCVD risk despite an otherwise reassuring standard lipid profile

  • Kidney disease, pregnancy, menopause, inflammation, or another clinical state may alter a value and the need for repeat testing

Low Causes

  • A low Lp(a) result usually indicates less Lp(a)-related inherited risk, but it does not remove risk from LDL-C, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, or other factors.

Critical Concerns

  • There is no Lp(a)-only emergency cutoff. Escalate acute ischemic or severe valve-symptom patterns according to the clinical presentation, not because of the marker alone.

  • A high result is a risk-enhancing factor, not a diagnosis of current arterial blockage or aortic stenosis.

  • Do not convert mg/dL to nmol/L with one fixed multiplier or compare results without checking the assay, unit, and clinical context.

Nursing Considerations

  • Verify the reported unit and assay context, prior result, current illness or pregnancy context, and the laboratory's preparation instructions; fasting is not automatically required for Lp(a), although a concurrently ordered lipid panel may have its own instructions.

  • Assess personal and family premature ASCVD, familial hypercholesterolemia, aortic-stenosis history or symptoms, LDL-C/non-HDL-C, selective ApoB, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, kidney disease, and the clinician-directed lipid plan.

  • Teach that lifestyle may change inherited Lp(a) little but still lowers overall cardiovascular risk; reinforce LDL-C management, blood-pressure and diabetes control, smoking cessation, activity, and follow-up rather than promising that one intervention normalizes Lp(a).

Nclex Traps

  • Lp(a) is not LDL-C and is not included in a routine lipid panel; a normal LDL-C result can coexist with elevated inherited Lp(a)-related risk.

  • At-least-once adult measurement does not mean every patient needs serial testing, and a high result does not independently authorize aspirin or a specific medicine.
