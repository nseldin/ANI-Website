Definition

Coxiella burnetii is a clinically important bacterium associated with acute Q fever, atypical pneumonia, hepatitis, and persistent focalized infection including endocarditis. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Coxiella burnetii is a Gram-negative coccobacillus with a obligate aerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, nonmotile, and obligate intracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Structure and traits

  • Content

    Control exposure to livestock birth products, use occupational protective measures, and apply animal-health and laboratory biosafety guidance. The reviewed route description is the reviewed zoonotic transmission and environmental exposure transmission route categories. The reviewed incubation estimate is 14 to 39 days; individual timing can still vary with inoculum, route, host factors, and the syndrome being measured. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    ecology-transmission

    Title

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Inhaled organisms survive in acidic phagolysosome-like vacuoles; heavy contamination of animal birth products permits low-dose aerosol transmission. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Coxiella burnetii is associated with acute Q fever, atypical pneumonia, hepatitis, and persistent focalized infection including endocarditis. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.

    Clinical significance

  • Content

    Phase-specific serology and PCR are interpreted by illness stage; persistent infection assessment requires compatible organ involvement and high phase I responses. A positive result can represent infection, colonization, past exposure, residual material, or contamination depending on the method and source; a negative result can reflect timing, specimen quality, prior therapy, or an untested target.
