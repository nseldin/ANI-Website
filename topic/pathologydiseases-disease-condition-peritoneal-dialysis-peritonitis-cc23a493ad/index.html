Definition

Peritoneal dialysis peritonitis is infection or inflammation of the peritoneal cavity in a patient receiving PD, typically recognized by cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, and inflammatory cells in drained dialysate. It threatens sepsis, membrane function, catheter survival, and the ability to continue PD, so cloudy effluent requires immediate evaluation.

Pathology

Organisms or chemical irritation activate mesothelial and peritoneal immune responses. Neutrophils and protein enter dialysate, making it cloudy; capillary permeability and membrane transport change, impairing ultrafiltration and causing pain.

Pathophysiology

Bacterial entry during an exchange can multiply during the dwell, producing local cytokine release and neutrophil recruitment. Inflammation increases small-solute transport and glucose absorption, so the osmotic gradient dissipates faster and net ultrafiltration can fall. Infection can spread into blood or persist in catheter biofilm. Organism, clinical response, and recurrence pattern determine whether antimicrobial therapy alone is sufficient or catheter removal is needed for source control.

Etiology

Touch contamination and connection breaks commonly introduce skin organisms; exit-site or tunnel infection can track inward; enteric disease, bacteremia, gynecologic sources, and fungal exposure create other patterns. Repeated, relapsing, refractory, polymicrobial, or fungal episodes suggest source-control problems.

Risk Factors

  • Connection or hand-hygiene break, contaminated equipment, or poor training support

  • Exit-site or tunnel infection

  • Prior peritonitis, catheter biofilm, or recent antimicrobial exposure

  • Constipation, diverticular or other enteric disease, invasive abdominal event, or immunosuppression

Signs Symptoms

  • Cloudy effluent

  • Diffuse abdominal pain or tenderness

  • Fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, poor drainage, or malaise

  • Hypotension, confusion, or reduced ultrafiltration with severe infection

Diagnostics

  • Use the ISPD diagnostic framework: at least two of three should be present—compatible clinical features such as abdominal pain and/or cloudy effluent; effluent white-cell count greater than 100 cells/µL after a dwell of at least 2 hours with more than 50% polymorphonuclear cells; or a positive effluent culture

  • Send drained effluent promptly for cell count with differential, Gram stain, and culture before avoidable antimicrobial delay

  • Inspect exit site and tunnel and obtain blood cultures when systemically ill

  • Review dwell duration and collection technique when interpreting cell count

  • Investigate enteric, fungal, refractory, relapsing, or polymicrobial patterns for source control

Labs

  • Effluent WBC greater than 100 cells/µL after at least a 2-hour dwell with more than 50% polymorphonuclear cells supports peritonitis as one criterion; short dwells may require interpreting the neutrophil percentage rather than the absolute count alone

  • Culture identifies the organism and guides therapy but can be negative

  • CBC, lactate, blood cultures, electrolytes, and glucose assess systemic illness

  • Ultrafiltration can decline during inflammation

Treatments

  • Begin prompt empiric therapy covering likely gram-positive and gram-negative organisms, then narrow to culture and current ISPD/local guidance

  • Use the route and regimen selected for PD peritonitis, severity, organism, residual function, and local protocol

  • Treat exit-site, tunnel, enteric, or other source

  • Remove the catheter when organism and refractory, relapsing, or fungal criteria make source control necessary

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess abdominal pain, cloudy effluent, fever, nausea, bowel symptoms, catheter exit site, recent contamination, procedures, and exchange technique because peritonitis may threaten the peritoneal membrane even before systemic signs appear.

  • Send properly collected effluent for cell count, differential, Gram stain, and culture before antibiotics when feasible because organism identification guides therapy, while cloudy effluent should be treated as presumed peritonitis until excluded.

  • Trend effluent clarity and leukocytes, pain, temperature, hemodynamics, intake, output, ultrafiltration, potassium, and glucose because treatment failure can cause sepsis, catheter loss, and inadequate dialysis.

  • Administer protocol-based intraperitoneal or systemic antimicrobials on schedule using meticulous aseptic exchanges because early adequate exposure treats infection while preventing additional touch contamination.

  • Escalate immediately for hypotension, rigid abdomen, severe worsening pain, altered consciousness, persistent cloudy effluent, or poor response within the protocol interval because sepsis, surgical abdomen, resistant infection, or catheter removal may require urgent action.

Complications

  • Sepsis, shock, abscess, and death

  • Catheter loss and temporary or permanent transfer to HD

  • Peritoneal fibrosis, adhesions, ultrafiltration failure, and technique failure

  • Relapsing or recurrent infection and antimicrobial resistance

Contraindications

  • Do not wait for fever when effluent is cloudy

  • Do not instill unprescribed agents or continue routine exchanges without urgent PD-team guidance

  • Do not retain an infected catheter when source-control criteria require removal

Red Flags

  • Cloudy effluent with abdominal pain, fever, or positive culture

  • Hypotension, rising lactate, confusion, or other septic-shock findings

  • Rigid abdomen, rebound tenderness, or severe focal abdominal pain

  • Persistent effluent leukocytosis or clinical worsening despite appropriate therapy

Patient Education

  • Teach patients to save cloudy effluent and contact the dialysis team immediately because treatment should begin promptly after correct specimen collection.

  • Explain hand hygiene, mask use, connection technique, and contamination reporting because hiding a touch break delays preventive treatment and increases peritonitis risk.

Nclex Traps

  • Cloudy effluent is the signature warning

  • Absence of fever does not exclude peritonitis

  • Collect effluent before avoidable antibiotic exposure

  • Catheter removal can be lifesaving source control, especially in fungal or refractory infection
