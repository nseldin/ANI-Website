Definition

Athetosis is a slow, continuous, nonrhythmic pattern of writhing movement that most often changes the posture of the fingers, hands, toes, or feet.

Plain Language

The hands, fingers, feet, or other body parts slowly twist and writhe without the person intending it.

Why It Matters

Athetosis can impair posture, walking, hand use, communication, feeding, and safety. It often overlaps with chorea or dystonia and can reflect cerebral palsy, brain injury, stroke, or another motor-system disorder.

Associated Conditions

Assessment Method

Observe at rest, during sustained posture, and during purposeful tasks, focusing on fingers, hands, toes, feet, face, and trunk; document speed, rhythm, distribution, tone, abnormal postures, task effect, developmental history, onset, medications, cognition, communication, swallowing, gait, and functional safety.

Positive Finding

Slow nonrhythmic sinuous writhing movements continuously change the position of the fingers, hands, feet, or other body parts.

Mechanism

Unstable motor-network output produces prolonged changing activation across opposing muscles, preventing a body part from holding a steady posture.

Clinical Interpretation

A positive Athetosis response raises concern for motor-network dysfunction producing slow continuously changing distal movement, often with cerebral palsy, perinatal brain injury, basal-ganglia disease, stroke, metabolic or genetic disease, or mixed chorea and dystonia because unstable motor-network output produces prolonged changing activation across opposing muscles, preventing a body part from holding a steady posture. Evidence Limitations

Athetosis can merge with chorea as choreoathetosis and can resemble slow dystonia; pain, voluntary movement, restlessness, and spastic posturing can complicate observation. Therefore, Athetosis cannot confirm or exclude motor-network dysfunction producing slow continuously changing distal movement, often with cerebral palsy, perinatal brain injury, basal-ganglia disease, stroke, metabolic or genetic disease, or mixed chorea and dystonia by itself; interpret the result in the population and clinical setting where it was assessed.

False Positive Situations

A false-positive maneuver can result from incorrect position or force, pain from chorea, dystonia, myoclonus, spasticity, guarding, or calling an incompletely defined response positive.

False Negative Situations

Athetosis may be missed with incomplete technique, guarding, early or partial disease, limited cooperation, or a response too subtle for the examiner.
