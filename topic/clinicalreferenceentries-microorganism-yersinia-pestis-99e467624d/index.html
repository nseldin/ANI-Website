Definition

Yersinia pestis is a clinically important bacterium associated with bubonic plague, septicemic plague, and pneumonic plague. The organism or agent name identifies the cause or association; it is not itself the disease diagnosis, and detection must be interpreted with the specimen, host, and syndrome.

    Title

    Identity and classification

  • Content

    Yersinia pestis is a Gram-negative coccobacillus with a facultative anaerobe oxygen profile. It is not acid-fast, not known to form spores, nonmotile, and facultative intracellular in its reviewed clinical behavior. These traits help narrow an early laboratory differential, but no trait pattern alone proves identity or susceptibility.

    Rodent and flea control, avoidance of sick wildlife, respiratory protection, Droplet Precautions for pneumonic plague, and contact management reduce transmission. The reviewed route description is the reviewed vector-borne transmission and zoonotic transmission and respiratory particle transmission route categories. Transmission risk therefore depends on the actual route, exposure, host, setting, and stage rather than the organism name alone.

    Ecology and transmission

  • Content

    Flea-associated inoculation or respiratory acquisition is followed by lymphatic or pulmonary growth, antiphagocytic type III secretion, and possible bloodstream dissemination. This mechanism explains the connection between exposure, host vulnerability, tissue effects, and the major syndromes instead of presenting the association as an isolated fact.

    Pathogenesis

  • Content

    Yersinia pestis is associated with bubonic plague, septicemic plague, and pneumonic plague. Severity and presentation vary by age, immune status, pregnancy, anatomic site, inoculum, and comorbidity. Urgent escalation is based on the syndrome—such as shock, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive neurologic findings, severe dehydration, or invasive infection—not merely on a positive organism label.
