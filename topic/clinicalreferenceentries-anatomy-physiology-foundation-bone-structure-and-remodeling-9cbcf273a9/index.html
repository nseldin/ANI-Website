Summary

Bone is living, vascular connective tissue built from collagen-rich organic matrix reinforced with calcium-phosphate mineral. Dense cortical bone provides strong outer support, trabecular bone distributes loads through an internal lattice, marrow occupies protected spaces, and coordinated osteoclast, osteoblast, and osteocyte activity continually repairs microdamage and adapts skeleton to mechanical and metabolic needs.

Quick Answer

Osteoclasts resorb old or damaged bone, osteoblasts lay down osteoid and support mineralization, and osteocytes sense mechanical strain from within the matrix. Remodeling preserves strength and helps regulate calcium and phosphate, but resorption that persistently exceeds formation produces net bone loss even when serum calcium remains normal.

Sections

    • Gross and microscopic structure

    • Long bones have a shaft or diaphysis, expanded ends or epiphyses, a metaphyseal region, articular cartilage, an outer periosteum, an inner endosteum, and a medullary cavity. Cortical bone forms a dense shell organized into osteons with concentric lamellae around vascular canals. Trabecular bone forms a lighter lattice aligned with stress and has a larger remodeling surface. Flat and irregular bones use different arrangements but the same tissue principles. Periosteum carries vessels, sensory nerves, and osteogenic cells; its pain sensitivity explains why fractures and periosteal injury can hurt intensely.

    • Matrix and bone cells

    • Osteoblasts produce osteoid, largely type I collagen plus matrix proteins, then support deposition of hydroxyapatite mineral. Some become osteocytes enclosed in lacunae and connected through canaliculi, where they sense strain, exchange signals, and coordinate remodeling. Osteoclasts are large multinucleated cells derived from the monocyte-macrophage lineage; they acidify and enzymatically dissolve mineralized matrix. Osteoprotegerin, RANK, and RANKL help regulate osteoclast formation. Collagen gives tensile resilience while mineral supplies compressive stiffness. Too little mineral causes soft bone; too little quantity or damaged architecture causes fragility by another mechanism.

    • Remodeling mechanism

    • A remodeling unit begins when osteocytes and local signals identify microdamage or altered load. Osteoclasts attach and resorb a small region; reversal cells prepare the surface; osteoblasts refill it with osteoid; mineralization then restores stiffness over time. Coupling resorption to formation prevents every repair from becoming net loss. Mechanical loading favors formation and alignment, while prolonged unloading accelerates resorption. Modeling changes bone size and shape during growth, whereas remodeling replaces existing tissue throughout life. Fracture healing adds hematoma and inflammation, soft callus, hard callus, and long remodeling rather than simply gluing two ends together.

    • Hormonal and nutritional regulation

    • Parathyroid hormone responds to low ionized calcium and supports renal calcium retention, phosphate excretion, vitamin D activation, and indirect osteoclast signaling when elevation is sustained. Active vitamin D increases intestinal calcium and phosphate availability and supports mineral balance. Calcitonin can inhibit osteoclast activity but has a smaller routine role in adult calcium regulation. Estrogen restrains excessive resorption; its decline accelerates turnover and trabecular loss. Thyroid excess, glucocorticoids, hypogonadism, malabsorption, kidney disease, low calcium or vitamin D, and poor protein intake alter remodeling. Serum calcium may remain normal because hormonal control sacrifices skeletal stores.

    • Failure consequences

    • Osteoporosis reflects reduced bone strength from low mass and deteriorated microarchitecture, often without abnormal serum calcium. Osteomalacia reflects defective mineralization of adult bone and can cause diffuse pain, weakness, and pseudofractures. Chronic kidney disease disrupts phosphate, vitamin D, PTH, and turnover, creating CKD-mineral and bone disorder. Paget disease produces disorganized high-turnover bone. Infection, tumor, ischemia, and prolonged corticosteroid exposure damage structure through different pathways. Fractures can bleed, injure nerves or vessels, trigger fat embolism, produce compartment syndrome, and cause immobility complications.

    • Clinical connections, labs, and diagnostics

    • DXA estimates areal bone mineral density and fracture risk but does not directly show every aspect of quality. Radiographs identify many fractures but can miss early stress, occult hip, or selected scaphoid injuries; CT and MRI answer different structural questions. Calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, PTH, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, creatinine, liver tests, CBC, thyroid or gonadal studies, and myeloma testing are selected by pattern. Bisphosphonates and denosumab reduce resorption through different targets; anabolic agents stimulate formation in selected high-risk patients. Glucocorticoids, aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation, some antiseizure drugs, and excess thyroid replacement can worsen risk.

    • Nursing assessment

    • Assess pain mechanism, trauma, deformity, swelling, bruising, crepitus without provoking it, limb length or rotation, ability to bear weight, and neurovascular status distal to injury. Review falls, height loss, kyphosis, prior fragility fracture, mobility, nutrition, menstrual or androgen history, steroid exposure, kidney disease, malabsorption, tobacco and alcohol use, and home hazards. After casting, fixation, or joint surgery, trend the six neurovascular concerns: pain, pallor, pulses, paresthesia, paralysis, and temperature, while remembering that severe compartment syndrome can occur before a pulse disappears.

    • Nursing actions and why

    • Immobilize suspected fractures in the position found unless an emergency protocol directs otherwise, support above and below the site, and avoid repeated manipulation because bone fragments can worsen bleeding and soft-tissue injury. Elevate and apply cold when appropriate while maintaining serial neurovascular checks. Promote safe weight-bearing and resistance exercise according to the plan because mechanical strain supports bone and muscle; add fall prevention because preventing impact can be as important as changing density. Administer calcium, vitamin D, antiresorptive, or anabolic therapy correctly, including administration precautions, dental considerations, and renal checks specific to the drug.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Open fracture, uncontrolled bleeding, absent or worsening distal perfusion, rapidly escalating pain despite medication, pain with passive stretch, tense swelling, new numbness or weakness, or a cool pale limb requires immediate escalation. Dyspnea, hypoxemia, confusion, fever, petechiae, or thrombocytopenia after a long-bone fracture can suggest fat embolism syndrome. New hip pain or inability to bear weight after a fall in an older adult may represent occult fracture even with a nondiagnostic first image. Severe back pain with weakness, saddle anesthesia, retention, fever, or cancer history threatens the spinal cord.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Bone is not inert storage; it is perfused, innervated, mechanically responsive tissue. Osteoporosis and osteomalacia are not synonyms. Normal serum calcium does not prove strong bones. Osteoclasts resorb bone but are necessary for repair; the problem is uncoupled or excessive activity, not their mere presence. A palpable pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome. Do not massage a suspected fracture, force a deformed limb straight, or place weight through it without clearance. Bone-density treatment does not replace fall prevention, nutrition, strength, vision, and medication review.
