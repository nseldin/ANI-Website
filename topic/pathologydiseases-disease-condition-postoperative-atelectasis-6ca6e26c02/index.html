Definition

Postoperative atelectasis develops when anesthesia, pain, diaphragmatic dysfunction, mucus retention, and shallow breathing collapse dependent alveoli after surgery. The characteristic pattern is early postoperative low lung volumes, bibasilar crackles or diminished sounds, mild hypoxemia, and plate-like opacities without a convincing infectious syndrome. Confirm or stage it with bedside respiratory and pain assessment with pulse oximetry; distinguish it from pneumonia, aspiration, pulmonary edema, mucus-plug lobar collapse, and pulmonary embolism by fever pattern, imaging, secretions, hemodynamics, and timing. The priority is to optimize analgesia without excessive sedation because pneumonia can complicate Postoperative atelectasis. can follow if the process advances.

Signs Symptoms

  • Timeline and trigger matter: exposure, ingestion, submersion, temperature stress, anesthesia, procedure, bite, or postoperative immobility plus vital-sign, neurologic, respiratory, skin, pain, and perfusion changes.

  • Escalate altered mental status, airway trouble, shock, dysrhythmia, severe temperature abnormality, rapidly spreading tissue injury, respiratory distress, or worsening pain/swelling.

Diagnostics

  • bedside respiratory and pain assessment with pulse oximetry

  • chest radiography when hypoxemia persists or an alternative diagnosis is possible

  • targeted evaluation for mucus plug, pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or embolism when the course is atypical

  • Distinguish Postoperative atelectasis from pneumonia, aspiration, pulmonary edema, mucus-plug lobar collapse, and pulmonary embolism by fever pattern, imaging, secretions, hemodynamics, and timing.

Labs

  • lactate may rise with shock

  • glucose can be immediately reversible

  • potassium and acid-base shifts can kill quickly

  • drug levels matter when available

Treatments

  • optimize analgesia without excessive sedation

  • mobilize early and coach deep breathing, coughing, and lung-expansion maneuvers

  • suction or perform bronchoscopy for retained secretions or a persistent obstructing plug

Nursing Priorities

  • Assess respiratory rate, depth, effort, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, pain, sedation, cough strength, and mobility because shallow ventilation after anesthesia allows dependent alveoli to collapse.

  • Provide effective multimodal analgesia, support the incision, and time upright positioning, deep breathing, coached coughing, and prescribed lung-expansion therapy after pain control because participation fails when every breath is guarded.

  • Mobilize early and change position regularly, using lateral or upright positioning matched to tolerance because movement increases tidal volume and recruits dependent lung regions.

  • Titrate ordered oxygen and assess for retained secretions; use suction or airway-clearance therapy when indicated rather than relying on incentive-spirometer numbers alone because anesthesia, pain, diaphragmatic dysfunction, mucus retention, and shallow breathing collapse dependent alveoli after surgery.

  • Trend fever, sputum, focal crackles, oxygen need, heart rate, calf findings, and chest-pain pattern because pneumonia, aspiration, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, and fluid overload can mimic or complicate atelectasis.

  • Escalate for rapidly rising oxygen need, respiratory fatigue, sudden pleuritic pain, hemoptysis, unilateral absent sounds, hemodynamic instability, or failure to improve with lung expansion and mobilization because anesthesia, pain, diaphragmatic dysfunction, mucus retention, and shallow breathing collapse dependent alveoli after surgery.

Complications

  • pneumonia can complicate Postoperative atelectasis.

  • worsening hypoxemic respiratory failure

  • prolonged ventilation can complicate Postoperative atelectasis.

  • mucus plugging with lobar collapse

  • missed pulmonary embolism when hypoxemia is attributed reflexively to atelectasis

Red Flags

  • Increasing oxygen requirement, severe work of breathing, exhaustion, or confusion

  • Sudden pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, tachycardia, or unilateral leg swelling

  • Unilateral absent breath sounds, hypotension, or suspected pneumothorax

  • Persistent fever, purulent sputum, focal consolidation signs, or worsening despite therapy

Patient Education

  • Explain that anesthesia, pain, and bed rest make breaths smaller, allowing tiny air sacs to close; pain control and frequent upright deep breathing help reopen them.

  • Use the prescribed breathing device with slow controlled breaths, cough with incision support, and walk with help at the scheduled intervals; report sudden chest pain or worsening breathlessness.

Nclex Traps

  • Postoperative fever should not automatically be blamed on atelectasis

  • Incentive spirometry is an adjunct to coached breathing and mobility, not a stand-alone cure
