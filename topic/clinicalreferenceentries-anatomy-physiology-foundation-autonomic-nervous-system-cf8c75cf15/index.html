Summary

The autonomic nervous system regulates involuntary smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands, and visceral organs through sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric networks. Most pathways use a two-neuron chain from the central nervous system through an autonomic ganglion to the target. The divisions usually provide coordinated, context-dependent control rather than acting as simple opposites.

Quick Answer

All autonomic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors. Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors; most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine onto alpha or beta receptors. Important exceptions include sympathetic sweat glands, which use acetylcholine, and the adrenal medulla, which releases catecholamines into blood.

Sections

    • Organization and location

    • Sympathetic preganglionic neurons arise from thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord and synapse in paravertebral chains or prevertebral ganglia, often producing broad coordinated responses. Parasympathetic outflow arises from brainstem cranial nerves and sacral cord, with ganglia near or within target organs, supporting more localized control. The enteric nervous system forms intrinsic networks in the gastrointestinal tract but communicates with both divisions. Hypothalamus, brainstem, spinal cord, limbic structures, baroreceptors, chemoreceptors, and visceral afferents integrate autonomic behavior with temperature, pressure, oxygen, emotion, digestion, bladder function, and endocrine responses.

    • Transmitters and receptors

    • Preganglionic acetylcholine activates neuronal nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia. Parasympathetic acetylcholine then acts mainly at muscarinic receptors. Sympathetic norepinephrine acts at alpha and beta adrenergic receptors, while circulating epinephrine from adrenal medulla reaches many tissues. Alpha-1 activation commonly contracts vascular and urinary smooth muscle; alpha-2 often reduces transmitter release and insulin secretion; beta-1 increases cardiac rate, conduction, and contractility; beta-2 relaxes selected smooth muscle and shifts potassium into cells; beta-3 supports lipolysis and bladder relaxation. Receptor location, dose, baseline tone, and disease determine the actual response.

    • Sympathetic physiology

    • Sympathetic activation redistributes resources during threat or exertion: heart rate and contractility rise, many vascular beds constrict, bronchi dilate, pupils dilate, sweating increases, and digestion and bladder emptying are deprioritized. It also supports blood pressure during standing through the baroreflex. This is not a universal whole-body switch. Coronary and skeletal-muscle flow may increase through local metabolism and receptor patterns, while skin or splanchnic vessels constrict. Excessive activation increases myocardial oxygen demand, afterload, glucose, lactate, tremor, and arrhythmia risk. Loss of sympathetic tone after high spinal injury can produce vasodilation, bradycardia, and neurogenic shock.

    • Parasympathetic and enteric physiology

    • Parasympathetic activity slows sinoatrial rate and atrioventricular conduction, constricts pupils, promotes salivation and lacrimation, supports bronchial secretion, increases gastrointestinal motility and secretion, facilitates bladder emptying, and participates in sexual arousal. Vagal effects on the ventricles are less dominant than on nodes. Enteric circuits coordinate peristalsis, secretion, local reflexes, and blood flow even without moment-to-moment conscious input. Parasympathetic activity is not simply 'rest' and sympathetic activity not simply 'stress'; both maintain baseline tone and can be active together in complex behaviors.

    • Reflex regulation and relationships

    • When standing lowers venous return and arterial stretch, baroreceptor firing decreases. Brainstem centers increase sympathetic and reduce vagal output, raising vascular tone and heart rate to preserve cerebral perfusion. Chemoreceptors respond to oxygen, carbon dioxide, and pH; bladder stretch triggers coordinated storage or voiding pathways; pupillary reflexes test afferent and autonomic efferent function. Autonomic responses interact with RAAS, ADH, cortisol, temperature control, and local mediators. Diabetes, amyloid, Parkinson disease, spinal injury, medications, dehydration, and prolonged bed rest can damage or decondition these reflexes.

    • Clinical and pharmacologic connections

    • Adrenergic agonists and antagonists, cholinergic agonists, antimuscarinics, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, ganglionic agents, stimulants, opioids, antidepressants, antipsychotics, anesthetics, and many antihypertensives alter autonomic function. Albuterol's beta-2 action bronchodilates but can cause tremor, tachycardia, lactate rise, and potassium shift. Atropine blocks muscarinic effects; it does not block nicotinic weakness. Beta blockers can blunt tachycardia that would otherwise reveal hypoglycemia or shock. Orthostatic vitals, ECG, glucose, electrolytes, bladder scan, gastric studies, sweat testing, tilt-table testing, and autonomic reflex testing answer selected clinical questions.

    • Nursing assessment

    • Trend supine and standing symptoms and vitals when safe, heart rate and rhythm, pulse and perfusion, pupils, temperature and sweating, oral moisture, lung sounds and secretions, bowel sounds and motility, bladder filling and output, sexual or vasomotor symptoms when relevant, glucose awareness, and falls or syncope. Review every medication and recent dose change. Distinguish dry flushed anticholinergic patterns from wet secretory cholinergic patterns, and recognize that older adults may present with delirium, retention, constipation, blurred vision, or falls rather than naming an autonomic adverse effect.

    • Nursing actions and why

    • Change position gradually, dangle before standing, use assistance and compression or hydration plans when ordered, and teach counterpressure maneuvers for selected orthostatic disorders because gravity challenges venous return. Monitor ECG and perfusion with vasoactive therapy, verify dose and route carefully, and titrate only to ordered endpoints. Institute bladder and bowel safety when retention or ileus is possible. Reduce anticholinergic burden when the team can do so, especially in older adults. During autonomic dysreflexia, sit the patient upright, loosen constriction, find and remove the trigger beginning with bladder causes, and treat severe pressure per protocol.

    • Urgent red flags

    • Hypotension with bradycardia and warm dry skin after cervical or high thoracic injury suggests neurogenic shock. Severe hypertension with pounding headache, flushing or sweating above a spinal lesion, bradycardia, and bladder or bowel trigger suggests autonomic dysreflexia and risks stroke or seizure. Syncope with exertion, chest pain, malignant rhythm, or injury needs urgent evaluation. Hyperthermia, delirium, very dry skin, urinary retention, and ileus can indicate severe anticholinergic toxicity; bronchorrhea, wheeze, bradycardia, diarrhea, and weakness suggest cholinergic poisoning. Airway, breathing, and circulation remain first.

    • Misconceptions and NCLEX traps

    • Sympathetic and parasympathetic effects are not exact opposites in every organ. Most blood vessels have little direct parasympathetic innervation. Sweat glands are sympathetic but cholinergic. Nicotinic receptors occur in autonomic ganglia and skeletal neuromuscular junctions, while muscarinic receptors mediate many parasympathetic target effects. A normal seated blood pressure does not exclude orthostatic failure. Beta-blocked patients may not develop expected tachycardia. Do not place a patient with autonomic dysreflexia flat; upright position helps reduce cerebral pressure while the trigger is removed.
