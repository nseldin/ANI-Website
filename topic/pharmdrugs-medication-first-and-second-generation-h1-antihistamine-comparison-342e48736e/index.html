Used To Treat

Histamine-mediated itching, sneezing, rhinorrhea, and urticaria. Second-generation agents are generally preferred for routine allergy control because they last longer and impair cognition less. Antihistamines do not replace intramuscular epinephrine in anaphylaxis.

Description

H1 antihistamines stabilize the inactive state of the H1 receptor, reducing histamine-driven itch, wheal, vasodilation, and sensory-nerve symptoms. First-generation drugs such as diphenhydramine cross the blood-brain barrier and also block muscarinic receptors, producing sedation, impaired learning, delirium, dry mouth, urinary retention, and falls. Second-generation drugs such as cetirizine are more peripherally selective and longer acting, although cetirizine can still cause drowsiness. The generation distinction is therefore a safety and performance issue, not a marketing label.

Mechanism

Histamine H1 receptors are Gq-coupled; constitutive and histamine-stimulated signaling activates phospholipase C, IP3, calcium, endothelial nitric oxide, vascular permeability, and sensory-nerve itch. H1 inverse agonists favor the inactive receptor conformation. Lipophilic first-generation molecules cross the CNS and bind off-target muscarinic, alpha-adrenergic, and serotonergic receptors to varying degrees. More polar second-generation agents are restricted by lower CNS penetration and transporters. Neither reverses the airway edema, shock, mediator cascade, or bronchospasm of anaphylaxis as comprehensively and rapidly as epinephrine's alpha1, beta1, and beta2 actions.

Boxed Warning

No current class-wide U.S. boxed warning. High-yield warning context: first-generation antihistamines can impair driving and clinical judgment even when the user does not feel sleepy, and anticholinergic toxicity is especially dangerous in older adults, glaucoma, urinary retention, and polypharmacy. In anaphylaxis, giving an antihistamine first delays epinephrine and can allow fatal airway or circulatory collapse.

Adverse Effects

  • First generation: sedation, confusion, blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, tachycardia, paradoxical excitation, and falls

  • Second generation: headache, dry mouth, and product-specific drowsiness, with cetirizine more sedating than fexofenadine for some patients

Contraindications

  • Avoid sedating anticholinergic drugs when delirium, falls, angle-closure glaucoma, urinary retention, or hazardous work makes risk unacceptable.

  • Do not use any oral H1 antihistamine as the primary treatment for anaphylaxis.

Nursing Essentials

  • Ask about driving, alcohol, sleep aids, opioids, and anticholinergic burden rather than asking only whether the patient has an allergy medicine.

  • For anaphylaxis, give IM epinephrine first and treat antihistamines only as adjuncts for persistent skin symptoms after stabilization.

Interactions

  • Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, sleep drugs, and other sedatives amplify CNS depression.

  • Anticholinergic drugs add urinary, ocular, bowel, cognitive, and heat-regulation toxicity.

Key Labs

  • Clinical symptom response, sedation, cognition, falls, urinary retention, and anticholinergic burden

  • Renal or hepatic function for product-specific dose adjustment; routine serum antihistamine levels are not useful

Nclex Traps

  • Diphenhydramine can reduce hives but does not treat anaphylactic shock.

  • Second generation means less CNS penetration, not zero sedation for every drug or patient.
